# Discussion questions: the facets a set should cover

Phase 7 of [SKILL.md](SKILL.md) links here. Every study ends with a discussion-questions section, and
until 2026-09-07 the only guidance on it was one paragraph in [style-guide.md](style-guide.md) saying
what they must *not* be about. This file says what they should cover.

## The set mirrors Key Takeaways

[key-takeaways.md](../../../docs/content/about/key-takeaways.md) already states the relationship:
"Key Takeaways delivers content and calls to action up front; discussion questions are open-ended
prompts for a reader or a group to work through afterward." So the facets are not arbitrary — the
questions ask the reader to do, for themselves, the same work the study did for them.

| Facet | The question turns on | Key Takeaways counterpart |
|---|---|---|
| **The language** | a Hebrew, Aramaic or Greek word, form or distinction the study established | — (the word studies in the body) |
| **The text in its context** | what the passage meant then-and-there — the exegesis | — (the walk-through) |
| **Type, prophecy, and Christ** | the pattern or prediction, and what it shows about Jesus | Types & Prophecy · Lessons about Jesus |
| **The hard part** | the genuine tension, ambiguity or contested reading the study named | — (the study's own open questions) |
| **Be transformed** | the reader's own thinking, attitude or action | Be Transformed |

Four to six questions is the usual range. **Include only the facets the study earned** — the same
discipline Key Takeaways already applies. A study with no word study gets no language question, and
forcing one produces a vocabulary quiz. A study with no genuine tension should not manufacture one.

A reasonable default order runs text → reader: language, context, Christ, the hard part, transformed.
It is a default, not a rule; a study whose whole point is Christological may well open there.

## The format: each question carries its facet as a bold label

A numbered list, each item opening with its facet in bold, then the text it stands on, then the
question. `hurt-by-the-church.md` is the reference specimen:

```markdown
## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** The "harshness" of Ezekiel 34:4 is the word used for Pharaoh's slavery
   (Exodus 1:13) and forbidden between Israelites (Leviticus 25:43). What does that comparison say
   about how God sees leaders who misuse His people?
2. **The text in its context.** Jesus explained the parable of the weeds Himself (Matthew 13:38).
   Why does it matter that the field is "the world"?
3. **Christ.** Jesus quoted Psalm 41:9 of Judas (John 13:18) and later restored Peter (John
   21:15-17). What does each tell you about how He handles betrayal?
4. **The hard part.** Paul said "Purge the evil person from among you" (1 Corinthians 5:13) and also
   "Why not rather suffer wrong?" (1 Corinthians 6:7). How do you tell which one a situation calls
   for?
5. **Be transformed.** Paul prayed of those who deserted him, "May it not be charged against them!"
   (2 Timothy 4:16). Who is the person you need to pray that for, and what would it cost you?
```

The label tells a group leader what kind of conversation each question opens before anyone reads
it, and it makes a missing facet visible at a glance — a set with no **Be transformed.** line is
obviously short of one. Use the five labels exactly as written: **The language.**, **The text in
its context.**, **Christ.**, **The hard part.**, **Be transformed.** (variants such as *Language.*,
*Context.* or *You.* drifted in before this was written and should be normalised when touched).
A sixth question on a theme the study earned may take its own short label — `hurt-by-the-church.md`'s
**Testing.**, `test-the-spirits.md`'s **Keep asking.** — but the set still ends on
**Be transformed.**

`validate-content.js` Check 25 warns on a question with no bold label and on a set with no
**Be transformed.** question.

## What each facet is asking for

**The language.** Not "what does *X* mean" — that is a comprehension check the study already
answered. Ask what the distinction *does*: what a smoother translation loses, what changes if the
other reading is right, why the author chose this word over the available alternative.

**The text in its context.** The then-and-there question. What the first hearers would have taken
from it, what the occasion was, why the author moves from the previous paragraph to this one.

**Type, prophecy, and Christ.** Where the study established a type, ask what it carries and what it
does not. Where it established a prophecy, the interesting question is usually what the prediction
*commits* you to, not whether it was fulfilled.

**The hard part.** Every study that has done honest work has one — a contested reading, an ambiguity
the sources never settled, two texts that resist each other. Put it to the reader as the texts, not
as the study's handling of them (see the rule below). "How do you hold these two sentences together?"
is a discussion question. "Did the study convince you?" is not. End it on the texts: "where has this
blurred for you?" turns the hard part into a second Be transformed question and leaves the tension
unexamined.

**Be transformed.** At least one question should land on the reader personally. This is the facet
most often missing: a set can be five sharp analytical prompts and give a group nothing to actually
do. The strongest version puts a real gap on the table — something Scripture says against something
the reader's own circles routinely say.

## Rules, each from a specimen in this corpus

- **Never about the study's own method.** The standing rule from
  [style-guide.md](style-guide.md#a-note-on-discussion-questions). Two of `rapture.md`'s four asked
  the reader to evaluate the page's even-handedness. `three-days-and-three-nights.md` had a subtler
  one — *"Why might an incidental count carry more evidential weight than a deliberate one?"* — which
  is a question about evidence-weighting technique wearing an epistemology costume. Harder to catch
  than "did this change your mind," and the same fault.
- **No rhetorical question whose answer the study just gave.** *"…does a sign have to be a
  measurement to be true?"* telegraphs the conclusion of the section above it. That is a
  comprehension check, and a group will feel led.
- **One question, not two joined by "and."** *"What is a sign for, and does a sign have to be a
  measurement to be true?"* is two prompts; a group answers the easier one and drops the other.
  (A clause that *sharpens* a single question — "…and what would be lost if it were only X?" — is
  fine. The test is whether both halves could be discussed separately.)
- **Root each question in a named text.** A group needs somewhere to turn. "What does this teach
  about death?" is a prompt for a sermon; "Samuel tells Saul *'tomorrow you and your sons shall be
  with me'* — what does that do to…" is a prompt for a Bible study.
- **No question a group can answer yes or no.** *"Is there someone you are withholding forgiveness
  from…?"* gets a silent nod. Ask *who*, *what* or *how*.
- **Never build a question on an English sense the original does not carry.** "The sum of your word
  is truth" (Psalm 119:160, ESV) means the whole of it; a question that reads "sum" as arithmetic
  bends the verse to fit a chronology study.
- **Give the original word beside a quotation, never inside it.** *"you are not setting your mind
  (φρονεῖς) on the things of God"* edits the ESV. Quote the verse whole, then name the word: "…Paul
  uses the same verb, φρονέω, for…".
- **Match the study's own proportions.** `three-days-and-three-nights.md` spent three of five
  questions on the arithmetic *after* a restructure had demoted that argument to a quarter of the
  page and called it "a small one" in the study's own conclusion.

## The failure mode that is specific to this section

**Question sets go stale silently, and they are the likeliest part of a study to do it.** When a
study is restructured, the prose gets re-read and the questions do not — because each question is
still individually defensible. Nothing looks wrong. `three-days-and-three-nights.md` carried its
original set through three revisions that doubled the theology, halved the arithmetic and added four
new sections; the questions still asked about time language, and no validator, link check or style
pass could see it. The user spotted it by reading.

So: **after any restructure, re-read the question set against the table above and ask which facets
the study now has that the questions do not touch.** This is the same class of defect as
`validate-content.js` checks 18 and 19 — a claim that was true when written and quietly stopped
being true — and it is not mechanically detectable either.
