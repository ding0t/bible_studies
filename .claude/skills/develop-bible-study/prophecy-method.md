# Reading a prophecy forward

The prophets and apocalyptic lens for [SKILL.md](SKILL.md#phase-4--exegesis-content-words-and-genre-lens),
worked out on Ezekiel 40-48 (2026-11-25). Use it whenever a study turns on a prediction:
whether it has been fulfilled, when, and in what. Phases 2 and 3 still come first. A prophecy is
covenant enforcement spoken to real people before it is a forecast
([how-to-read-the-bible.md](../../../docs/content/scripture/how-to-read-the-bible.md)).

The order below is the guardrail. Scripture's own reading of the prophecy comes first, then the
oldest readers, and the study notes come last
([cross-references.md](cross-references.md#commentaries-and-study-notes)).

## 1. Read the prophecy in its book

- **Date it and place it in the book's order.** Ezekiel dates his visions (Ezekiel 1:1-2; 8:1; 40:1),
  and the order is part of the argument. Chapters 33-39 build to "my sanctuary in their midst
  forevermore" (Ezekiel 37:26-28), and chapters 40-48 then show that sanctuary.
- **Find the frame.** A vision often answers an earlier one in the same book. In Ezekiel the glory
  leaves by the east (Ezekiel 10:18-19; 11:22-23) and returns by the east (Ezekiel 43:1-5). Name the
  frame before you name a fulfilment.
- **Separate the conditional from the unconditional.** Some prophecies carry a stated condition
  ("if they are ashamed", Ezekiel 43:11; the rule in Jeremiah 18:7-10). Others rest on God alone ("It
  is not for your sake… that I am about to act", Ezekiel 36:22). Ask what the condition governs. It
  may govern whether the hearers take part, or whether the promise stands at all.

## 2. Let the New Testament read it, and classify each use

Gather every New Testament use with `bible_trace` and `bible_lists` (`ot-quotations`). Then label
each use by **what the New Testament writer claims**, because the labels carry different weight:

| Use | Signal in the text | Example | Does it close the Old Testament expectation? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fulfilment** | "fulfilled", "this is what was spoken", "today this Scripture" | Luke 4:21 of Isaiah 61:1-2a | Yes, for the words quoted |
| **Split fulfilment** | The quotation stops part-way, or the context reserves the rest | Luke 4:19 stops before "the day of vengeance" (Isaiah 61:2) | Only the part quoted |
| **Type** | τύπος and its kin, or an explicit pattern | 1 Corinthians 10:6, 11 | No. A type resembles; it does not replace |
| **Application** | The promise's words are applied to a new people, with no fulfilment formula | 2 Corinthians 6:16 applies Ezekiel 37:27 to the church | No, unless the writer says so |
| **Imagery reused** | Shared pictures, changed details | Revelation 21:10-22:2 reuses Ezekiel 40-48 and has no temple | No. Read the changes as carefully as the borrowings |

Only the first two close an expectation, and only for the words quoted. This is the dispensational
reading's working rule, and it should be stated as such: "the gifts and the calling of God are
irrevocable" (Romans 11:29, ESV). A study that treats an application or reused imagery as the whole
fulfilment has to show where the New Testament says so.

## 3. Audit the details against every candidate fulfilment

This is the core of the method: a **fulfilment ledger**. List the prophecy's checkable particulars
(a building's measures, a river, a boundary, who lives there, whether death or sin is present). List
the candidate fulfilments: the near history, Christ's first coming, the church, the thousand years,
the new creation. Mark each cell **meets**, **fails** or **partly**, and give a verse for each mark.

- **A candidate has to meet every particular, or show from the text why one is figurative.** "The
  text is symbolic" is a conclusion that needs a signal in the text: a stated interpretation, as in
  Daniel 7:17 ("These four great beasts are four kings"), or a detail that cannot be literal on its
  own terms. A modern reader's discomfort with a detail is no such signal.
- **The details that exclude a candidate weigh most.** Ezekiel's temple has priests who marry and
  bury their dead (Ezekiel 44:22, 25), a sea with fishermen and salt marshes left unhealed (Ezekiel
  47:10-11), and offerings "for anyone who has sinned through error" (Ezekiel 45:20). Each one
  excludes the new creation of Revelation 21:1-4.
- **Record the history a near fulfilment needs.** A near fulfilment must have happened, in a source:
  Ezra 6:18 says the returned exiles ordered the second temple "as it is written in the Book of
  Moses."
- **A present-day event offered as a fulfilment goes through the same ledger.** This is reading the
  Bible through the headlines. Jerusalem's sealed Golden Gate is often offered for Ezekiel 44:2. The
  gate in the text belongs to the measured temple of the vision (Ezekiel 40:6-16), and it is shut
  "for the LORD, the God of Israel, has entered by it" (Ezekiel 44:2, ESV). That entry has not
  happened.
- Put the ledger in the state file (`prophecy_ledger`) and, where it carries the argument, in the
  study as a table. It shows a reader why a conclusion was reached, and it can be checked one cell at
  a time.

## 4. Name each figure from its words and its particulars

- **Check every Hebrew or Greek word behind an English title.** "Prince" renders נָשִׂיא (*nasi*) in
  Ezekiel, שַׂר (*sar*) in Isaiah 9:6 and Daniel 8:25, נָגִיד (*nagid*) in Daniel 9:25, and ἀρχηγός
  (*archēgos*) in Acts 5:31. Then run `bible_concordance` on the word inside the book. Ezekiel uses
  *nasi* 37 times: for Zedekiah (Ezekiel 12:10), for Gog (Ezekiel 38:2), for "my servant David"
  (Ezekiel 34:24; 37:25), and 20 times for the unnamed prince of chapters 44-48.
- **A title shared with Christ is a lead. The particulars decide.** Ezekiel's prince offers a sin
  offering "for himself" (Ezekiel 45:22, ESV) and has sons who inherit (Ezekiel 46:16). Neither fits
  Christ, and the sons do not fit a risen saint either (Luke 20:35-36). Where one title covers two
  figures, name both. Ezekiel 34 has two shepherds, the LORD (Ezekiel 34:15) and David
  (Ezekiel 34:23), and Jesus claims both roles (John 10:11).
- **Weigh a word that sounds absolute by its range in the same book.** עוֹלָם (*olam*, "long
  duration") is the "forever" of the LORD's dwelling (Ezekiel 43:7) and the "perpetual statute" of
  the daily offering (Ezekiel 46:14). The Day of Atonement was "a statute forever" too (Leviticus
  16:34). Let the New Testament say which order outlasts which (Hebrews 8:13; Revelation 21:3, 22).

## 5. Near, far, and the join

- **Look for the join the New Testament reveals.** A single Old Testament sentence can hold two
  comings with the centuries between them hidden. The standing example is Isaiah 61:2 at Luke 4:19.
  Others to test: Malachi 3:1 (Mark 1:2) beside Malachi 3:2-4; Zechariah 9:9 (Matthew 21:5) beside
  9:10; Micah 5:2 (Matthew 2:6) beside 5:3-4; Joel 2:28-32 in Acts 2:17-21, whose sun and moon signs
  did not occur at Pentecost; Daniel 9:26 beside 9:27. The site's treatment, with Larkin's
  mountain-peaks chart, is [Two mountain peaks](../../../docs/content/last-things/immediately-after.md#two-mountain-peaks);
  link to it rather than re-explaining it.
- **Claim a near fulfilment and a far one only with a textual reason for each.** The near one has to
  have happened. The far one has to be required by a particular the near one failed. "Double
  fulfilment" asserted without that is a way of keeping every option open.
- **Watch for a partial near fulfilment that points forward.** The glory did come to the temple in
  person (John 1:14; Malachi 3:1), and He left it "desolate" (Matthew 23:38). Say what was fulfilled
  and what was left over.
- **A pattern the New Testament does not draw stays a pattern.** Give the shared words and places,
  call it a pattern, and never call it a type. Ezekiel 11:23 and Zechariah 14:4 share "stand", "on
  the mountain" and "on the east" (מִקֶּדֶם, *miqqedem*). Jesus leaves the temple for that mountain
  (Matthew 24:1-3) and ascends from it (Acts 1:12), and no New Testament writer joins either event to
  Ezekiel.

## 6. Ask the oldest readers, in date order

Gather the witnesses in this order and **date each one**:

1. **Inner-biblical.** A later prophet reading an earlier one: Zechariah 14:8 on Ezekiel 47's river;
   Daniel 9:2 reading Jeremiah's seventy years.
2. **Second Temple Jewish.** Tobit 14:5 (deuterocanonical, raw files only; see
   [references/README.md](../../../references/README.md)) expects a lesser temple after the exile and
   a glorious one later "as the prophets have spoken".
   The Qumran scrolls are the Jewish witnesses certainly older than Jesus: `dss_text` reads them by
   siglum, column and line, or searches the Hebrew (4Q252 v 3 reads Genesis 49:10 as "the Messiah
   of righteousness, the Branch of David"). Weigh `extant_words`; a restored word is not evidence.
   The Psalms of Solomon (`bible_verse`, `PsSol`, `swete-lxx`; after 48 BC by 2:30-31) and the
   Septuagint's renderings belong here too.
3. **Rabbinic.** The Targums are in `bible_verse` (`targum-onkelos`, `targum-jonathan`,
   `targum-writings`, ...; Hebrew numbering), dated in sources.toml [profiles.targums]: all later
   than the New Testament, so they show a reading's reception, never its age. `edersheim_messianic`
   is the finding list for the rest (Edersheim's Appendix IX). Read midrash and Talmud via
   `references/build/sefaria.py`. Check each version's licence before quoting,
   and date a midrash by its compilation, not by the rabbi it names. *Lamentations Rabbah*,
   Petichta 25 (Sefaria's title is "Eikhah Rabbah") traces the Presence from Ezekiel 9-11 to the
   Mount of Olives, where it waited three and a half years for Israel to repent. Any parallel with
   the Gospels is your observation, never the rabbis'.
4. **The earliest church**, from `$BIBLE_MEDIA_ROOT/reference/patristics/`: Justin, Irenaeus and
   Papias before Origen and Augustine. Note when a figurative reading first appears and what travels
   with it. *Barnabas* 16 makes the temple the believer's heart in the same chapter that calls the
   Jews "wretched".

These readers are witnesses to how a text was heard, never Scripture, and never the deciding vote.
They are often a better check than a modern note on what the words could mean to their first hearers.

## 7. Test the objection the New Testament seems to raise

When a New Testament text appears to close the question (Hebrews 10:18 against Ezekiel's offerings),
read exactly what it closes in its own context. Hebrews 10:18 closes the offering "for sin" where
there is forgiveness. Hebrews 9:13 grants the old offerings a real but limited effect, "the
purification of the flesh". Then check whether the prophecy contains what the objection assumes.
Hebrews argues from the high priest, the veil, the mercy seat and the Day of Atonement (Hebrews
9:3-7), and Ezekiel 40-48 names none of them. State the objection at full strength before answering
it.

## 8. Mark the confidence of each conclusion

Use one of five labels and say which one applies:

- **Fulfilled, the New Testament says so.**
- **Fulfilled in history**, with the source.
- **Partly fulfilled**, with the join named.
- **Not yet fulfilled.** No candidate meets the particulars.
- **Contested**, with textual reasons on both sides.

Check the conclusion against [statement-of-faith.md](../../../docs/content/about/statement-of-faith.md)
only after the ledger is done (SKILL.md Phase 5).

## 9. What the ledger has shown: features to look for

The ledger of the Old Testament's prophecies of Christ (`docs/data/prophecies-of-christ.toml`, 118
entries) turned up features that recur. Look for each when reading a prophecy. The counts are as of
2026-11-26; recount from the ledger before a study prints one. The reader sees the ledger on
`jesus/prophecies-of-christ.md`, rendered by `hooks/prophecies.py`; link a study to one entry as
`#prophecy-<id>`, and re-run `references/build/export_prophecies.py` after editing the ledger.

- **Who fulfilled it.** His opponents fulfilled 15 of the 26 passion prophecies (`control`), and none
  of the 19 prophecies His opponents fulfilled was read of the Messiah before Jesus: "in condemning
  Him they fulfilled the words of the prophets" (Acts 13:27, BSB). An act done by people who did not
  know the text could not be arranged by them.
- **What was known, and what was hidden.** The line and the reign were read of the Messiah before
  Jesus (lineage, 6 of 8 known before); the suffering was not (passion, 0 of 26). Six entries carry
  a New Testament text saying it was understood only afterwards (John 2:22; 12:16; 20:9; Luke
  18:34; 24:45). Fill `recognition` and `understood_after` from section 6's witnesses.
- **Which text the quotation follows.** Of 120 quoting uses, 50 follow the Septuagint, 29 agree with
  both texts, 17 render the Hebrew afresh and 24 follow neither. The fresh renderings cluster where
  the Septuagint misses the point: "pierced" (John 19:37), "the potter" (Matthew 27:10), "heel"
  (John 13:18), "with the lawless" (Luke 22:37). Compare every quotation with both.
- **The omission that respects the near referent.** John 13:18 leaves out "whom I trusted" (Psalm
  41:9), and Hebrews 1:5 leaves out "When he does wrong" (2 Samuel 7:14). The quotation keeps the
  part that outruns the first speaker: test each quoted line for what is more than the first person
  could say of himself (section 4).
- **A change of person.** Zechariah 13:7 commands "Strike the shepherd"; Matthew 26:31 has "I will
  strike", naming God as the agent.
- **The LORD's text becomes Jesus' text.** Fourteen entries (`yhwh_text`): Isaiah 8:13 at 1 Peter
  3:15, Malachi 3:1's "before Me" as "before You", Isaiah 40:3's "our God" as "His".
- **The split.** Seventeen entries hold both comings in one prophecy. The divider is often "until"
  (Micah 5:3; Hosea 5:15; Psalm 110:1 with Hebrews 10:12-13; Matthew 23:39), or Jesus stopping
  short (Luke 4:19; Matthew 11:5). Fill `split.signal` with the word that shows the join (section 5).
