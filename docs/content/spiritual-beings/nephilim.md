# The Nephilim: Sons of God and the Giants of Genesis

## The text itself

> ✝️ Genesis 6:1-4 (ESV)
>
> 1 When man began to multiply on the face of the land and daughters were born to them, 2 the sons of God saw that the daughters of man were attractive. And they took as their wives any they chose. 3 Then the LORD said, "My Spirit shall not abide in man forever, for he is flesh: his days shall be 120 years." 4 The Nephilim were on the earth in those days, and also afterward, when the sons of God came in to the daughters of man and they bore children to them. These were the mighty men who were of old, the men of renown.

Four verses, and nearly every phrase in them is debated: who are the "sons of God," what does "Nephilim" mean, and how does a group present "in those days" turn up again, by name, centuries later at the edge of the Promised Land? The second question comes from Numbers:

> ✝️ Numbers 13:33 (ESV)
>
> 33 And there we saw the Nephilim (the sons of Anak, who come from the Nephilim), and we seemed to ourselves like grasshoppers, and so we seemed to them.

This study works through both passages on their own terms first, then follows the giant clans across Deuteronomy, Joshua, and Samuel to answer the two questions that prompted it: did the Nephilim exist after the flood, and are they still around.

## Key Takeaways

**Lessons about Jesus.** Jesus himself supplies the only New Testament comparison to this era, and he's precise about what the comparison covers: "as were the days of Noah, so will be the coming of the Son of Man" (Matthew 24:37, ESV) is about people "eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage" in total unawareness "until the flood came and swept them all away" (24:38-39) — ordinary complacency meeting sudden judgment, not a claim about giants or genetics. The comparison is worth taking exactly as far as Jesus took it, and no further.

**Memory verses.** Genesis 6:3 (ESV) — "My Spirit shall not abide in man forever, for he is flesh: his days shall be 120 years." Matthew 24:37 (ESV) — "As were the days of Noah, so will be the coming of the Son of Man."

**Be Transformed.** The ten spies who invoked the Nephilim by name did so inside a report Scripture itself calls "evil" (Numbers 13:32) — and died for spreading it (14:36-37). Caleb and Joshua saw the identical giants and drew the opposite conclusion (14:6-9). The same facts, filtered through fear versus trust, produced opposite reports. Examine which of those two you default to when a real obstacle is in front of you.

**Prayer.** Lord, you know every uncertain thing in this text better than any commentator does. Where Scripture speaks plainly, give me the courage of Caleb, not the fear of the ten. Where Scripture leaves a question open, give me the humility to leave it open too, rather than trading your authority for the confidence of a guess. Amen.

## Historical and literary context

Genesis 6:1-8 sits at a hinge point in the book: chapter 5's genealogy has just carried the line from Adam to Noah, and this short unit explains *why* God is about to judge the whole earth with a flood. "The LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth" (6:5) is the verdict; verses 1-4 are the narrator's explanation of what led to it. Whatever else is uncertain about "sons of God" and "Nephilim," the passage's own job in the book's argument is not uncertain — it is the hinge between the world's expanding population (echoing the "be fruitful and multiply" of 1:28, now curdled into "great" wickedness) and the flood that follows.

Numbers 13:25-33 has a different, equally clear job: it is the spies' report from their forty-day mission into Canaan, and Numbers itself distinguishes it from a straightforward account. The narration in 13:17-24 is plain reconnaissance — routes, terrain, a cluster of grapes so large it took two men to carry it. The report *in the people's hearing* (13:26-33) is a different register: fortified cities "very large" (13:28), a long list of named nations (13:29), and finally the claim that "the land, through which we have gone to spy it out, is a land that eats up its inhabitants" (13:32) and that its Anakite population "come from the Nephilim" (13:33). Numbers' own narrator labels this report a *dibbah* — more on that word below — and the consequence for the ten men who brought it is immediate and severe: "the men who brought up a bad report of the land... died by plague before the LORD" (14:36-37). That framing matters for how much weight the Nephilim claim in 13:33 can bear, and it's addressed directly in the word study below rather than skipped past.

## Word studies

**Nephilim,** <span dir="rtl">נְפִלִים / נְפִילִים</span> (*nephilim*, H5303). The word occurs exactly twice in the entire Old Testament — Genesis 6:4 and Numbers 13:33 — which is itself worth pausing on: nearly everything popularly "known" about the Nephilim is inference and tradition built on two verses. The noun's root is *naphal* (<span dir="rtl">נָפַל</span>, H5307), "to fall," but a root is not automatically a word's live meaning (the same caution any word study has to observe), and lexicographers have proposed several distinct senses from that root: "fallen ones" (morally fallen, or "fallen" from a heavenly status), "those who fall upon" others — i.e., aggressors or tyrants, from a different sense of the same verb — or, via a later interpretive tradition, "giants." The earliest attested interpretation is the last one: the Septuagint renders *nephilim* as γίγαντες (*gigantes*, "giants") in both of its two occurrences. That's a real, old reading — but the ESV Study Bible's note on Genesis 6:4 adds an important caution: since Numbers 13:33 is the more vivid of the two verses (spies comparing themselves to grasshoppers next to men of "great stature," 13:32), the "giant" sense may have been read back into Genesis 6:4 from Numbers, rather than being Genesis's own point. The Hebrew word's core meaning is more uncertain than the popular "giant" gloss suggests.

**Sons of God,** <span dir="rtl">בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים</span> (*bene ha-elohim*). This exact construct phrase occurs in only four other places in the Hebrew Bible, and all four are in Job — 1:6, 2:1, and 38:7 — where it unambiguously names members of God's heavenly court presenting themselves before him, Satan among them in the first two instances. That's a genuine data point in favor of reading Genesis 6:2 and 6:4 the same way: angelic beings, not human ones. Three views have been argued historically, and no single one commands universal agreement:

1. **Fallen angels.** Supported by the phrase's consistent angelic sense in Job, and by New Testament texts that pair angelic sin with the flood in the same breath — 2 Peter 2:4-5 ("God did not spare angels when they sinned... and did not spare the ancient world, but preserved Noah") and Jude 1:6-7 (angels who "did not stay within their own position of authority, but left their proper dwelling," paired immediately with Sodom's "unnatural desire," ESV).

    The objection usually raised is Jesus's statement that in the resurrection people "neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven" (Mark 12:25) — but that describes angels' *present* state in heaven, not a claim that angels are incapable of the kind of transgression Genesis 6 and Jude both describe as already having happened.
2. **Tyrannical human kings.** In this view "sons of God" is an ancient Near Eastern idiom for despotic rulers claiming divine status (compare how some ANE kings styled themselves), drawn from Cain's ungodly line running through Lamech (Genesis 4:19-24) — possibly, on this view, kings acting under demonic influence rather than being angels themselves.
3. **The godly line of Seth.** The most common view in later church history: Seth's descendants (the line that "called upon the name of the LORD," Genesis 4:26) intermarrying with Cain's ungodly line, blurring the distinction between the two lines the genealogies of chapters 4-5 had carefully kept separate.

The Septuagint itself translates the identical Hebrew phrase two different ways within its own pages — **ἄγγελοι τοῦ Θεοῦ** ("angels of God") in Job 1:6 and 38:7, but literally **υἱοὶ τοῦ Θεοῦ** ("sons of God") in Genesis 6:2 and 6:4. That's a real observation about how one ancient translation handled the phrase in two different books (Old Greek Job is a notably freer, more periphrastic translation than Old Greek Genesis), not proof that the LXX's translators held a settled view on Genesis 6 specifically — it would overclaim the evidence to read more into it than that.

**Mighty men, men of renown,** <span dir="rtl">גִּבֹּרִים אֲשֶׁר מֵעוֹלָם אַנְשֵׁי הַשֵּׁם</span> (*gibborim... anshei ha-shem*). Genesis 6:4 identifies whoever is in view — the Nephilim themselves, or their offspring from the sons-of-God union; the Hebrew syntax doesn't force a choice — as *gibborim* (H1368, "mighty men," from a root meaning "strong"), further described as *anshei ha-shem*, "men of the name" or "men of renown." *Gibbor* is not an inherently sinister word — the same root describes David's own elite warriors (the *gibborim* of 2 Samuel 23) and God himself ("Mighty God," *El Gibbor*, Isaiah 9:6). What made *these* gibborim notable enough for the narrator to name them isn't stated beyond "men of renown" — but the immediate next verse (6:5, "the wickedness of man was great") makes clear the narrator isn't holding them up as admirable.

**Rephaim,** <span dir="rtl">רְפָאִים</span>. A lexical wrinkle to mark carefully and not build on: the consonants *r-p-'-y-m* carry two distinct Strong's numbers for two distinct senses. H7497 is the historical giant clan named repeatedly in Deuteronomy and Joshua (below). H7496 is an entirely different poetic usage — "shades" or "departed spirits" of the dead in the underworld (Isaiah 14:9's "it stirs up the *rephaim* for you," Proverbs 21:16, Psalm 88:10). These are tracked as separate lexical entries in the standard reference tools, not simply two shades of one meaning, and this study treats them the same way — a real point of interest about the word, not a doctrine to build on the coincidence of spelling.

**Anak / Anakim,** <span dir="rtl">עֲנָק / עֲנָקִים</span> (H6062). A proper name/gentilic with no separate root entry in the standard word lists. Numbers 13:33 is the *only* verse in the whole Bible that calls the Anakim "sons of the Nephilim" — and, as the historical-context section above already flagged, it's a claim made inside the spies' *dibbah*, not stated anywhere else in Scripture as settled fact.

**Evil report,** <span dir="rtl">דִּבָּה</span> (*dibbah*, H1681). The word Numbers 13:32 itself uses for what the ten spies "brought up" to the people. It's a strong, negative term — "slander" or "defamation" is closer to its force than a neutral "report" — and it recurs at 14:36-37 to identify precisely why those ten men died: not for scouting the land, which Moses had commanded, but for the *dibbah* they spread about it. Whatever the Anakim-from-Nephilim claim in 13:33 means historically, the text's own vocabulary flags the report carrying it as something Israel was meant to disbelieve, not receive as reliable intelligence.

## Did the Nephilim exist after the flood?

This is the first question the study set out to answer, and it has to be split into two separate claims that the text does not treat identically.

**The giant-statured population groups are historically secure, independent of any spy report.** Long before and after Numbers 13, Scripture narrates — in plain, unremarked-upon fact, not embedded in anyone's fear-driven speech — a cluster of unusually large peoples in Canaan and Transjordan:

> ✝️ Deuteronomy 2:10-11, 20-21 (ESV)
>
> 10 (The Emim formerly lived there, a people great and many, and tall as the Anakim. 11 Like the Anakim they are also counted as Rephaim, but the Moabites call them Emim... 20 (That also is counted a land of Rephaim. Rephaim formerly lived there — but the Ammonites call them Zamzummim — 21 a people great and many, and tall as the Anakim...)

> ✝️ Deuteronomy 3:11 (ESV)
>
> 11 (For only Og the king of Bashan was left of the remnant of the Rephaim. Behold, his bed was a bed of iron... nine cubits was its length, and four cubits its breadth, according to the common cubit.)

Deuteronomy 9:2 confirms the Anakim specifically: "a people great and tall, the sons of the Anakim, whom you know, and of whom you have heard it said, 'Who can stand before the sons of Anak?'" Joshua narrates Israel's actual conquest of them: "There was none of the Anakim left in the land of the people of Israel. Only in Gaza, in Gath, and in Ashdod did some remain" (Joshua 11:22). And that surviving remnant in Gath is exactly where 1 Samuel and 2 Samuel later place Goliath ("a champion... named Goliath of Gath, whose height was six cubits and a span," 1 Samuel 17:4) and, after him, four more named men "born to the giant in Gath" who fell to David's men (2 Samuel 21:15-22). None of this narration is anyone's frightened claim — it's the plain record of who Israel fought, across four centuries, from the conquest to David's reign.

**What is not stated this plainly is that these clans specifically descend from a second episode of Genesis 6:1-4's "sons of God" union.** The only verse anywhere in Scripture that makes that specific genealogical claim is Numbers 13:33 — and it is spoken by the ten spies, inside the report Numbers 13:32 itself calls a *dibbah*, by men who die for having brought it (14:36-37). The ESV Study Bible's note on this passage states the caution plainly: "Given the exaggerated tone of the spies' account, their reference to Nephilim is most likely an expression of their fear and an excuse for their disobedience, rather than an accurate statement indicating that the Nephilim were still living after the flood." Genesis's own genealogy in chapter 10 traces Canaan's descendants — the ancestor of the very peoples later called Anakim and Rephaim — through ordinary human lineage from Ham (Genesis 10:15-19), with no second supernatural incursion narrated anywhere in the text.

So: unusually large, formidable population groups in Canaan and Transjordan are not in question — Deuteronomy, Joshua, and Samuel establish that as plainly as any other historical detail in those books. That they were the literal genetic continuation of Genesis 6:4's Nephilim, surviving a flood that (per Genesis 7:21-23) destroyed "all flesh" outside the ark, is a claim Scripture puts only in the mouths of ten men under judgment for exaggerating — not a claim the text makes in its own narrating voice anywhere else. Holding the first as established and the second as unproven is not splitting the difference for its own sake; it's tracking where the text's own evidentiary weight falls.

### An archaeological footnote: the Kfar Monash spearheads

One physical find is worth reporting here, with its limits stated as clearly as its facts. In 1962, farmer Zvi Yizhar uncovered a buried cache of copper tools and weapons at Kfar Monash, on Israel's coastal Sharon Plain, dated by its excavators to the Early Bronze Age (the third millennium BC) — centuries before Israel's conquest narratives, but from the same land later associated with the Anakim and Rephaim. Archaeologists Ruth Hestrin and Miriam Tadmor published the hoard in 1963: alongside axes, adzes, daggers, and hundreds of copper plates, it contained four spearheads, the largest of which runs 66 cm long and weighs 2.05 kg — oversized by the standard of Bronze Age Levantine weapons typology, with a pronounced midrib and a massive hooked tang. It's on permanent display at the Israel Museum in Jerusalem.

What this does *not* establish is a population of giants. Nothing about the hoard's context ties it to unusually tall skeletal remains, and no burial or human remains were found with it at all — it's a buried weapons-and-tools cache, not a grave. The open scholarly question about pieces this large in Bronze Age Levantine hoards is whether they were made for combat at all, or as display and prestige objects signaling a smith's or owner's status — a question raised about oversized Levantine bladed weapons generally, not a verdict specific to Kfar Monash. An oversized spearhead is real, dated, and museum-held evidence of skilled and ambitious Bronze Age metalworking in Canaan; it is popular giant-hunting literature, not the archaeologists who published it, that reads it as evidence for giant-statured owners. The physical fact and the Nephilim-adjacent interpretation are two different things, and this study reports only the first as established.

## Are the Nephilim still around?

The Bible's own narrative gives this one a clean stopping point: 2 Samuel 21:22 names the last four "born to the giant in Gath," records that "they fell by the hand of David and by the hand of his servants," and the giant clans do not appear again in the Old Testament narrative after that. No further mention, OT or NT, describes Nephilim, Rephaim, Anakim, or any comparable group as present, active, or expected to return.

The one New Testament text that gets reached for here is Jesus's own comparison in Matthew 24:

> ✝️ Matthew 24:37-39 (ESV)
>
> 37 For as were the days of Noah, so will be the coming of the Son of Man. 38 For as in those days before the flood they were eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, until the day when Noah entered the ark, 39 and they were unaware until the flood came and swept them all away, so will be the coming of the Son of Man.

Jesus tells his hearers, in the same breath, exactly what the comparison is about: ordinary life continuing — eating, drinking, marrying — in complete unawareness, "until the flood came and swept them all away." That's the whole content of the analogy as Jesus states it: sudden judgment arriving on people who saw no reason to expect it. He does not mention the Nephilim, the sons of God, or anything about their supposed return in this text or anywhere else. Extending "as the days of Noah" into a claim about giants, or angelic corruption, reappearing before Christ's coming reads a great deal into four words that Jesus himself immediately defines in a different direction. That extension is popular in some corners of prophecy teaching, and a curious reader can pursue it (see References below) — but it isn't what this passage itself says, and this study isn't going to claim more for it than the text supports.

## Theological principle

Two questions, two different kinds of answer, and the difference between them is itself the lesson. Where Scripture speaks in its own narrating voice — the giant clans' historical reality, their eventual defeat, Jesus's own stated content for "the days of Noah" — the text is plain and this study can be equally plain. Where Scripture leaves a question open — the identity of the "sons of God," whether a second angelic incursion followed the flood — three centuries of serious, orthodox interpreters have disagreed, and the responsible response to that is to say so, not to manufacture a false certainty in either direction. Reverence for Scripture means resisting the pull to sound more certain than the text itself is.

## Discussion questions

1. Before this study, which view of "sons of God" would you have assumed without knowing there were others? Does knowing Job uses the identical phrase for angels change how you weigh that view?
2. Numbers 13:32 calls the spies' report a *dibbah* — "evil report" — and the text records real consequences for it. What does it look like, in your own life, to let real fear curdle into an exaggerated account that others then act on?
3. Caleb and Joshua saw the same fortified cities and the same tall Anakim the other ten spies did (Numbers 14:6-9), and drew the opposite conclusion. What made the difference wasn't the facts on the ground — what was it?
4. This study concludes that Matthew 24:37 is about complacency, not about giants returning. Where else have you seen a short, memorable phrase from Scripture get stretched to carry more than its own context supports?
