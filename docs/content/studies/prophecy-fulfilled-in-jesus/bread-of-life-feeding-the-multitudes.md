# Bread of Life: The Feeding of the 5,000 and the 4,000

Two hillside meals, four Gospel accounts, and one question Jesus asks afterwards that his disciples
cannot answer: *"Do you not yet understand?"* (Mark 8:21, ESV). This study works through what there was
to understand.

## The two feedings, side by side

| | **The 5,000** | **The 4,000** |
|---|---|---|
| Accounts | Matthew 14:13-21; Mark 6:30-44; Luke 9:10-17; John 6:1-15 | Matthew 15:32-39; Mark 8:1-10 |
| Where | Jewish Galilee -- near Bethsaida (Luke 9:10), across the Sea of Tiberias (John 6:1) | Following the circuit through Tyre, Sidon and the Decapolis (Mark 7:24, 7:31) -- Gentile territory |
| Loaves | 5 (barley, John 6:9) + 2 fish | 7 + "a few small fish" |
| Crowd | about 5,000 men, besides women and children | about 4,000 men, besides women and children (Matt 15:38) |
| Duration | one late afternoon | three days (Mark 8:2) |
| Left over | 12 **κόφινοι** (*kophinoi*) | 7 **σπυρίδες** (*spyrides*) |
| Blessing verb | εὐλόγησεν (*eulogēsen*, "blessed") | εὐχαριστήσας (*eucharistēsas*, "having given thanks") |
| Aftermath | the crowd tries to make him king by force (John 6:15); the Bread of Life discourse follows | the Pharisees demand a sign (Mark 8:11); Jesus rebukes the disciples over the loaves (8:14-21) |

The feeding of the 5,000 is the only miracle in all four Gospels — which says something about how
central the early church took it to be.

## Are these really two events?

Some readers assume the second feeding is the first one retold, a single story that grew a second
version in transmission. The text answers that itself, and hard.

**First, Jesus recalls both, with both sets of numbers**, in a single conversation:

> ✝️ Mark 8:19-21 (ESV)
>
> 19 "When I broke the five loaves for the five thousand, how many baskets full of broken pieces did
> you take up?" They said to him, "Twelve."
> 20 "And the seven for the four thousand, how many baskets full of broken pieces did you take up?"
> And they said to him, "Seven."
> 21 And he said to them, "Do you not yet understand?"

**Second — and this is the decisive detail — the Greek uses two different words for "basket," and
never once mixes them up.** Concordanced across the entire Greek New Testament:

- **κόφινος** (*kophinos*, G2894) — Matthew 14:20; 16:9; Mark 6:43; 8:19; Luke 9:17; John 6:13.
  Six occurrences, and every single one belongs to the feeding of the 5,000 or to Jesus's later
  recollection of it.
- **σπυρίς** (*spyris*, G4711) — Matthew 15:37; 16:10; Mark 8:8; 8:20. Four occurrences, and every one
  belongs to the 4,000 or its recollection. The word appears once more in the New Testament, at
  Acts 9:25, where Paul is lowered down a city wall in one — so a *spyris* is a large hamper, big
  enough to hold a man.

Four Gospel writers, two feedings, ten occurrences, not a single crossover in either direction. Whatever
else the accounts do, they are not confused about which meal they describe. Nearly all English versions
render both words "basket," so an English reader cannot see the argument at all — Jesus's own careful
distinction at Mark 8:19-20 disappears in translation.

One further inference, worth separating from that hard evidence: the lexicons commonly identify a
*kophinos* as the small stiff wicker basket a Jewish traveller carried his provisions in, and *spyris*
as the general Hellenistic word for a large hamper. If that holds, the vocabulary quietly marks the two
crowds as Jewish and Gentile — twelve Jewish provision baskets in Galilee, seven Greek hampers in the
Decapolis. The distinction between the words is textual fact; the ethnic marker is an inference, and
should be held as one.

## Historical and cultural background

**Where the second feeding happens matters.** Mark 7:24 has Jesus going to Tyre and Sidon; 7:31 brings
him back "through the region of the Decapolis" — and it is there, "in those days" (8:1), that the 4,000
are fed. Matthew adds a detail strange if the crowd were Jewish and natural if it were not: watching him
heal, they "glorified the God of *Israel*" (Matthew 15:31, ESV). Mark 8:3's "some of them have come from
far away" and the three-day duration fit a thinly-settled Gentile region with no nearby Jewish village
to buy bread in — the opposite of Mark 6:36, where the disciples assume villages are close enough to
send five thousand people shopping.

**Two hundred denarii** (Mark 6:37; John 6:7) is not a rhetorical figure. At the standard rate of a
denarius for a day's labour (Matthew 20:2), that is two hundred days' wages — around eight months of
work for one meal. The disciples have actually done the arithmetic, and the number is their way of
saying *this cannot be done*.

**Barley, and the season.** John alone specifies the loaves were **κριθίνους** (*krithinous*, "of
barley," G2916) — the cheap grain, animal fodder and poor people's bread. He alone also dates the
miracle: "the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was at hand" (6:4). Passover falls at the barley harvest,
so the detail is agriculturally coherent — and it puts the whole sign under the shadow of the feast at
which a lamb is killed and unleavened bread eaten.

**The blessing.** Mark 6:41's "he blessed" (**εὐλόγησεν**, *eulogēsen*) reflects the Jewish *berakhah*
over bread, spoken *to God* rather than pronounced over the food — "Blessed are you, LORD our God, King
of the universe, who brings forth bread from the earth." Jesus does what any Jewish head of a household
did at any meal. At the second feeding, in Gentile territory, Mark instead writes **εὐχαριστήσας**
(*eucharistēsas*, "having given thanks," 8:6) — as does John at the first (6:11). Too small to build on
alone, but it points the same way as the baskets.

## Old Testament roots

### The manna

Everything in this episode is standing on Exodus 16. God rains bread from heaven; the people are to
gather it **a day at a time**; and the arrangement is explicitly a test:

> ✝️ Exodus 16:4 (ESV)
>
> "Behold, I am about to rain bread from heaven for you, and the people shall go out and gather a
> day's portion every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not."

The bread was named with a question. Seeing it, Israel said **מָן הוּא** (*mān hū'*), "What is it?"
(Exodus 16:15) — and the name stuck. The *Theological Wordbook of the Old Testament* lists two
separate roots for these same consonants, 1209 (*mān*, "manna") and 1210 (*mān*, "what"), which
preserves the pun in the lexicon itself. Israel's daily bread was called *Whatsit*.

Two rules governed it, and both matter here. It could not be hoarded — kept overnight it "bred worms
and stank" (Exodus 16:20) — and yet nobody went short: "whoever gathered much had nothing left over,
and whoever gathered little had no lack" (16:18, ESV). Sufficiency, enforced in both directions.
Deuteronomy states the purpose outright:

> ✝️ Deuteronomy 8:3 (ESV)
>
> And he humbled you and let you hunger and fed you with manna, which you did not know, nor did your
> fathers know, that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone, but man lives by
> every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD.

That is the verse Jesus quotes back at Satan in the wilderness when invited to make bread for himself
(Matthew 4:4). Hold the two scenes together: **offered bread for his own hunger, he refuses and quotes
Deuteronomy 8; faced with a hungry crowd, he makes bread without being asked.** The miracle is not
about whether bread can be produced. It never was.

### The craving

A second wilderness food story is the dark twin of the first. In Numbers 11 the people have manna and
want meat:

> ✝️ Numbers 11:4-6 (ESV)
>
> "Oh that we had meat to eat! We remember the fish we ate in Egypt that cost nothing, the cucumbers,
> the melons, the leeks, the onions, and the garlic. But now our strength is dried up, and there is
> nothing at all but this manna to look at."

God gives them what they crave, in obscene quantity — quail heaped three feet deep for a day's journey
in every direction (11:31) — and then:

> ✝️ Numbers 11:33-34 (ESV)
>
> While the meat was yet between their teeth, before it was consumed, the anger of the LORD was
> kindled against the people, and the LORD struck down the people with a very great plague.
> Therefore the name of that place was called Kibroth-hattaavah, because there they buried the people
> who had the craving.

**קִבְרוֹת הַתַּאֲוָה** — *Qivrot ha-Ta'avah*, "the graves of craving." Psalm 78 gives the verdict on
both wilderness stories in one breath: God "rained down on them manna to eat" (78:24) and also "gave
them what they craved" (78:29) — and the craving killed them (78:29-31). The judgment is the text's own,
not a reader's moralising: not punished for being hungry, punished for despising sufficiency and
demanding surplus.

Notice what they say they miss: *the fish we ate in Egypt*. On a Galilean hillside, Jesus hands out
bread and fish, freely, and nobody dies.

### The prophet, the shepherd, and Elisha's barley loaves

Three more Old Testament threads run straight into these accounts.

**"Like sheep without a shepherd."** Mark's stated reason for the first feeding is not hunger: "he had
compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd" (6:34, ESV). The phrase is Moses's,
asking God for a successor so that "the congregation of the LORD may not be as sheep that have no
shepherd" (Numbers 27:17) — the LXX shares the key vocabulary with Mark (πρόβατα … ποιμήν), though not
verbatim. Ezekiel makes it a promise about God himself:

> ✝️ Ezekiel 34:15-16 (WEB)
>
> "I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep, and I will cause them to lie down," says the Lord
> Yahweh. "I will seek that which was lost, and will bring back that which was driven away, and will
> bind up that which was broken, and will strengthen that which was sick…"

Mark's next moves are exactly that: Jesus makes the crowd **lie down** on **green grass** (6:39) and
they are **fed to the full** (6:42). The Greek carries a pun English can't: "satisfied" is
**ἐχορτάσθησαν** (*echortasthēsan*, G5526), from **χόρτος** (*chortos*, "grass") — the verb means, at
root, *to pasture*. Shepherdless sheep made to lie down in green pasture and grazed to satisfaction:
Psalm 23:1-2, performed.

**The prophet like Moses.** The crowd's conclusion is correct: "This is indeed the Prophet who is to
come into the world!" (John 6:14, ESV) — Deuteronomy 18:15-18's prophet like Moses, identified by a man
who has just fed Israel in a wilderness with bread from heaven. Their inference is wrong, and violently
so: they move to "take him by force to make him king" (6:15), and he withdraws. A bread-king was exactly
the Messiah first-century Galilee under Roman occupation wanted.

**Elisha's twenty loaves.** The closest structural parallel anywhere in the Old Testament is not the
manna but a small miracle in 2 Kings:

> ✝️ 2 Kings 4:42-44 (WEB)
>
> A man from Baal Shalishah came, and brought the man of God some bread of the first fruits: twenty
> loaves of **barley** and fresh ears of grain in his sack. Elisha said, "Give to the people, that they
> may eat." His servant said, "What, should I set this before a hundred men?" But he said, "Give it to
> the people, that they may eat; for Yahweh says, 'They will eat, and **will have some left over**.'"
> So he set it before them and they ate and **had some left over**, according to Yahweh's word.

Every beat of the Gospel accounts is here: an inadequate supply, a servant's incredulous objection at
the ratio, a command to distribute anyway, everyone eating, food left over. The loaves are barley, as in
John, and firstfruits — **לֶחֶם בִּכּוּרִים**, *leḥem bikkurim*, a construct chain with *leḥem* in the
construct state, so literally "bread of firstfruits," harvest-offering grain. Jesus does what Elisha did
at forty times the scale, and Elisha was already doing it "according to the word of the LORD." Only now
the one giving the word and the one distributing the bread are the same person.

### Three details in how Mark stages it

**They are seated the way Moses organised Israel.** The crowd sits "by hundreds and by fifties" (6:40)
— the administrative units Moses used when he appointed leaders on Jethro's advice: "rulers of thousands,
rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens" (Exodus 18:25, WEB; the *ESV Study Bible*'s
note on Mark 6:39-40 draws the same connection). A wilderness, a hungry crowd, and a man ordering Israel
into Mosaic ranks — the new-Moses framing is in the staging, before a loaf is broken.

**"*You* give them something to eat" is emphatic, and the two "them"s are different people.** The Greek
runs *Δότε αὐτοῖς ὑμεῖς φαγεῖν*, and **ὑμεῖς** ("you") is the point: an imperative already carries its
subject in the verb ending, so a separate subject pronoun is emphatic — "***you*** give them something
to eat." The contrast Jesus draws, you rather than the villages you just proposed, is marked in the
grammar, not merely available to a translator.

That verse then uses *αὐτοῖς* ("to them") twice, four words apart, for two different groups: the first
(*he said to them*) is the **disciples**, the second (*give them something to eat*) the **crowd** — and
the infinitive "to eat" takes the crowd as its subject too. English can't keep those apart, so a reader
following "he said to them… give them something to eat" cannot see the sentence pivot. Unpacked: *you
disciples give the crowd something for the crowd to eat.* Relatedly, verse 41's four verbs have no stated
subject at all; it carries from **Mark 6:30**, eleven verses earlier. The whole feeding is narrated off
one distant mention of Jesus's name.

**And Mark has just shown us the other kind of banquet.** Immediately before, Herod throws a birthday
"supper for his nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of Galilee" (6:21) — a private feast for the
powerful, ending with John the Baptist's head on a platter. Then Jesus hosts a meal on the grass for
anyone who walked there, and everyone leaves full. Ancient writers habitually set characters against
each other this way (*NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible*), and the juxtaposition is hard to miss once
seen: two banquets, two kings, back to back.

## What Jesus actually taught: John 6

The crowd, fed once, comes looking for him again the next day (John 6:22-25). What does he give them?

Not bread.

> ✝️ John 6:26-27 (ESV)
>
> Jesus answered them, "Truly, truly, I say to you, you are seeking me, not because you saw signs, but
> because you ate your fill of the loaves. Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food
> that endures to eternal life, which the Son of Man will give to you."

That is the pivot of the episode, and the rebuke is precise. He does not say *you are wrong to be
hungry*; he says they came for the wrong thing — they witnessed a **sign** (σημεῖον) and treated it as
a **meal**. The verb in "do not work" is **ἐργάζεσθε** (*ergazesthe*, G2038), labour or work for wages,
and it has a prophetic ancestor saying nearly the same thing in nearly the same words:

> ✝️ Isaiah 55:1-2 (ESV)
>
> Come, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters; and he who has no money, come, buy and eat! … Why do
> you spend your money for that which is not bread, and your **labor** for that which does not
> satisfy?

The Hebrew is **יְגִיעַ** (*yəgîaʿ*, H3018, "toil, the product of toil") spent **לְלוֹא לְשָׂבְעָה**
(*lelo' lesov'ah*), "for not-to-satisfaction" — the same complaint as Jesus's: you are working hard for
food that cannot fill you, when what fills is free.

The crowd counters by quoting Scripture at him: *Moses gave our fathers manna — what will you do?*
(6:30-31). Their citation is from the Greek Psalter, and it checks out. LXX Psalm 77:24 (= Psalm 78:24)
reads *"and he rained manna on them to eat, and he gave them bread of heaven"*; John 6:31 has *"he gave
them bread out of heaven to eat"* — the same words, recombined from the psalm verse's two halves. They
know the text.

Jesus's answer turns on a verb tense. It was **not Moses** who gave that bread, and the giving is
**not finished**: "my Father **gives** you the true bread from heaven" — present tense, happening now,
in front of them (6:32). Then:

> ✝️ John 6:35 (ESV)
>
> Jesus said to them, "I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever
> believes in me shall never thirst."

**Ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ ἄρτος τῆς ζωῆς** — the first of John's seven "I am" sayings. Note what just happened
structurally. They asked for a permanent manna supply: "Sir, give us this bread always" (6:34, ESV —
the Greek **κύριε**, *kyrie*, can be a confession of lordship or a polite "sir," and the ESV reads the
crowd as merely deferential, which the rest of the chapter bears out). His reply refuses the request in
the only way that could satisfy it: *the bread is not a thing I dispense, it is who I am.* The gift and
the Giver are the same — which is why more bread would not have been a better answer.

### The offensive verb

As the discourse presses on, John changes his vocabulary. Through 6:49-53 the verb for eating is the
ordinary **ἐσθίω/φαγεῖν** (*esthiō/phagein*); from 6:54 he switches to **τρώγω** (*trōgō*, G5176), a
blunter word — "chew, gnaw, munch" — and keeps it to the end in present-tense participles (6:54, 56, 57,
58): *the one who keeps chewing my flesh*. Concordanced across the New Testament, *trōgō* occurs only
six times — four here, one of Judas "who ate my bread" (John 13:18), one of the world eating and
drinking before the flood (Matthew 24:38).

Credit where due: **the ESV marks this shift and marks it consistently**, rendering "eat" for *esthiō*
through 6:49-53 and "**feeds on**" at all four *trōgō* verses, so an ESV reader sees the change without
knowing Greek. Most versions can't say the same — WEB and the Berean Study Bible use plain "eats" at
6:54, 56 and 58, catching it only at 6:57; the ASV has "eateth" straight through.

Against overreading: by the first century *trōgō* had largely become an ordinary word for "eat," so it
won't bear "John chose a bestial verb on purpose." But the switch is deliberate — it arrives exactly
when the crowd starts objecting (6:52), and it is sustained. Jesus becomes *more* concrete under
pressure, not less. And the crowd hears it: "This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?" (6:60), after
which "many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him" (6:66).

### A thread worth following in a second translation

A second word runs through this chapter, and here the honours go the other way. In "the food that
**endures** to eternal life" (6:27, ESV) the verb is **μένω** (*menō*, G3306) — abide, remain — and it
is the same verb twenty-nine verses later: "whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood **abides** in
me, and I in him" (6:56, ESV). Food that abides, in a person who abides: one word, one thread.

No major modern version keeps that thread visible. The ESV renders it "endures" then "abides"; the WEB
has "remains" then "lives"; the Berean Study Bible has "endures" then "remains". Each breaks the link
in a different place. The one that holds it together is the old ASV (1901), with "**abideth**" in both
verses — the same translation that flattens *trōgō*.

Two threads, two different Bibles, and no single English version showing both — which is worth knowing
as a habit rather than a curiosity about this chapter: where a word is doing structural work, read the
passage in a second, more woodenly literal translation, and expect each version to be strong in
different places.

### The words that became the Supper

One more verified detail. Mark narrates the feeding with four verbs in sequence: he **took**
(λαβών), **blessed** (εὐλόγησεν), **broke** (κατέκλασεν), **gave** (ἐδίδου) — Mark 6:41. At the Last
Supper he uses the same four lemmas in the same order: **took** (λαβών), **blessed** (εὐλογήσας),
**broke** (ἔκλασεν), **gave** (ἔδωκεν) — Mark 14:22, "Take; this is my body." The feeding is narrated
in the vocabulary the church would later use for the bread of the Supper, and John 6:51 makes the
connection the discourse's own climax: "the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my
flesh." (For the Passover setting of that later meal, see
[The Last Supper and the Four Cups](../feasts/last-supper-four-cups.md).)

## Is there significance in the numbers?

**Jesus himself treats the numbers as meaningful.** At Mark 8:19-21 he does not re-explain the
miracles; he has the disciples recite the figures — five, twelve, seven, seven — then rebukes them for
still not understanding (Matthew 16:9-12 records the same exchange). So the numbers are load-bearing.
What the text never does is *say what they mean*, hence the tiering below. The framework is this site's
own [Hebrew alphabet](../../hebrew-studies/hebrew-alphabet.md#numbers-as-symbols) page, and so is its
caution: a pattern to notice, not a code to crack.

**On firm ground — the counts Jesus repeats.** The loaves and baskets are exact, and they are what he
quotes back. The crowd sizes are not: Matthew 14:21 and Mark 8:9 both say "about" (ὡσεί / ὡς). So
whatever the numbers carry rests on *five, twelve, seven, seven*, not on the thousands.

**A strong pattern — twelve and seven.**

- **Twelve** baskets, gathered by **twelve** disciples, in Jewish Galilee — Israel's number throughout
  Scripture: twelve tribes, twelve apostles, the New Jerusalem's twelve gates and foundations
  (Revelation 21:12-14). Israel is fed, a full basket left per disciple.
- **Seven** loaves and **seven** hampers, in Gentile territory — completion and wholeness from
  Genesis 2:2 onward. With the geography and the basket vocabulary, the two meals together say: Israel
  first, completely provided for; then the nations, completely provided for, out of the same hands. The
  pattern of Romans 1:16 and of Isaiah 25:6-9's banquet "for all peoples," told in loaves.

**Traditional, and speculative.** A long tradition reads the **five** loaves as the five books of Moses
(fitting, if the sign presents Jesus as the prophet like Moses); Bullinger's scheme makes five the number
of grace; others note that five loaves plus two fish makes seven. None of this is stated in Scripture,
none of it is anything Mark or John does with the numbers, and no doctrine here depends on it. Enjoy it
as an observation; don't preach it as an argument.

**Plainly literal.** The three days (Mark 8:2) explain why the crowd would faint on the road; the two
hundred denarii is a wage calculation. Not everything numbered is a symbol.

## Theological principle

God's provision in Christ is genuinely abundant — twelve baskets and seven hampers more than the
crowds could eat — but it is given to reveal the Giver, not to remove the need for him. So the sign
gets misread in two opposite directions, and Scripture corrects both: the crowd in John 6 wants the
bread without the Bread, and the generation in Numbers 11 wants more than the daily portion. One is
offered a person and walks away (6:66); the other gets what it craved, and a plague with it
(11:33-34; Psalm 78:29-31). Between grasping for surplus and despising sufficiency, the pattern
taught is to take today's portion from the Father's hand and let it point past itself to the Son.

This doesn't rest on the narratives alone. It is stated flatly elsewhere: "man does not live by bread
alone" (Deuteronomy 8:3); "seek first the kingdom of God… and all these things will be added to you"
(Matthew 6:33); "why do you spend… your labor for that which does not satisfy?" (Isaiah 55:2); "give
me neither poverty nor riches… lest I be full and deny you" (Proverbs 30:8-9); "if we have food and
clothing, with these we will be content" (1 Timothy 6:8).

## Jesus is our manna: the link to "daily bread"

The petition "Give us this day our daily bread" (Matthew 6:11) turns on **ἐπιούσιος** (*epiousios*),
the rarest word in the New Testament — only here and Luke 11:3. As the
[Lord's Prayer study](../prayer/lords-prayer.md) works through, every proposed derivation converges on
the same force: *today's sufficiency, not a stockpile*. Its Old Testament model is the manna, which
could not be stockpiled by design (Exodus 16:18-20) — and which Jesus then identifies as pointing at
himself (John 6:32-35, 6:49-51).

So the petition is not a small domestic request inside an otherwise lofty prayer. It is the same trust
the manna was built to teach, and — read through John 6 — a daily request for Christ himself, who does
not spoil, cannot be hoarded, and has to be received again today. That is also the shape of the
warning: the wilderness generation's sin was not hunger but craving *amplification* — more than the
portion, security stored against tomorrow so that today's dependence could end. Ask for today; ask
again tomorrow. A stockpile would end the relationship that is the point.

The text keeps a balance here, though, because this overshoots easily into a spirituality of scarcity.
Jesus does not feed people meanly, and it is **he** who orders the leftovers gathered: "Gather up the
leftover fragments, that nothing may be lost" (John 6:12, ESV). Abundance is not the enemy of daily
dependence — waste and hoarding are.

## Key lessons

- **The miracle was a sign, not a service.** The crowd that treated it as catering got rebuked.
- **Jesus refuses to be useful on the wrong terms.** Offered a crown for feeding people, he withdraws
  (John 6:15); asked for a permanent bread supply, he offers himself (6:34-35). He will not be reduced
  to a solution for felt needs.
- **He uses what the disciples have, not what they lack.** "How many loaves do you have? Go and see"
  (Mark 6:38) — and *they* do the distributing.
- **Israel first, then the nations, from the same hands.** Twelve Jewish baskets in Galilee, seven
  hampers in the Decapolis, and Jesus insisting the disciples remember both sets of numbers.
- **Provision teaches dependence, and surplus can end it.** Proverbs 30:8-9 states the reason plainly:
  full barns tempt us to forget who filled them.
- **Daily bread is a christological request.** After John 6 it cannot be only about groceries.

1. Jesus rebukes them for seeking him "because you ate your fill of the loaves" (6:26). What would it
   look like to seek Jesus *for* something rather than for himself — and how would you know you were?
2. Asking for the bread "always" (6:34) sounds like faith. Why was it the wrong request, and what did
   Jesus give instead?
3. Numbers 11 has God give people exactly what they craved, as judgment. Does that change how you think
   about answered prayers for more than you need?
4. Jesus starts with five loaves someone actually had. Where are you waiting for adequate resources
   before offering what you've got?
5. "Give us this day our daily bread" asks for today only. What in your life is organised around never
   needing to pray that again?
6. The disciples watched both feedings and still worried about one loaf in the boat (Mark 8:14-21).
   Where do you have a track record of God's provision you've stopped drawing on?

## References & Recommended Reading

- **ESV Bible** (Crossway) — primary translation quoted throughout, per this site's translation
  conventions. **World English Bible (WEB)** used for the Old Testament background passages
  (2 Kings 4:42-44, Ezekiel 34:15-16), quoted from this repo's own text database. **ASV (1901)** and
  the **Berean Study Bible** consulted for the translation comparisons on *menō* and *trōgō* above.
  Note that the WEB is a public-domain revision of the ASV whose New Testament leans on the
  Majority Text, while the ESV stands in the Tyndale-KJV stream and follows a critical text — hence
  small divergences like Matthew 15:39's "Magdala" (WEB, YLT) against "Magadan" (ESV, ASV, BSB).
- **MACULA Greek Linguistic Datasets (SBLGNT)** and **MACULA Hebrew Linguistic Datasets (WLC)**,
  queried via this repo's `references/build/bible-text.db` — the source of every Strong's number,
  Louw-Nida domain code, morphological note, and concordance count above, including the
  *kophinos*/*spyris* distribution and the *trōgō* count. Its clause-level syntax and coreference
  annotation supplied the emphatic *ὑμεῖς* in Mark 6:37, the disambiguation of that verse's two
  *αὐτοῖς*, the implicit subject of Mark 6:41's four verbs, and the construct state of *leḥem
  bikkurim* in 2 Kings 4:42.
- **Brenton's Septuagint** (public domain), same database — used to verify that John 6:31 quotes
  LXX Psalm 77:24, and to compare Numbers 27:17's shepherd language with Mark 6:34.
- ***Theological Wordbook of the Old Testament*** (TWOT), ed. R. Laird Harris, Gleason L. Archer Jr.,
  and Bruce K. Waltke (Moody Publishers) — roots 1105a (*leḥem*) and 1209/1210 (*mān*, listed as two
  distinct roots for the same consonants), consulted via this repo's committed TWOT Strong's-number
  map.
- ***ESV Study Bible*** (Crossway, 2016) — consulted after the exegesis above was drafted, as a check
  rather than a source. Independently confirmed the Numbers 27:17 / Ezekiel 34 shepherd background, the
  Psalm 23:2 echo, the 200-denarii wage calculation, and the Elisha parallel; its note on Mark 6:39-40
  supplied the Exodus 18:25 "hundreds and fifties" connection, which I had missed.
- ***NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible*** (Zondervan, 2016), ed. John H. Walton and Craig S. Keener —
  likewise consulted last. Source of the Herod's-banquet contrast noted above, and of the observation
  that green grass places the scene in springtime, corroborating John 6:4's Passover dating.
- **E. W. Bullinger**, *Number in Scripture* (1894) — the classical source behind the number-symbolism
  framework this site summarises on its [Hebrew alphabet](../../hebrew-studies/hebrew-alphabet.md)
  page, deferred to rather than duplicated in the numbers section above. Public domain.

  study), [The Last Supper and the Four Cups](../feasts/last-supper-four-cups.md), and
