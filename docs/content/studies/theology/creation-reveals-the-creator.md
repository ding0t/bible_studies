# What Creation Declares: General Revelation in Romans 1:19-23

No one who has ever lived gets to say God left no evidence. That is the specific, narrow claim Paul makes in Romans 1:19-23: creation itself -- apart from any prophet, any Scripture, any preacher -- already shows every person enough of God's power and nature to make unbelief a choice rather than an innocent gap in the evidence. This study works through that passage and its parallels first, then looks at where that ancient claim still shows up in the natural world today: in the staggering machinery inside a single cell, in the layers of rock and the bones buried in them, and in the light astronomers are only now old enough to see.

## Key Takeaways

### Lessons about Jesus

Creation's testimony to God's "eternal power and divine nature" (Romans 1:20, ESV) is not, in the end, testimony to a distant designer. Scripture names the one through whom it was made: "by him all things were created... and in him all things hold together" (Colossians 1:16-17, ESV); "he is the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature, and he upholds the universe by the word of his power" (Hebrews 1:3, ESV). What Romans 1 says every person can perceive in the created order is, on the New Testament's own terms, the power and nature of the eternal Word -- general revelation's object and special revelation's subject turn out to be the same person.

### Memory verses

- Romans 1:20 (ESV) -- "For his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made. So they are without excuse."
- Psalm 19:1 (ESV) -- "The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork."

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** General revelation is real and sufficient to condemn, but it was never designed to save. If your own confidence rests on "I believe in a Creator" without ever reckoning with the gospel Paul goes on to preach starting at Romans 1:16, you are standing exactly where Romans 1:18-23 says leaves a person without excuse -- not where it says a person is safe.
- **Attitude.** Romans 1:21 names the actual sin as ingratitude before it names idolatry: "they did not honor him as God or give thanks." Let genuine wonder at the created order -- a cell, a night sky, a fossil bed -- turn into thanksgiving addressed to someone, not just appreciation of a fact.
- **Do.** Practice naming God as the reason for what you notice in creation, out loud, to someone else -- the way Paul does extemporaneously in Acts 14 and 17. That is what turns a private conviction into the kind of testimony Romans 1:19-23 describes creation itself as already giving.

### Prayer

Lord, the heavens you stretched out and the intricate cell you designed both declare what no one alive can honestly say they never saw: your power, and that you are God. Forgive the times gratitude toward you gave way to self-sufficiency, or wonder at what you made stopped short of worship of the one who made it. Thank you that you did not leave the evidence there -- you sent your Son, "the exact imprint of your nature" (Hebrews 1:3), so that what creation could only prove, the gospel could actually save. Give me eyes to keep seeing you in what you have made, and a voice, like Paul's at Lystra and Athens, ready to say so. Amen.

## Historical and literary context

**Romans' setting.** Paul writes to a congregation he had not founded and had not yet visited (Romans 1:8-13), almost certainly from Corinth near the end of his third missionary journey, around AD 57, ahead of a hoped-for stop in Rome on his way to a new mission field in Spain (Romans 15:22-24, 15:28). Because he is introducing himself rather than answering a local crisis the way he does in Corinth or Galatia, Romans reads less like a letter to a known problem and more like a full, deliberate exposition of Paul's gospel -- which is exactly why 1:18-23 gets the space here that a shorter, more occasional letter would not have given it.

**Where 1:19-23 sits in Paul's argument.** Romans 1:16-17 states Paul's thesis: the gospel is God's power for salvation, because in it God's righteousness is revealed to be received by faith. Everything from 1:18 through 3:20 exists to establish why that gospel is needed by everyone -- Gentiles first (1:18-32), then Jews (2:1-3:8), then a summary verdict on both (3:9-20, "none is righteous, no, not one," 3:10) -- before Paul turns to how God's righteousness is given by faith in 3:21 onward. Romans 1:19-23 is not a stand-alone case for God's existence; it is the opening move in Paul's case for universal human guilt, and it makes that case on a specific ground: not that Gentiles never had the evidence, but that they suppressed it (1:18, katechontōn, "holding it down/back"). The **ESV Study Bible**'s note on 1:19-20 draws out a conclusion the passage's own place in Paul's argument requires: this text shows that general revelation does not save -- it removes excuse. Paul will say outright a chapter and a half later that "no one seeks for God" (3:11, ESV). That is the guardrail for everything that follows in this study: creation's testimony is real, but its job in Paul's argument is to convict, not to convert.

**Job's setting, for comparison.** Job is patriarchal-era wisdom literature -- undated, but set before the Mosaic law, among a wealth and lifespan pattern that fits the patriarchal age. Its climax (Job 38:1-42:6) is the Lord's own speech from the whirlwind, and it never answers Job's demand for an explanation of his suffering. Instead it reorients Job entirely around the created order he cannot command: the cosmos (ch. 38), the animal kingdom (ch. 39), and finally two exhibits framed as the Lord's own top examples of raw, untamable power -- Behemoth (40:15-24) and Leviathan (ch. 41). Centuries before Paul and in a wholly different genre, God is already doing in Job what Romans 1:20 describes creation as always doing: making his power perceptible through what he has made.

## Working through Romans 1:19-23

> ✝️ Romans 1:19-23 (ESV)
>
> 19 For what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them. 20 For his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made. So they are without excuse. 21 For although they knew God, they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him, but they became futile in their thinking, and their foolish hearts were darkened. 22 Claiming to be wise, they became fools, 23 and exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal man and birds and animals and creeping things.

**"What can be known" (v.19).** Paul's Greek is *to gnōston tou theou* (**γνωστόν**, *gnostos*, G1110) -- literally "the knowable [thing] of God." The word bounds the claim before it is even made: this is what *can* be known, not everything there is to know. Romans 1:19-23 is not claiming creation reveals the Trinity, the incarnation, or the cross -- only, as v.20 will specify, God's eternal power and divine nature.

**"Is plain... has shown" (v.19).** Paul pairs two words from the same root, one static and one active. **φανερός** (*phaneros*, G5318, "manifest, evident") describes the settled fact -- it simply is plain. Then **ἐφανέρωσεν**, from **φανερόω** (*phaneroo*, G5319), an aorist active verb -- "God made it manifest" -- credits the disclosure to God's own initiative, not to human reasoning that found him out. The verse's own grammar answers a question people sometimes bring to this passage: general revelation is not a philosophical proof humans construct by staring hard enough at nature; it is something God actively does to every person, that happens also to be evident.

**"His invisible attributes... in the things that have been made" (v.20).** The verse states its own paradox outright: **ἀόρατα** (*aorata*, G517, "invisible things") are **νοούμενα** (*nooumena*, from **νοέω**, *noeo*, G3539, "perceived by the mind" -- a present passive participle, ongoing perception, not a one-time epiphany) through **ποιήμασιν** (*poiemasin*, from **ποίημα**, *poiema*, G4161, "things made"). That last word has exactly one other New Testament occurrence: Ephesians 2:10, "we are his workmanship [*poiema*]." The same Greek word Paul uses for the created universe he uses for redeemed believers -- both, in Paul's vocabulary, are God's handiwork. The verb for how this perception happens, **καθορᾶται** (*kathoratai*, from **καθοράω**, *kathorao*, G2529), occurs nowhere else in the New Testament; the *kata-* prefix intensifies plain "seeing" into "seeing clearly, thoroughly."

**"Eternal power and divine nature" (v.20).** Two more rare words name what specifically gets perceived. **ἀΐδιος** (*aidios*, G126, "eternal, everlasting") appears in the New Testament only here and in Jude 6 ("eternal chains") -- distinct from the far more common *aiōnios*. **θειότης** (*theiotes*, G2305, "divine nature/quality") is a New Testament hapax legomenon, and worth distinguishing from a similar-looking word Paul uses elsewhere: Colossians 2:9 says the fullness of **θεότης** (*theotes*, "deity, Godhead") dwells bodily in Christ. *Theiotes* (Romans 1:20) is the divine quality perceivable *from* the works; *theotes* (Colossians 2:9) is the full essence of deity itself, revealed *in* Christ. That distinction is the whole argument of this study in miniature: creation shows you *that* there is a God of great and eternal power (*theiotes*); it takes the incarnate Son to show you *who* God fully is (*theotes*).

**"Without excuse" (v.20).** **ἀναπολόγητος** (*anapologetos*, G379) is a legal term -- the negated root of the English word "apologetics." It occurs only here and in Romans 2:1, both times about a person with no defense to offer. Paul's case has a specific shape: not "everyone should logically conclude God exists," but "no one has a legal leg to stand on for not honoring the God the evidence already disclosed."

**The actual sin (vv.21-23).** Verse 21 names the failure before naming its result: "although they knew God, they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him." The root sin is ingratitude and refused honor, not intellectual error -- the futile thinking and darkened hearts are consequence, not cause. What follows is a straight line, not a leap: knowing God without honoring him (v.21) becomes professed wisdom that is actually foolishness (v.22), which becomes trading the glory of the immortal God for images of mortal creatures (v.23) -- the concrete, first-century form this took being Greco-Roman idol worship, "images resembling mortal man and birds and animals and creeping things." The specific idols named are occasional detail; the underlying pattern -- suppressed truth about God relocates itself onto something less than God -- is the transcultural claim.

## The same claim, in other genres

Paul's argument in Romans 1 is not a novelty in Scripture; it is the theological argument Scripture makes repeatedly, in different genres, well before and after Romans was written.

> ✝️ Psalm 19:1-4 (ESV)
>
> 1 The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork. 2 Day to day pours out speech, and night to night reveals knowledge. 3 There is no speech, nor are there words, whose voice is not heard. 4 Their voice goes out through all the earth, and their words to the end of the world. In them he has set a tent for the sun,

David's praise psalm makes the identical claim Paul makes -- creation communicates ("pours out speech," "reveals knowledge") -- almost a millennium earlier, in worship rather than argument. Verse 3's deliberate paradox, "there is no speech... whose voice is not heard," makes the same point Romans 1:20 makes with *aorata... nooumena*: this communication crosses every human language barrier because it isn't verbal in the first place.

Paul himself makes the same appeal twice in Acts, both times to pagan audiences with no access to the Hebrew Scriptures:

> ✝️ Acts 14:15-17 (ESV)
>
> 15 "Men, why are you doing these things? We also are men, of like nature with you, and we bring you good news, that you should turn from these vain things to a living God, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and all that is in them. 16 In past generations he allowed all the nations to walk in their own ways. 17 Yet he did not leave himself without witness, for he did good by giving you rains from heaven and fruitful seasons, satisfying your hearts with food and gladness."

At Lystra, stopping a crowd from sacrificing to him and Barnabas as gods, Paul's on-the-spot appeal is entirely to general revelation -- rain, harvest, provision -- because that is the only common ground he and his hearers share in the moment.

> ✝️ Acts 17:24-28 (ESV)
>
> 24 The God who made the world and everything in it, being Lord of heaven and earth, does not live in temples made by man, 25 nor is he served by human hands, as though he needed anything, since he himself gives to all mankind life and breath and everything. 26 And he made from one man every nation of mankind to live on all the face of the earth, having determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their dwelling place, 27 that they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him and find him. Yet he is actually not far from each one of us, 28 for "'In him we live and move and have our being'; as even some of your own poets have said, "'For we are indeed his offspring.'"

At the Areopagus, Paul goes further still, quoting Greek poets approvingly (Epimenides and Aratus, by most identifications) mid-sermon. That is Romans 1:19-23 working exactly as Paul says it does: even pagan philosophy, suppressing the full truth, still retained fragments of what general revelation had shown it.

## Job's two exhibits: Behemoth and Leviathan

> ✝️ Job 38:4-7 (ESV)
>
> 4 "Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Tell me, if you have understanding. 5 Who determined its measurements -- surely you know! Or who stretched the line upon it? 6 On what were its bases sunk, or who laid its cornerstone, 7 when the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy?

God's answer to Job's suffering is not an explanation; it is an interrogation built entirely on the created order Job did not make and cannot command. The speech climaxes in two named creatures:

> ✝️ Job 40:15-19 (ESV)
>
> 15 "Behold, Behemoth, which I made as I made you; he eats grass like an ox. 16 Behold, his strength in his loins, and his power in the muscles of his belly. 17 He makes his tail stiff like a cedar; the sinews of his thighs are knit together. 18 His bones are tubes of bronze, his limbs like bars of iron. 19 "He is the first of the works of God; let him who made him bring near his sword!

> ✝️ Job 41:1 (ESV)
>
> 1 "Can you draw out Leviathan with a fishhook or press down his tongue with a cord?

Behemoth transliterates Hebrew <span dir="rtl">בְּהֵמוֹת</span> (*behemot*, H930, TWOT root 208b) -- an intensive plural of *behemah* ("beast," H929), functioning as a kind of superlative: "the beast of beasts." Leviathan is <span dir="rtl">לִוְיָתָן</span> (*livyatan*, H3882, TWOT root 1089b), from a root meaning "to twine or coil" -- "the twisted one." Identifying exactly which animals these names originally referred to is an open question, not a settled one, and this study represents both live positions rather than picking a winner the text itself doesn't clearly pick. The ESV Study Bible's note on 40:15 takes the majority scholarly view: Behemoth "almost universally" identified as a hippopotamus, and Leviathan (41:1 note) most likely a crocodile, with the more extravagant description -- "he makes his tail stiff like a cedar" -- explained as hyperbolic royal-court poetry emphasizing overwhelming, uncontrollable power rather than a literal zoological description. Young-earth writers, Answers in Genesis prominent among them, argue the fit is poor on its own terms: a hippopotamus's tail is a short, thin stub, nothing a Hebrew poet reaching for hyperbole would likely reach for a cedar tree to describe, and they identify Behemoth instead as a large sauropod-type dinosaur and Leviathan as some other now-extinct marine reptile, consistent with a young-earth timeline in which such creatures were still living when Job was written. Both readings agree on what actually matters for this study's argument: whatever Behemoth and Leviathan specifically were, the Lord names them as creatures no human can subdue, offered as living proof of a power Job has no answer to -- Romans 1:20's *aidios dynamis* ("eternal power"), acted out narratively centuries before Paul puts a name to the theological principle.

## What creation still declares

Romans 1:20 doesn't name a mechanism for how "the things that have been made" show God's eternal power -- Paul's readers had no access to cell biology, geology, or astrophysics. What follows is not exegesis of Romans 1; it's illustration, drawn from outside Scripture, of what that verse claims creation has always been doing. Answers in Genesis and the Institute for Creation Research, the sources most associated with this reading and the ones behind the arguments below, hold a young-earth, flood-geology framework; several of the specific empirical claims here are contested within mainstream science, and this study says so where that's the case rather than presenting one side's account as settled.

**The cell.** However complex biologists once assumed the "simplest" living cell must be, actual attempts to build a minimal one have found the floor higher than expected. In 2016, the J. Craig Venter Institute published a synthetic organism, JCVI-syn3.0, engineered to carry only the genes a free-living cell needs and nothing more -- and still required 473 genes to survive and reproduce, 149 of them with functions still not understood (Hutchison et al., *Science* 351.6280, 2016). That result doesn't depend on any view of the earth's age; it's simply how much irreducible machinery even the most stripped-down cell scientists have ever built needs. Answers in Genesis and other design proponents argue the interdependence goes further still: DNA, RNA, and protein each depend on the other two to function at all, so a system that only "works" once every part is already present is difficult to build by the small, sequential, each-step-must-help survival changes evolutionary theory requires. This is the same shape of argument Michael Behe made famous with the bacterial flagellum in *Darwin's Black Box* (1996) -- "irreducible complexity" -- and it remains contested in the scientific literature: critics point to partial-function precursor structures like the Type III secretory system as a possible evolutionary stepping-stone, and design proponents respond that a working precursor for a different function doesn't establish a viable path to the flagellum specifically. The debate is real and ongoing; what isn't contested is the underlying fact the argument starts from -- even "simple" life is staggeringly intricate.

**The fossil record and the flood.** Answers in Genesis and the Institute for Creation Research read the global fossil record as the deposit of a single catastrophic, worldwide flood (Genesis 6-9) rather than gradual accumulation over deep time -- marine creatures at the lowest layers, then successive ecological zones buried as flood waters rose, which they argue explains why marine fossils appear in rock layers on every continent, including near the top of the Himalayas. Their strongest specific evidence is the polystrate fossil: a single tree trunk running vertically through several rock layers that, by ordinary slow-deposition timelines, would represent thousands of years -- evidence, they argue, that those layers instead formed rapidly around the tree, not gradually over it. Mainstream geology's standard reply, developed at type sites like the Joggins Fossil Cliffs in Nova Scotia, is that these trees are typically found still rooted in a paleosol (an ancient soil horizon), consistent with the tree having grown in place and then been buried over time by normal sedimentation, not dropped in from a floating log mat -- a reading creationist geologists dispute in turn, pointing to an absence of clearly mature paleosols at some of these sites and to some polystrate trees found upside down, which growing-in-place doesn't obviously explain. This is a live dispute between young-earth and mainstream geology, not a settled question either direction, and this study doesn't adjudicate it -- only reports both sides' actual argument.

**Soft tissue in dinosaur bone.** In 2005, paleontologist Mary Schweitzer reported flexible, apparently soft material -- blood-vessel-like structures and what appeared to be cells -- recovered from inside a *Tyrannosaurus rex* femur conventionally dated at roughly 68 million years old. Answers in Genesis's David Menton argued in 2012 that this level of preservation is implausible over that timespan and fits far better with a young-earth timeline of a few thousand years, comparable to soft-tissue preservation documented in Egyptian mummies and the Tyrolean Iceman. Schweitzer's own account is worth stating plainly because it complicates a simple version of this story either direction: she is an outspoken Christian, was raised in a young-earth-creationist environment herself, and does not accept the young-earth conclusion drawn from her own discovery. Her ongoing research instead argues that iron released from hemoglobin during decay can cross-link and preserve tissue proteins far longer than previously modeled -- a new fossilization mechanism, not evidence the bone is thousands rather than millions of years old. The dispute here is over whether soft-tissue survival requires revising *how long* preservation can naturally occur, or revising *how old* the fossils actually are -- and the paleontologist who made the discovery has been explicit that she reads it as the former.

**The early universe.** In 2022, the James Webb Space Telescope began returning images of galaxies at greater distances -- and therefore greater lookback times -- than any previous instrument. Answers in Genesis and the Institute for Creation Research both published articles arguing the early galaxies JWST found are more numerous, more massive, and more structurally mature than Big Bang cosmology predicted for the universe's infancy, and that this counts as evidence against the Big Bang model itself. Mainstream astronomers agree the early, massive galaxies JWST found are a real and unexpected result -- but read it as a problem for the *details* of current galaxy-formation models (how quickly galaxies can assemble), not as evidence against the Big Bang framework as a whole, which rests on several independent lines of evidence (the cosmic microwave background, the observed abundance of light elements, the redshift-distance relation) that JWST's galaxy counts don't address directly. It's also worth being precise about what JWST was actually built to do: no telescope can observe the Big Bang itself, only its consequences at increasing distance, and JWST's early-galaxy data has prompted ongoing refinement of formation timelines in the mainstream literature since 2022, not the abandonment of the underlying model.

**A caution, not a citation.** Some popular young-earth material also points to supposed dinosaur images in ancient art -- Peru's Ica stones and the Acámbaro figurines of Mexico chief among them -- as evidence humans and dinosaurs coexisted within recorded history. Both have been discredited: the carver behind a large share of the Ica stones admitted to producing them for profit in the twentieth century, and thermoluminescence dating placed the Acámbaro figurines' firing in the 1930s-40s, shortly before their supposed "discovery." Even Creation Ministries International's own creation.com publishes a piece warning fellow creationists away from this evidence by name ("Ica stones, bad arguments"). A similar claim -- fossilized human and dinosaur footprints found together in the Paluxy riverbed in Texas -- was formally retracted by creationist researchers themselves decades ago once closer study showed the "human" prints were eroded dinosaur tracks. None of this is cited here as evidence for anything; it's flagged because a study aiming at "objective observable truth" (the standard the whole subject is being held to) can't selectively apply that standard only to claims it's inclined to doubt.

## Two revelations, not one

Romans 1:19-23 makes a real, specific claim: every person, everywhere, has been shown enough of God through creation to be without excuse for unbelief. It does not claim, and this study has not tried to make it claim, that creation can show anyone the gospel. Paul says exactly what special revelation is for two chapters later: "faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ" (Romans 10:17, ESV) -- not through the night sky, however clearly it "declares the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1). General revelation's job in Scripture's own argument is to remove every excuse; only the preached word saves. Whatever a reader concludes about the age of the earth or the mechanics of the fossil record, that is the claim Romans 1:19-23 actually makes, and it stands or falls on the text, not on any of the illustrations gathered above.

## Discussion questions

1. Romans 1:21 names the root sin as failing to honor God and give thanks, with futile thinking and idolatry as the *result*, not the cause. Where in your own life has admiration for something God made stopped short of gratitude directed at him?
2. The ESV Study Bible's note on Romans 1:19-20 says general revelation condemns but does not save. Does that change how you'd use a "look at creation" argument in a conversation with someone who doesn't believe in God -- what can it actually accomplish, and what does it need to be followed by?
3. This study presented both AiG's and mainstream science's reading of the soft-tissue and JWST evidence rather than picking one. Does holding two competing explanations of the same data change how confident either side's stated conclusion should be?
4. Paul quotes pagan poets approvingly at the Areopagus (Acts 17:28) because their own tradition had preserved a fragment of what general revelation showed them. What "fragments" of truth about God do you notice preserved in sources -- art, philosophy, other worldviews -- that don't otherwise share your convictions?
