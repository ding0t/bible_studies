# The King on a Donkey: The Humble Beast from Abraham to the Triumphal Entry

Jesus sends two disciples into a village with an odd errand: "you will find a donkey tied there,
with her colt beside her. Untie them and bring them to Me" (Matthew 21:2, BSB). A King who could
have entered Jerusalem any way He chose sends for a donkey. To see why, follow the animal through
Scripture, from Abraham's household to the Mount of Olives.

**In one sentence:** In Scripture the donkey is the beast of house and road, Abraham's wealth, the
mount of judges' sons and of a woman hurrying to the prophet, an animal the law protected even for
an enemy and redeemed with a lamb; so when Zechariah's King comes "humble and riding on a donkey"
with the war horse cut off, He comes as the King of peace on the animal of everyday life, and Jesus
rode it into Jerusalem to give Himself as the Lamb who redeems you.

### Types & Prophecy

Zechariah 9:9 promised a King who comes "humble and riding on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a
donkey" (BSB), and Matthew 21:5 and John 12:15 quote it of Jesus' entry. Jacob's blessing on Judah
already pictured the King of Judah's line tying "his colt to the choicest branch" (Genesis 49:11,
BSB). Zechariah's next verse, a King who will "proclaim peace to the nations" with a dominion "to
the ends of the earth" (Zechariah 9:10, BSB), waits for His return. And the law gave a picture: the firstborn donkey, which could not go on the
altar, was redeemed with a lamb (Exodus 13:13), as you are redeemed by the Lamb (1 Peter 1:18-19).

### Lessons about Jesus

- **He chose His mount.** He sent for the animal and fulfilled the prophecy on purpose (Matthew
  21:2-5).
- **He is the King of peace.** He came on the beast of household and road, and the war horse is
  cut off (Zechariah 9:10).
- **He is gentle and humble.** Matthew calls the King on the donkey πραΰς (*praus*, G4239,
  "gentle"; Matthew 21:5), and Jesus calls Himself "gentle and humble in heart" with the same word
  (Matthew 11:29, BSB).
- **He rode to the cross.** He entered as King to give Himself as "a lamb without blemish or spot"
  (1 Peter 1:19, BSB).

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** God's King shows His greatness by coming low. He chose a working animal, and later a
  cross (Zechariah 9:9; Philippians 2:8).
- **Attitude.** Come to Him without fear. The King on the donkey is "gentle and humble in heart"
  (Matthew 11:29, BSB), and Zion is told, "Do not be afraid" (John 12:15, BSB).
- **Do.** The colt's owners asked one question, heard "The Lord needs it", and let it go (Luke
  19:33-34, BSB; Mark 11:6). This week, give one ordinary thing of yours, time, a car, a meal, for the Lord's
  work when someone asks.

## The words

Hebrew has three words for the animal: <span dir="rtl">חֲמוֹר</span> (*chamor*, H2543), the common
word for a donkey, in 93 verses; <span dir="rtl">אָתוֹן</span> (*aton*, H860), a she-donkey, in
28; and <span dir="rtl">עַיִר</span> (*ayir*, H5895), a young male, a colt, in 8. The mule,
<span dir="rtl">פֶּרֶד</span> (*pered*, H6505), is a different animal, half horse, and Scripture
keeps it apart.

The New Testament has four. ὄνος (*onos*, G3688), "donkey", is used five times, four of them at
the entry into Jerusalem (Matthew 21:2, 5, 7; John 12:15) and once by Jesus about the Sabbath
(Luke 13:15). πῶλος (*pōlos*, G4454), "colt", is used twelve times in eleven verses, all at the entry.
ὑποζύγιον (*hypozygion*, G5268), "beast of burden", is used twice: of the colt's mother (Matthew
21:5) and of Balaam's she-donkey (2 Peter 2:16). John calls the animal an ὀνάριον (*onarion*,
G3678), "a young donkey" (John 12:14).

## The beast of house and road

**She was wealth.** Abram "acquired sheep and cattle, male and female donkeys" (Genesis 12:16, BSB),
and Job's wealth is counted in she-donkeys, 500 before his trials and 1,000 after them (Job 1:3;
42:12). Jacob sent Esau "20 female donkeys, and 10 male donkeys" to make peace (Genesis 32:15, BSB;
32:16 in the Hebrew).

**She carried the load.** Joseph's brothers "loaded the grain on their donkeys" (Genesis 42:26,
BSB), and Joseph sent his father "ten donkeys loaded with the best of Egypt, and ten female donkeys
loaded with grain and bread ..." (Genesis 45:23, BSB). Abigail loaded her gifts for David on donkeys (1
Samuel 25:18).

**She went missing.** "The donkeys of Saul's father Kish wandered off", and the search brought Saul
to Samuel, who told him, "As for the donkeys you lost three days ago, do not worry about them"
(1 Samuel 9:3, 20, BSB). A powerful man's lost animals (1 Samuel 9:1) led to the anointing of Israel's first king.

**She was saddled for the hardest journey.** "Abraham got up early the next morning, saddled his
donkey" and set out for Moriah with Isaac, and at the foot of the mountain he said, "Stay here
with the donkey ... The boy and I will go over there to worship" (Genesis 22:3, 5, BSB). Numbers
uses the same words of Balaam: "in the morning Balaam got up, saddled his donkey" (Numbers 22:21,
BSB). Both verses have "in the morning" and <span dir="rtl">וַיַּחֲבֹשׁ</span> (*vayyachavosh*,
H2280, "saddled"), and each man has "his two servants", <span dir="rtl">שְׁנֵי נְעָרָיו</span>
(*shenei ne'arav*; Genesis 22:3; Numbers 22:22). The animals differ: Abraham's is a *chamor*,
Balaam's an *aton*. The rabbis heard the echo: "Let the saddling done by our father Avraham in order
to go and fulfill the will of the One at whose word the world came into existence counteract the
saddling done by Bilam in order to go and curse Israel" (*Genesis Rabbah* 55:8, Sefaria Community
Translation). Numbers does not draw the line itself, so hold it as an echo: one man rode out to
give his son to God, the other to sell a curse.

This shows that God works in ordinary life. The animal of the farm and the road carried His
purposes, from a lost herd in Benjamin to the mountain where God provided a ram in Isaac's place (Genesis
22:13).

## The mount of peace and standing

**She carried people of standing.** Deborah's song calls on the leaders as "You who ride white
donkeys" (Judges 5:10, BSB), and in Hebrew they are she-donkeys. The judge Jair "had thirty sons
who rode on thirty donkeys. And they had thirty towns" (Judges 10:4, BSB), and Hebrew uses one
word, <span dir="rtl">עֲיָרִים</span> (*ayarim*, H5895), for the donkeys and the towns, a pun on
*ir* (H5892, "town"). Abdon's
"forty sons and thirty grandsons ... rode on seventy donkeys" (Judges 12:14, BSB). Abigail came
"riding her donkey" to meet David (1 Samuel 25:20, BSB). The Shunammite woman "saddled the donkey",
a she-donkey, to reach Elisha (2 Kings 4:24, BSB). Balaam rode his "all your life" (Numbers 22:30,
BSB), with his two servants (Numbers 22:22): a man of rank on the mount of one.

**The king's household rode them.** When David fled Absalom, Ziba met him with "a pair of saddled
donkeys", and said, "The donkeys are for the king's household to ride" (2 Samuel 16:1-2, BSB).
David had gone up the Mount of Olives "weeping as he went up" (2 Samuel 15:30, BSB).

**The mule is kept apart.** At Solomon's coronation David says, "Set my son Solomon on my own mule"
(1 Kings 1:33, BSB): a royal mount, and a different animal. Zechariah's King comes on a donkey.

**The horse is war's animal.** The king of Israel "must not acquire many horses for himself"
(Deuteronomy 17:16, BSB). "Some trust in chariots and others in horses, but we trust in the name of
the LORD our God" (Psalm 20:7, BSB). Isaiah pronounces woe on "those who go down to Egypt for help,
who rely on horses" (Isaiah 31:1, BSB). Zechariah sets the two side by side: the King comes on a
donkey, and God says, "I will cut off the chariot from Ephraim and the horse from Jerusalem, and
the bow of war will be broken" (Zechariah 9:10, BSB).

This shows that God's King rules without trusting in force. The donkey was honourable, the mount of
judges, of households and of a king's household in flight, and it was the animal of peace.

## The law's care

**Even an enemy's donkey.** "If you encounter your enemy's stray ox or donkey, you must return it to
him. If you see the donkey of one who hates you fallen under its load, do not leave it there; you
must help him with it" (Exodus 23:4-5, BSB). The same for a brother's (Deuteronomy 22:4). Israel was
not to yoke an ox and a donkey together (Deuteronomy 22:10), or covet a neighbour's donkey (Exodus
20:17). Moses and Samuel each defend their whole leadership with it: "I have not taken one donkey
from them" (Numbers 16:15, BSB); "Whose ox or donkey have I taken?" (1 Samuel 12:3, BSB).

**Sabbath rest.** "On the seventh day you must cease, so that your ox and your donkey may rest"
(Exodus 23:12, BSB; Deuteronomy 5:14). Jesus argued from it when He healed on the Sabbath: "Does not
each of you on the Sabbath untie his ox or donkey from the stall and lead it to water? Then should
not this daughter of Abraham, whom Satan has kept bound for eighteen long years, be released from
her bondage on the Sabbath day?" (Luke 13:15-16, BSB). If you untie a donkey on the Sabbath, God will untie a daughter of Abraham.

**Redeemed with a lamb.** The donkey was not an animal for the altar (Numbers 18:15, 17), and its
firstborn belonged to God all the same: "You must redeem every firstborn donkey with a lamb, and if
you do not redeem it, you are to break its neck. And every firstborn of your sons you must redeem"
(Exodus 13:13, BSB; Exodus 34:20). The firstborn donkey lived because a lamb was given in its place.
The law does not say the lamb died; in Jewish practice it went to the priest alive, and "he can
redeem with the same lamb many times" (*m. Bekhorot* 1:4, Kulp translation).

This shows that God cares for what serves His people and that He redeems what He claims. The donkey
redeemed by a lamb is the gospel's shape, and the gospel goes further: "you were redeemed ... with
the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Peter 1:18-19, BSB). Your Lamb was
given for you, and He died.

## Donkeys that teach

**Balaam's she-donkey.** She saw the angel of the LORD three times before the prophet did, and the
LORD opened her mouth. Peter calls her "a donkey, otherwise without speech, that spoke with a man's
voice and restrained the prophet's madness" (2 Peter 2:16, BSB). Her story is told in [The Donkey
and Her Prophet](../biblical-figures/the-donkey-and-her-prophet.md).

**The donkey that knows its manger.** God's complaint against Israel opens with her: "The ox knows
its owner, and the donkey its master's manger, but Israel does not know; My people do not
understand" (Isaiah 1:3, BSB). A donkey knows who feeds it.

**The bridle.** "A whip for the horse, a bridle for the donkey, and a rod for the backs of fools!"
(Proverbs 26:3, BSB). Balaam beat his own donkey, faithful to him for all her years.

**A donkey beside a lion.** When the man of God from Judah disobeyed the LORD's word, "a lion met him
on the road and killed him", and his body lay "with the donkey and the lion standing beside it ...
The lion had not eaten the body or mauled the donkey" (1 Kings 13:24, 28, BSB). The judgment was
exactly God's, and the donkey stood by as its witness.

**Samson's jawbone.** He "found the fresh jawbone of a donkey ... and struck down a thousand men"
(Judges 15:15, BSB). God used the plainest thing to hand.

This shows that God teaches through humble things. A dumb animal rebuked a prophet, and a donkey's
loyalty shamed a people who did not know their Lord.

## The King's mount

### Judah's colt

Jacob's blessing on Judah gives the tribe the scepter, "until Shiloh comes and the allegiance of the
nations is his", and then: "He ties his donkey to the vine, his colt to the choicest branch" (Genesis
49:10-11, BSB). Who Shiloh is, is debated (the BSB's footnote gives "Or until the one to whom tribute
belongs"), but the line promises the nations' allegiance to Judah's ruler, and in his reign the vine
is so plentiful that he ties his donkey to it. The Hebrew is <span dir="rtl">עִירֹה</span> (*iroh*,
H5895, "his colt") and <span dir="rtl">בְּנִי אֲתֹנוֹ</span> (*beni atono*, H860, "the son of his
she-donkey"). The Greek Old Testament renders the second "the colt of his donkey", τὸν πῶλον τῆς
ὄνου, the pair of words the Gospels use at the entry (Matthew 21:2; John 12:15): an echo, since
neither Gospel quotes Genesis.

### Zechariah's King

Zechariah uses the same pair: "on a colt, the foal of a donkey", <span dir="rtl">עַיִר בֶּן־אֲתֹנוֹת</span>
(*ayir ben atonot*, "a colt, the son of she-donkeys"). *Ayir* and *aton* stand together in three
verses of the Old Testament: Jacob's gift to Esau (Genesis 32:15), Judah's blessing (Genesis
49:11), and Zechariah 9:9. The King of Judah's blessing rides into Zechariah's prophecy on Judah's colt.

The King is <span dir="rtl">עָנִי</span> (*ani*, H6041), "humble", a word that also means "poor"
or "afflicted"; the Greek Old Testament and Matthew render it πραΰς (*praus*), "gentle". He is
"righteous and victorious" (Zechariah 9:9, BSB), and the BSB's footnote gives "Or righteous and
endowed with salvation". The line "on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey" is Hebrew
parallelism, one animal described twice, and the "and" joins the two descriptions.

Read it in its own setting first. Zechariah prophesied to the Jews back from exile, from the second
year of Darius (Zechariah 1:1), and chapter 9 is an oracle, "the burden of the word of the LORD"
(Zechariah 9:1, BSB). It moves through the lands around Israel in judgment, then God promises, "I
will camp around My house ... never again will an oppressor overrun My people" (Zechariah 9:8,
BSB). Only then does the King come, and after Him God frees the prisoners "because of the blood of
My covenant" (Zechariah 9:11, BSB). The King on the donkey comes to a people God has just defended,
as their King of peace.

### The entry, four times

All four Gospels tell it (Matthew 21:1-11; Mark 11:1-10; Luke 19:29-40; John 12:12-16).

- **Matthew** has the disciples find "a donkey tied there, with her colt beside her" (Matthew 21:2,
  BSB); the Greek donkey, ὄνος, is feminine there too. He quotes Zechariah, opening with words from
  Isaiah, "Say to the Daughter of Zion" (Matthew 21:5, BSB; Isaiah 62:11).
- **Mark** and **Luke** name the colt only, one "on which no one has ever sat" (Mark 11:2; Luke
  19:30, BSB). The law asked the same of animals set apart for God: a heifer "that has never been
  yoked or used for work" (Deuteronomy 21:3, BSB; Numbers 19:2; 1 Samuel 6:7). That fits the colt,
  though the Gospels do not draw the comparison.
- **Luke** adds that the crowd sang "Blessed is the King who comes in the name of the Lord!" (Luke
  19:38, BSB), from Psalm 118:26, and that as He came near, "He wept over" the city (Luke 19:41,
  BSB). David had climbed the same Mount of Olives weeping as he fled the city (2 Samuel 15:30);
  Jesus came down it weeping over the city He entered. That is an echo in reverse, and Luke does
  not point to it. [Prophecy: Events and Times](../last-things/prophecy-events-times.md#the-flagship-calculation-daniels-seventy-weeks-to-the-triumphal-entry)
  counts Daniel's seventy weeks to this day.
- **John** says Jesus found "a young donkey" and sat on it, quoting "Do not be afraid, O Daughter of
  Zion" (John 12:14-15, BSB), and admits the disciples "did not understand these things" until
  "after Jesus was glorified" (John 12:16, BSB).

The owners asked why the colt was being untied, and the answer was, "The Lord needs it" (Luke 19:34,
BSB), and in Mark, "will return it shortly" (Mark 11:3, BSB). The Lord of the prophecy borrowed
what He fulfilled it with.

Matthew closes the scene with the city's question: "When Jesus had entered Jerusalem, the whole city
was stirred and asked, 'Who is this?'" and the crowds answered, "This is Jesus, the prophet from
Nazareth in Galilee" (Matthew 21:10-11, BSB). The crowds knew the prophet. Matthew has already told
the reader He is the King of Zechariah 9.

### One animal or two?

Matthew alone has two animals, and he writes, "They brought the donkey and the colt and laid their
cloaks on them, and Jesus sat on them" (Matthew 21:7, BSB). Some readers charge that Matthew
misread Zechariah's parallelism as two animals and pictured Jesus riding both at once. What "them"
means is contested. The Greek αὐτῶν (*autōn*) has the same form for the animals and for the cloaks,
τὰ ἱμάτια (*ta himatia*, G2440). The *ESV Study Bible* reads it as the cloaks, the nearest plural
(note on Matthew 21:6-7). The earlier "on them" in the same verse means the animals, and the
*NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible* reads the cloaks as laid on both, with Jesus "probably" sitting
only on the colt (note on Matthew 21:7). Either way, Matthew records two animals because two were
brought, the mother walking beside an unbroken colt, and his quotation keeps Zechariah's single
line intact: "on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey" (Matthew 21:5). Mark, Luke and John
settle which one carried Him: "He sat on it", the colt (Mark 11:7, BSB; Luke 19:35; John 12:14).

Zechariah's first line came true on the Mount of Olives. The second waits: the chariot cut off, peace
proclaimed to the nations, a dominion "to the ends of the earth" (Zechariah 9:10, BSB). This site
reads that as His return to reign, when His "feet will stand on the Mount of Olives" (Zechariah
14:4, BSB; Acts 1:11-12). Then He comes on a different animal: "there before me was a white horse.
And its rider is called Faithful and True. With righteousness He judges and wages war" (Revelation
19:11, BSB). He came first on a donkey, to make peace "through the blood of His cross" (Colossians 1:20, BSB). He comes again on a horse, to
judge. The horse Zechariah cut off is Ephraim's and Jerusalem's own, the war horse Israel trusted
(Zechariah 9:10; Psalm 20:7); the white horse is the King's, and He rides it to bring that war to an
end.

This shows that God keeps His promises down to the animal. The King came exactly as Zechariah said,
humble, to die, and the Lamb who rode the donkey will reign.

## What the text does not say

The ox and donkey of Christmas scenes are not in Luke. Luke says three times that the baby lay "in
a manger" (Luke 2:7, 12, 16, BSB) and names no animal. Matthew's flight to Egypt names no donkey
either (Matthew 2:13-15), though Moses had once taken his family to Egypt on one (Exodus 4:20).

Luke's word for the manger is φάτνη (*phatnē*, G5336). He uses it once more, when Jesus speaks of
untying a donkey "from the stall" on the Sabbath (Luke 13:15, BSB), and the Greek Old Testament
uses it in Isaiah 1:3: "the donkey its master's manger". Luke does not quote Isaiah, so this is an
echo the reader may hear rather than a claim the text makes. If you hear it, it says something
true: the donkey knows its master's manger, and the shepherds came to the manger and knew their
Lord (Luke 2:15-20), while Jerusalem "did not recognize the time of your visitation from God" (Luke
19:44, BSB).

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** Zechariah's King is <span dir="rtl">עָנִי</span> (*ani*), "humble, poor,
   afflicted", and Matthew renders it πραΰς (*praus*), "gentle", the word Jesus uses of Himself in
   Matthew 11:29. What does each rendering show you about the King?
2. **The text in its context.** Zechariah 9:9 is followed by 9:10, where the chariot and the war
   horse are cut off. How does verse 10 explain the donkey in verse 9?
3. **Christ.** The firstborn donkey was redeemed with a lamb (Exodus 13:13). The King on the donkey
   rode into Jerusalem at Passover (John 12:1, 12). What does that put together for you?
4. **The hard part.** Matthew 21:7 says Jesus "sat on them". What could "them" mean, how do the
   other three Gospels help, and what does the charge that Matthew misread Zechariah miss?
5. **Be transformed.** The owners gave up their colt when they heard "The Lord needs it" (Luke
   19:34). What of yours is the Lord asking for, and what makes it hard to untie?
