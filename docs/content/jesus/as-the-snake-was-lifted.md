# As the Snake Was Lifted Up: The Bronze Serpent and the Son of Man

Israel is dying of snakebite, and God's remedy is a snake. Not an antidote and not a sacrifice — a
bronze copy of the thing killing them, raised on a pole, healing anyone who looks at it. Centuries
later, in a night conversation with a Pharisee, Jesus points back at that pole and says it was
about him.

### Types & Prophecy

**Type.** The bronze serpent is a **τύπος** of Christ crucified, and Jesus is the one who identifies
it (John 3:14) — a type the reader is not left to spot. Point for point: the object is made in the
likeness of the thing killing the people, yet carries none of its venom; it is raised on a pole
where the whole camp can see it; it heals by being looked at, with no offering, payment, or ritual
attached; and it is God's provision given after confession and intercession, not a cure Israel
devised. Jesus applies each of those to his own death.

**Prophecy.** Genesis 3:15 states in words what the pole shows in pattern: the woman's offspring
will take a wound to the heel and crush the serpent's head. The type argues from resemblance; the
prophecy argues from a stated prediction coming true. Both land on the same cross.

### Lessons about Jesus

- Jesus read his own death out of Numbers 21 before it happened, and read it as something that
  *had* to happen — **δεῖ**, "it is necessary" (John 3:14).
- He took the likeness of what stood under judgment without taking its guilt: made "to be sin for
  us" (2 Corinthians 5:21, ESV), sent "in the likeness of sinful flesh" (Romans 8:3, ESV), "yet
  without sin" (Hebrews 4:15, ESV).
- In John's vocabulary his crucifixion *is* his exaltation. The verb for being raised on the cross
  is the verb the Septuagint uses for the Servant's glory (Isaiah 52:13).
- What he asks of the dying is a look. "Whoever believes in him" (John 3:15, ESV) is the same shape
  of response as "everyone who is bitten, when he sees it, shall live" (Numbers 21:8, ESV).

### Memory verses

- **John 3:14-15 (ESV)** — "And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of
  Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life."
- **Numbers 21:8 (ESV)** — "Make a fiery serpent and set it on a pole, and everyone who is bitten,
  when he sees it, shall live."

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** Test what you have quietly added to "look and live." The Israelite bitten in the camp
  had nothing to bring and nothing to put right first.
- **Attitude.** Hold God-given things loosely enough that they stay means rather than objects. The
  bronze serpent was a real gift of God for centuries before Hezekiah had to smash it (2 Kings
  18:4).
- **Do.** Name one thing you are treating as a condition of God accepting you, and take it to him as
  something already settled at the cross.

### Prayer

Father, you did not deal with our dying from a distance. You sent your Son into the likeness of the
thing that is killing us, and lifted him up where everyone could see. You are the God who provides
the remedy and then asks only that we look at it. Teach us to stop bringing you what you never asked
for. Turn our eyes to your Son, and let us live.

## The two passages

> ✝️ Numbers 21:4-9 (ESV)
>
> 4 From Mount Hor they set out by the way to the Red Sea, to go around the land of Edom. And the people became impatient on the way. 5 And the people spoke against God and against Moses, "Why have you brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? For there is no food and no water, and we loathe this worthless food." 6 Then the LORD sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people, so that many people of Israel died. 7 And the people came to Moses and said, "We have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD and against you. Pray to the LORD, that he take away the serpents from us." So Moses prayed for the people. 8 And the LORD said to Moses, "Make a fiery serpent and set it on a pole, and everyone who is bitten, when he sees it, shall live." 9 So Moses made a bronze serpent and set it on a pole. And if a serpent bit anyone, he would look at the bronze serpent and live.

> ✝️ John 3:14-16 (ESV)
>
> 14 And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, 15 that whoever believes in him may have eternal life. 16 For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life.

Jesus makes the connection himself (John 3:14), so the link between the two passages is given
rather than proposed. What remains is each passage on its own terms, and then the exact shape of the
application.

## Genre and setting

**Numbers.** Old Testament narrative inside a covenant history. It reports what happened and lets
placement and outcome carry the verdict, without stopping to tell the reader what to conclude — so
where an episode sits in the book is part of what it means. The events of 21:4-9 fall near the end
of the forty years, shortly after Aaron's death (Numbers 20:22-29) and Edom's refusal of passage,
which forced the detour "by the way to the Red Sea" (21:4). Authorship is traditionally Mosaic. The
book's arc runs from a first generation that dies in the wilderness to a second standing at the edge
of Canaan, and it is the second generation the finished book addresses.

**John.** A Gospel, written late in the first century, with its purpose stated outright: "these are
written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may
have life in his name" (John 20:31, WEB). Two Johannine habits are running in chapter 3: a saying
misunderstood by its hearer and then clarified, and a pointer at the cross long before the cross is
narrated.

**The complaint and the judgment.** The grievance is specific — "there is no food and no water, and
we loathe this worthless food" (21:5) — and it is aimed at God's own provision, the manna. The
serpents of 21:6 answer rebellion against that provision with an immediate, visible consequence.

This is the last of the wilderness generation's murmuring episodes, and Numbers 11:1-3 is its
closest match: the people complain, fire falls, they cry to Moses, Moses prays, the fire abates.
Numbers 16 runs the same course, ending with Aaron's censer between the dead and the living
(16:46-48). The form is not universal. Exodus 16's grumbling draws provision with no judgment at
all (16:2-5), and Numbers 14's confession comes after sentence has been passed and is answered with
defeat at Hormah (14:39-45).

**Serpent imagery in the ancient world.** Serpent symbolism was religiously loaded across the
ancient Near East, and Egypt's uraeus — the rearing cobra on Pharaoh's crown, signifying protection
and royal authority — is the example Israel had spent generations looking at. God's instruction
inverts it. The image to be made is of the creature currently killing them, and the object holds no
power of its own: healing comes from looking at what God said to look at.

**What became of the object.** Centuries on, Israel still had the bronze serpent and was burning
incense to it. Hezekiah "broke in pieces the bronze serpent that Moses had made... and he called it
Nehushtan" (2 Kings 18:4, WEB), destroying it alongside the high places, the pillars, and the
Asherah. The Hebrew names it with the same construct phrase as Numbers 21:9 — <span dir="rtl">נְחַשׁ
הַנְּחֹשֶׁת</span> — and then reduces it to <span dir="rtl">נְחֻשְׁתָּן</span>, *nechushtan*, "the
bronze thing" (H5180). An object God himself commanded, kept in service past its purpose, ended on
the same list as an Asherah pole.

## Literary context

**Numbers 21:4-9** is the final murmuring-and-judgment unit of the wilderness period, sitting where
the book turns from wandering to conquest: itinerary notices follow (21:10-20), then the defeats of
Sihon and Og (21:21-35). Inside the unit, the point is that trusting God's provided remedy turns
judgment into life — for the generation whose entire history has been unbelief.

**John 3:14-15** sits inside Jesus's night conversation with Nicodemus (John 3:1-21), after Jesus
asks "the teacher of Israel" why he does not understand these things (3:10), implying the Hebrew
Scriptures Nicodemus taught should already have prepared him. Three verses intervene before the
serpent, and the last of them sets the title: no one has ascended into heaven except the Son of Man
who came down from it (3:13). That same Son of Man is the one who "must be lifted up" in 3:14.

**John 3:16 continues 3:14-15.** It opens **Οὕτως γάρ**, "for thus" — a connective pointing back at
what was just said — and it reuses the frame of 3:15: **ἵνα πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων... ἔχῃ ζωὴν αἰώνιον**,
"that everyone believing... may have eternal life." The two are not identical. John 3:15 has
**πιστεύων ἐν αὐτῷ**, the Gospel's only instance of *pisteuō* with **ἐν**; 3:16 has John's usual
**πιστεύων εἰς αὐτόν** and inserts **μὴ ἀπόληται ἀλλά**, "should not perish but." What 3:16 supplies
is the motive and the cost behind the lifting up: God loved the world, and gave his Son. The most
quoted verse in the Bible is the explanation of the bronze serpent.

**A textual note on 3:15.** Translations diverge here because the manuscripts do. The ASV reads
"that whosoever believeth may in him have eternal life" (John 3:15, ASV); the WEB reads "that
whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life" (John 3:15, WEB), carrying a
longer reading assimilated to 3:16 in the later Greek text. The ESV quoted above follows the shorter
one. Nothing in the typology turns on the difference.

**"Lifted up" is John's word for the cross.** The verb returns at 8:28 — "When you have lifted up
the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he" (John 8:28, WEB) — and at 12:32: "And I, if I am
lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself" (John 12:32, WEB), where John adds his own
gloss for the reader, "But he said this, signifying by what kind of death he should die" (12:33,
WEB). Every one of these is spoken before the crucifixion happens; the Nicodemus conversation is
chapter 3 of 21. The word predicts the cross without naming it.

## Word studies

**Nachash / nechoshet.** <span dir="rtl">נָחָשׁ</span> / <span dir="rtl">נְחֹשֶׁת</span> ("serpent" /
"bronze," H5175 / H5178). Numbers 21:9 says Moses made a <span dir="rtl">נְחַשׁ נְחֹשֶׁת</span>,
*nechash nechoshet* — the noun for the creature in construct with the noun for the metal, "a serpent
of bronze." The sound-play is in the Hebrew, not an artifact of translation. What Moses is told to
make is an image of the exact thing killing the people.

**Saraph.** <span dir="rtl">שָׂרָף</span> ("burning one," H8314) occurs seven times in the Hebrew
Bible. Five describe serpents — Numbers 21:6 and 21:8, Deuteronomy 8:15, Isaiah 14:29 and 30:6 — and
two describe the six-winged beings attending God's throne in Isaiah's temple vision (Isaiah 6:2, 6).
The shared sense is "burning," most likely the burning of the bite in the case of the wilderness
serpents. Two things sharing a root are not thereby the same kind of being, and the text makes no
such identification.

**Nes.** <span dir="rtl">נֵס</span> ("standard, banner, signal pole," H5251) is what the serpent is
set on in Numbers 21:8 and 21:9. It is also what Isaiah's messianic figure becomes: "the root of
Jesse, who stands as a banner of the peoples," whom "the nations will seek" (Isaiah 11:10, WEB). One
word covers the pole raised for Israel's healing and the ensign the nations come to. Isaiah 11 is
not citing the Numbers scene; the vocabulary simply runs through both.

**ὑψόω** (*hypsoō*, "to lift up, exalt," G5312) — the load-bearing term. John uses it twice in one
sentence at 3:14: Moses **ὕψωσεν** the serpent, and the Son of Man must **ὑψωθῆναι**. John's own
gloss at 12:33 fixes what he means by it — the manner of Jesus's death.

Where else the verb appears is what gives the choice its weight. The Septuagint opens Isaiah's fourth
Servant Song with it: **Ἰδοὺ συνήσει ὁ παῖς μου, καὶ ὑψωθήσεται καὶ δοξασθήσεται σφόδρα** (Isaiah
52:13, Brenton LXX) — rendered literally, "Behold, my servant will understand, and he will be lifted
up and glorified exceedingly." Behind the Greek the Hebrew stacks three verbs of exaltation,
<span dir="rtl">יָרוּם</span> *yarum*, <span dir="rtl">וְנִשָּׂא</span> *venissa*, and
<span dir="rtl">וְגָבַהּ</span> *vegavah* (H7311 / H5375 / H1361): "he will be high, and lifted up,
and greatly exalted."

John's vocabulary then splits along a clean line. Every saying about Jesus's death spoken *before* it
happens uses *hypsoō*: twice at 3:14, again at 8:28, and twice more at 12:32 and 12:34. Once the
narrative reaches the event, John writes **σταυρόω** (*stauroō*, "crucify," G4717) eleven times in
chapter 19, and never uses *hypsoō* again. The veiled word carries the prediction; the plain word
reports the execution. And the veiled word is Isaiah's word for the Servant's glory — which is why,
in John, the cross is the humiliation and the enthronement at once.

**ὄφις** (*ophis*, "serpent," G3789). The word John uses of the bronze serpent at 3:14
(**τὸν ὄφιν**) is the word Revelation uses of Satan: **ὁ ὄφις ὁ ἀρχαῖος**, "the old serpent, he who
is called the devil and Satan" (Revelation 12:9, WEB). The chain runs the length of Scripture:

- **Genesis 3:14-15** — the serpent cursed, and the woman's offspring promised to crush its head
- **Numbers 21** — a bronze image of that same creature, lifted up as the means of healing
- **John 3:14** — that image applied to Christ
- **Revelation 12:9** — the deceiver named as "the old serpent"

Christ is lifted up in the form associated with the curse and the deceiver, in order to break the
curse and defeat the deceiver.

## What carries forward, and what doesn't

The literal particulars stay in the wilderness. A specific bronze object on a specific pole, made
once, for one generation bitten by actual snakes at a locatable point on the road around Edom: it
was never a repeatable rite, and Israel's attempt to keep it in service ended under Hezekiah's
hammer.

What carries forward is the pattern Jesus himself carries forward — a remedy God provides, shaped
like the thing under judgment, free of the fault that brought the judgment, lifted up in public, and
received by looking. Jesus applies each of those to his own crucifixion in John 3:14-15, which is
the warrant for reading them as more than a single episode in Israel's history.

## Theological principle

God's remedy for sin and death takes the form of the very thing under judgment, without sharing the
fault that brought the judgment, and is received by simple trust rather than by further ritual or
merit. The bronze serpent had no venom of its own; it was shaped like the thing killing Israel, and
anyone who looked at it lived.

Paul states the same principle in his own vocabulary, with no reference to the serpent image: God
made Christ, "who knew no sin, to be sin for us" (2 Corinthians 5:21, ESV), and sent his Son "in the
likeness of sinful flesh" to "condemn sin in the flesh" (Romans 8:3, ESV). Christ bears the likeness
of what is under judgment, "yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15, ESV). The principle is taught explicitly
elsewhere in Scripture, and does not rest on the serpent typology alone.

## Discussion questions

1. Does it change how you read John 3:16 to see it as the direct continuation of the bronze-serpent
   comparison in 3:14-15, rather than a stand-alone verse?
2. The Israelites weren't asked to do anything except look at the bronze serpent. Is there a
   temptation, in your own faith, to add something to "look and live" that Scripture doesn't
   actually require?
3. Israel eventually worshiped the very object God gave them for healing. Are there good things in
   your own life — even God-given ones — at risk of quietly becoming the object of your trust
   instead of the God who gave them?
4. How does it strike you that Christ is described as being made "in the likeness of sinful flesh"
   and "to be sin" for us, taking the very form of what he came to defeat?
