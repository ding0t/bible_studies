# "You Are Gods": Psalm 82, John 10 and the Authority God Lends

It is winter in Jerusalem, at the Feast of Dedication, and men are lifting stones. They have just
heard Jesus say, "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30, ESV), and they have understood Him exactly:
"you, being a man, make yourself God" (John 10:33, ESV). Jesus answers with one line from a psalm in
which God calls a room full of judges "gods" and then sentences every one of them to die.

**In one sentence:** God calls those He sets in authority אֱלֹהִים because the authority is His, and
He judges them by what they do with it; Jesus quoted that psalm to the authorities who were judging
Him, and showed by the Father's own works that the authority they borrowed is His by right.

## Key Takeaways

### Lessons about Jesus

- **He claimed what no lent title covers.** The judges of Psalm 82 were called gods for an office.
  Jesus said He was the one "whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world" (John 10:36, ESV),
  and that "the Father is in me and I am in the Father" (John 10:38, ESV). The men holding the stones
  heard a claim to be God, and they were right.
- **He pointed to His works as the proof.** "The works that I do in my Father's name bear witness
  about me" (John 10:25, ESV). The judges' works condemned them; His works testified for Him.
- **He holds the judging the psalm prayed for.** Psalm 82 ends, "Arise, O God, judge the earth"
  (Psalm 82:8, ESV), and Jesus said "the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son"
  (John 5:22, ESV). John does not join the two verses; reading them together is this study's
  suggestion.

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** Every authority you hold, at home, at work or in a church, is lent by God, and He will
  ask what you did with it for the weak (Psalm 82:3-4).
- **Attitude.** Honour Jesus as you honour the Father (John 5:23). His works settle who He is.
- **Do.** Name one person under your care who is weak or overlooked, and act for them this week.

## The charge at the Feast of Dedication

> ✝️ John 10:31-39 (ESV)
>
> 31 The Jews picked up stones again to stone him. 32 Jesus answered them, "I have shown you many
> good works from the Father; for which of them are you going to stone me?" 33 The Jews answered him,
> "It is not for a good work that we are going to stone you but for blasphemy, because you, being a
> man, make yourself God." 34 Jesus answered them, "Is it not written in your Law, 'I said, you are
> gods'? 35 If he called them gods to whom the word of God came—and Scripture cannot be broken— 36 do
> you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, 'You are blaspheming,' because
> I said, 'I am the Son of God'? 37 If I am not doing the works of my Father, then do not believe me;
> 38 but if I do them, even though you do not believe me, believe the works, that you may know and
> understand that the Father is in me and I am in the Father." 39 Again they sought to arrest him,
> but he escaped from their hands.

### A feast about a man who made himself god

"At that time the Feast of Dedication took place at Jerusalem. It was winter" (John 10:22, ESV). The
feast, Hanukkah, remembered the rededication of the temple in December 164 BC, after the Seleucid
king Antiochus IV Epiphanes had desecrated it (1 Maccabees 1:59; *ESV Study Bible* note on John
10:22). The *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* notes that the festival "partly commemorated
liberation from Antiochus IV Epiphanes, a man who claimed to be divine" (note on John 10:31). The
Greek of 2 Maccabees records his confession as he died: "it is right to be subject to God, and that
a mortal should not arrogantly think himself equal to God" (2 Maccabees 9:12, my translation of Brenton's
Septuagint text, *mē thnēton onta isothea phronein*).

So the charge in John 10:33 lands in a week when Jerusalem was remembering exactly that sin. A man
making himself God was the crime they celebrated God for punishing.

### Who was accusing Him

John calls the accusers "the Jews" (John 10:24, 31, 33). In this Gospel the phrase often means the
Jerusalem authorities, and John 9:22 shows these ones at work: "the Jews had already agreed that if
anyone should confess Jesus to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue" (ESV). The Pharisees
had questioned the man born blind and "cast him out" (John 9:34, ESV), and some of them were standing
by when Jesus answered (John 9:40). Jesus then spoke of thieves and robbers who climb into the
sheepfold (John 10:1), and at Dedication He told the crowd around Him, "you are not among my sheep"
(John 10:26, ESV). The festival is a new scene, and the argument has the same opponents in
substance: men with authority over God's people, using it against Him.

## One word, many holders

### What אֱלֹהִים covers

<span dir="rtl">אֱלֹהִים</span> (*ʾĕlōhîm*, el-oh-HEEM, H430) occurs 2,601 times in the Hebrew Bible.
Most of the time it means God. The *NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible* names judges, Moses, angels
and the gods of the nations among its other holders (note on Psalm 82:1), and 1 Samuel 28:13 adds a
spirit from the dead:

| Who | Text | What the word does there |
|---|---|---|
| The judges of Israel | Exodus 22:8-9, 28 | Cases "shall come before God" and "the one whom God condemns" pays double (ESV). The LSB footnotes Exodus 21:6, "Or the judges who acted in God's name." "You shall not revile God, nor curse a ruler of your people" (Exodus 22:28, ESV) pairs *ʾĕlōhîm* with a ruler, and the CSB footnotes it, "Or judges." |
| Moses | Exodus 4:16; 7:1 | "You shall be as God to him"; "I have made you like God to Pharaoh" (ESV). |
| Heavenly beings | Psalm 8:5 | "A little lower than the heavenly beings" (ESV), where the Hebrew has *ʾĕlōhîm* and the Septuagint "angels" (CSB footnote). Hebrews 2:7 quotes the Septuagint: "lower than the angels" (ESV). |
| A spirit from the dead | 1 Samuel 28:13 | "I see a god coming up out of the earth" (ESV). |
| The gods of the nations | Exodus 12:12; Psalm 96:5 | "On all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgments" (ESV). |

The word names a rank: one who stands above others and acts with authority that comes from beyond
them. Moses was "as God" to Pharaoh because he carried God's word with God's power behind it. The
judges were *ʾĕlōhîm* because the verdict they gave was God's verdict. **This shows that God shares
His work with those He appoints, and puts His own name on the office while they hold it.**

### How the grammar marks the difference

*ʾĕlōhîm* is plural in form. When it means the one God, the verb that goes with it is normally
singular: "God created" in Genesis 1:1 is <span dir="rtl">בָּרָא</span> (*bara*, H1254), third masculine
singular. When it means a group, the verb is plural. Exodus 22:9 is the clearest case: "the one whom
*ʾĕlōhîm* condemns" uses <span dir="rtl">יַרְשִׁיעֻן</span> (*yarshiʿun*, H7561), a plural verb, which is
why the NLT footnotes Exodus 22:8, "Or before the judges."

Psalm 82:1 puts both uses in one line:

> ✝️ Psalm 82:1 (ESV)
>
> 1 A Psalm of Asaph. God has taken his place in the divine council; in the midst of the gods he
> holds judgment:

The first *ʾĕlōhîm* takes a singular participle, <span dir="rtl">נִצָּב</span> (*nitsav*, H5324, "has
taken his place"), and a singular verb, <span dir="rtl">יִשְׁפֹּט</span> (*yishpot*, H8199, "he judges"). The second is the crowd
He stands among. The assembly itself is <span dir="rtl">עֲדַת אֵל</span> (*ʿadat ʾEl*, "the
assembly of God"), with the singular <span dir="rtl">אֵל</span> (*ʾEl*, H410). The LSB footnotes
it, "Heb El; the one true God."

**The rule is general, and it has exceptions.** In Genesis a plural verb sometimes goes with
*ʾĕlōhîm* where the subject is God: "when God caused me to wander" (Genesis 20:13, ESV) and "there God had
revealed himself to him" (Genesis 35:7, ESV), with <span dir="rtl">הִתְעוּ</span> (*hitʿu*, H8582) and
<span dir="rtl">נִגְלוּ</span> (*niglu*, H1540), both plural. The context decides; the grammar usually
agrees with it.

### The names never given away

Two names are never lent as titles. The judges are called "gods" and "sons of the Most High" (Psalm
82:6), and the Most High, <span dir="rtl">עֶלְיוֹן</span> (*ʿElyon*, el-YOHN, H5945), is the one who
calls them that. The adjective *ʿelyon* can describe people, as when David's heir is made "the highest
of the kings of the earth" (Psalm 89:27, ESV), but as a name it belongs to God alone. The covenant name, <span dir="rtl">יְהוָה</span> (YHWH, H3068), rendered "the LORD", is
God's own: "This is my name forever" (Exodus 3:15, ESV). "I am the LORD; that is my name; my glory I
give to no other" (Isaiah 42:8, ESV). Deuteronomy 10:17 says it in one phrase: "the LORD your God is
God of gods and Lord of lords" (ESV). **So God lends the title for the work, and keeps His name and
His glory to Himself.** That distinction is the hinge of John 10.

## Gods over the nations

Scripture also uses this vocabulary for heavenly powers set over the peoples of the earth. Psalm 82
points that way itself: it ends with God inheriting "all the nations" (Psalm 82:8, ESV).

### The nations divided

> ✝️ Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (ESV)
>
> 8 When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance, when he divided mankind, he fixed the
> borders of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God. 9 But the LORD's portion is his
> people, Jacob his allotted heritage.

The Masoretic Text reads "sons of Israel" in Deuteronomy 32:8. The ESV follows a Dead Sea Scroll,
4Q37, which reads "sons of God", and the Septuagint, which reads "angels of God" (*angelōn theou*);
the NLT, CSB and LSB footnotes all record the split. Only two words of that verse survive in the
scroll, so the reading rests on the Septuagint as much as on the scroll. On the ESV's reading, the
Most High assigned the nations to heavenly beings and kept Israel for Himself. Deuteronomy 4:19-20
says something close: the sun, moon and stars are "things that the LORD your God has allotted to all
the peoples," but "the LORD has taken you" (ESV).

### Princes in heaven

Daniel 10 names such powers. The angel sent to Daniel was held back: "The prince of the kingdom of
Persia withstood me twenty-one days, but Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me" (Daniel
10:13, ESV). Next comes "the prince of Greece" (Daniel 10:20), and Michael is "your prince" (Daniel
10:21), Israel's. The *ESV Study Bible* calls the prince of Persia "an evil angel associated with
the Persian Empire" (note on Daniel 10:13). The word is <span dir="rtl">שַׂר</span> (*sar*, SAR,
H8269), the same word Psalm 82:7 uses: "fall like any prince," literally "like one of the
<span dir="rtl">שָׂרִים</span> (*sarim*, H8269)." Daniel also uses *sar* for human officials (Daniel 1:7), so the
word alone does not decide who is meant.

Other texts show God among a heavenly court: "the host of heaven standing beside him" (1 Kings
22:19, ESV); "the sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD" (Job 1:6, ESV); "Who among
the heavenly beings is like the LORD, a God greatly to be feared in the council of the holy ones"
(Psalm 89:6-7, ESV), where "heavenly beings" is <span dir="rtl">בְּנֵי אֵלִים</span> (*bene ʾelim*).

### What the nations worshipped

Scripture holds two things together about the gods of the nations. They are nothing: "all the gods
of the peoples are worthless idols, but the LORD made the heavens" (Psalm 96:5, ESV), a pun on
*ʾĕlōhîm* and <span dir="rtl">אֱלִילִים</span> (*ʾelilim*, H457, "worthless things"). And there is
something behind them: "They sacrificed to demons that were no gods" (Deuteronomy 32:17, ESV). Paul
says both: "there may be so-called gods in heaven or on earth" (1 Corinthians 8:5, ESV), and "what
pagans sacrifice they offer to demons and not to God" (1 Corinthians 10:20, ESV).

God judges these powers as He judges human rulers. "On that day the LORD will punish the host of
heaven, in heaven, and the kings of the earth, on the earth" (Isaiah 24:21, ESV). Paul names them
"the rulers, … the authorities, … the cosmic powers over this present darkness" (Ephesians 6:12,
ESV), τὰς ἀρχάς (*tas archas*, G746) and τὰς ἐξουσίας (*tas exousias*, G1849), and says God seated
Christ "far above all rule and authority and power and dominion" (Ephesians 1:21, ESV). At the cross
"he disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame" (Colossians 2:15, ESV). **This
shows that God gives authority in heaven as well as on earth, and that every holder of it answers to
Him and will be judged by Him.**

## Psalm 82: authority given, abused and judged

The psalm has a frame and a speech. The psalmist speaks in verses 1 and 8; God speaks in verses 2-7
(*NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible*, note on Psalm 82).

### The charge and the job description

> ✝️ Psalm 82:2-5 (ESV)
>
> 2 "How long will you judge unjustly and show partiality to the wicked? Selah 3 Give justice to the
> weak and the fatherless; maintain the right of the afflicted and the destitute. 4 Rescue the weak
> and the needy; deliver them from the hand of the wicked." 5 They have neither knowledge nor
> understanding, they walk about in darkness; all the foundations of the earth are shaken.

"How long" is usually the cry of the sufferer to God. Here God asks it of the judges (*NIV Biblical
Theology Study Bible*, note on 82:2). Verses 3-4 are the work they were given: justice for the weak,
the fatherless, the afflicted, the needy. Their authority was meant to be seen in that work. What
they did instead was favour the wicked, and verse 5 says what that does: "all the foundations of the
earth are shaken." Injustice in a court is a crack in creation.

### The sentence

> ✝️ Psalm 82:6-8 (ESV)
>
> 6 I said, "You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you; 7 nevertheless, like men you shall die,
> and fall like any prince." 8 Arise, O God, judge the earth; for you shall inherit all the nations!

"I said" is the word that made them gods: God gave them the title. "Nevertheless" takes it away. The
ones God called gods will die like <span dir="rtl">אָדָם</span> (*ʾadam*, H120, "man") and fall like any
*sar*. Then the psalmist asks God to do the judging Himself. **This shows that God holds every
authority to account, and that the weak can appeal past a corrupt court to Him.**

### Who are the gods? (contested)

**This is contested, and good readers hold each of three views.** The translators record the fork
themselves: the CSB footnotes the gods of 82:1, "Or the heavenly beings, or the earthly rulers," and
the LSB, "Or rulers."

- **Human judges.** The *ESV Study Bible* argues that because these gods "judge" among men and "die
  like men", they are "human rulers, who hold their authority as representatives of the true God"
  (note on Psalm 82:1). Exodus 22:8-9 uses *ʾĕlōhîm* of Israel's judges. And Jesus' own gloss points
  this way: "he called them gods to whom the word of God came" (John 10:35, ESV), which most naturally
  means people who received God's word.
- **Heavenly powers.** The ESV renders 82:1 "the divine council," and Psalm 89:5-7, 1 Kings 22:19 and
  Job 1:6 show God among heavenly beings. "Like men you shall die" can be read as a sentence on beings
  who would otherwise not die. Verse 8's "all the nations" fits Deuteronomy 32:8 on the Septuagint's
  reading. The *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* takes the gods as heavenly beings and adds that
  the word "is used both of supernatural beings and of the human rulers who are their agents" (note
  on 82:6). The Septuagint reads 82:1 as "the assembly of gods" (*synagōgē theōn*), plural, where the
  Hebrew has "the assembly of God."
- **Israel at Sinai.** The *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* reports that "some ancient Jewish
  interpreters" applied the psalm "to Israel receiving the law at Mount Sinai" (note on John 10:34).
  That reading fits "to whom the word of God came" (John 10:35) closely, and the same note allows
  that Jesus may be appealing to it. The note's own preference is that the psalm "probably mocks
  ancient Near Eastern rulers who saw themselves as divine."

The heavenly reading and the human reading can stand together. Isaiah 24:21 has the LORD punishing "the host of heaven,
in heaven, and the kings of the earth, on the earth" together, and Daniel 10 shows the princes of
Persia and Greece fighting over what happens at Persia's court. On either reading the point stands:
the title was lent, the holders abused it, and God revokes it.

## The rebuke in the quotation

### "To whom the word of God came"

Jesus called the psalm "your Law" (John 10:34), using "Law" for the Scriptures as a whole, and His
Greek matches the Septuagint word for word: Ἐγὼ εἶπα· Θεοί ἐστε (*Egō eipa: theoi este*, "I said,
you are gods"). Then He argued from the lesser to the greater. If God called men "gods" because His
word came to them, and "Scripture cannot be broken" (John 10:35, ESV), then the one "whom the Father
consecrated and sent into the world" cannot be charged with blasphemy for saying "I am the Son of
God" (John 10:36, ESV). The *ESV Study Bible* notes that the whole argument rests on one word,
"gods", and on Scripture being true to the word (note on John 10:35).

"Consecrated" is ἡγίασεν (*hēgiasen*, from ἁγιάζω, *hagiazō*, G37, "set apart, make holy"). At a
feast for the consecration of the temple, Jesus says the Father consecrated Him. The *NIV Cultural
Backgrounds Study Bible* notes that some read this as Jesus presenting Himself as the new temple
(note on John 10:36; compare John 2:21).

### Men who sat on Moses' seat

Jesus' spoken argument is about the title. The psalm He chose also carries a verdict, and the men
He quoted it to fit that verdict closely. **Whether Jesus meant the verdict is a reading, and it is
contested.** The *ESV Study Bible* and the *NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible* read John 10:34-36
as an argument about the title alone: if human judges can be called gods, the title fits the Son of
God far better (notes on John 10:34). John does not say Jesus meant the rest of the psalm, and two
of the texts below come from elsewhere: Matthew 23 is another Gospel and another day, and Ezekiel 34
shares no wording with Psalm 82. The *NLT Life Application Study Bible* goes further than the
others and says "Jesus was rebuking the religious leaders" (note on John 10:34-36). The case for
hearing the verdict too rests on how well the men fit it:

- **They held authority from God to teach.** "The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses' seat, so
  do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do. For they preach, but do not
  practice" (Matthew 23:2-3, ESV). Jesus honoured their office and condemned their works, which is
  Psalm 82 in two sentences.
- **They had just failed the weak.** Psalm 82:3-4 tells judges to rescue the weak and needy. The
  authorities in John 9 interrogated a beggar who had been given his sight, then "cast him out" (John
  9:34, ESV). Jesus went and found him (John 9:35).
- **Jesus had already named them false shepherds.** "He who does not enter the sheepfold by the door
  but climbs in by another way, that man is a thief and a robber" (John 10:1, ESV). Ezekiel 34 had
  said the same of Israel's leaders: "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not
  healed" (Ezekiel 34:4, ESV).
- **Their works showed whose they were.** "If you were Abraham's children, you would be doing the
  works Abraham did, but now you seek to kill me" (John 8:39-40, ESV).
- **They were judging unjustly at that moment.** Jesus had told them, "Do not judge by appearances,
  but judge with right judgment" (John 7:24, ESV). Now they held stones over a man for His good works
  (John 10:32).

If the verdict is in view, to quote "you are gods" to such men was to stand them in the psalm's
dock, because the psalm goes on: "nevertheless, like men you shall die." Either way, the argument
Jesus spelled out is the one about the title, and it holds on its own.

### How they took it

They tried again to seize Him. "Again they sought to arrest him, but he escaped from their hands"
(John 10:39, ESV). The argument from the psalm did not soften them; they still heard a claim to be
God. John does not say whether the psalm, the rebuke inside it, or Jesus' last sentence, "the Father
is in me and I am in the Father" (John 10:38), moved them to act. All three were in front of them.
What the text does show is that they understood Him to be claiming what the judges of Psalm 82 were
never given.

## The works of the Father

The judges' authority was meant to be shown in their works, and their works condemned them. Jesus
put His claim on the same ground and invited them to test it:

> ✝️ John 10:25 (ESV)
>
> 25 Jesus answered them, "I told you, and you do not believe. The works that I do in my Father's
> name bear witness about me,

He said it again in John 5:36: "the works that the Father has given me to accomplish, the very works
that I am doing, bear witness about me that the Father has sent me" (ESV). In John 9 the work was
sight for a man born blind. The authorities could not dispute the work, so they cast out the man.

Here the two halves of the study meet. The judges of Psalm 82 held God's title and did the wicked's
work. Jesus did the Father's work, the work Psalm 82:3-4 described, and the authorities called it
blasphemy. **This shows that God is known by His works, and that the Father has put His own works in
the hands of His Son.** "The Father is in me and I am in the Father" (John 10:38, ESV) is a claim no
office could make. It names a union, and the works are its evidence.

The judging God reserved to Himself in Psalm 82:8 is in the Son's hands as well: "the Father judges
no one, but has given all judgment to the Son, that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the
Father" (John 5:22-23, ESV). The New Testament never joins John 5:22 to Psalm 82:8. Read together,
the Son is the one who answers the psalm's last prayer. So you may bring every injustice to Jesus,
whom the Father has made Judge, and honour Him with the honour you give the Father.

## A title lent for an office

Some teach that believers are "little gods" and cite Psalm 82:6. The psalm gives the title to judges
for their office and then sentences them to die like men; it is a verse of judgment. Jesus quoted it
to separate Himself from those gods, as the Son the Father consecrated. And Paul, who knew there are
"so-called gods in heaven or on earth," wrote that "for us there is one God, the Father, … and one
Lord, Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 8:5-6, ESV). The study [Pride](../sin/pride.md#you-are-gods)
works through the three verses usually cited.

What God does give you is real. He lends you authority in your home, your work and your church, and
He calls it His. You will answer to Him for the weak who were under it.

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** In Exodus 22:9 *ʾĕlōhîm* takes a plural verb, and in Psalm 82:1 the first
   *ʾĕlōhîm* takes singular verbs. What does reading the verb tell you that the noun alone could not?
2. **The text in its context.** Jesus was answering men who had cast a healed beggar out of the
   synagogue (John 9:34). How would they have heard "Give justice to the weak and the fatherless"
   (Psalm 82:3)?
3. **Christ.** Jesus said, "If I am not doing the works of my Father, then do not believe me" (John
   10:37). What does it say about Him that He offered His works as the test of His claim?
4. **The hard part.** Psalm 82:7 says the gods will "die like men", and Isaiah 24:21 punishes "the
   host of heaven" and "the kings of the earth" together. How do those texts bear on whether the gods
   of Psalm 82 are human judges or heavenly powers?
5. **The powers.** Daniel 10:13 shows a prince of Persia resisting God's messenger, and Colossians
   2:15 says Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities." What does the second verse change about
   how you read the first?
6. **Be transformed.** Psalm 82:3-4 is the job God gave to everyone He set over others. Who is weak
   or overlooked under the authority God has lent you, and what would justice for them look like this
   week?
