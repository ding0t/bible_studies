# The Angel of the LORD: God the Son Before Bethlehem

An Egyptian slave woman runs away from her mistress into the desert, pregnant and alone. Someone
finds her by a spring and calls her by name. He tells her to go back, promises her more descendants
than she can count, and names the son she is carrying. When He has gone, Hagar gives a name to the
One who spoke to her, and Moses, telling the story, says who it was: "the LORD" (Genesis 16:13,
BSB). Genesis has just called Him "the angel of the LORD" four times.

**In one sentence:** The angel of the LORD speaks as God, holds the power to forgive, is not
stopped when people fall before Him and is called the LORD by the narrators, yet Scripture sets Him
beside the LORD who sends Him; the
New Testament names the One who makes the unseen Father known, so the likeliest reading is that He
is God the Son before Bethlehem, who found Hagar at a spring and called Moses from the bush, and
who came in the flesh as Jesus to find you.

## Key Takeaways

*(This section follows the [Key Takeaways](../about/key-takeaways.md) format — see that page for what each part is for.)*

### Prophecy

Malachi 3:1 promises that "the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to His temple—the Messenger of
the covenant" (BSB). The messenger sent ahead of Him is John the Baptist (Mark 1:2), so the Lord who
comes after John is Jesus.

### Lessons about Jesus

- **He was at work before He was born.** He was before Abraham (John 8:58), and the Son who makes
  the Father known (John 1:18) most likely came to Hagar, Abraham, Jacob, Moses, Gideon and Manoah
  as the angel of the LORD.
- **He is God, and He is sent.** The angel speaks as the LORD and is sent by the LORD, the pattern
  the New Testament gives the Son (John 1:1; 3:17).
- **He came to the weak and the guilty,** and He forgives: "See, I have removed your iniquity"
  (Zechariah 3:4, BSB; Mark 2:7-10).

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** God has never been far off or unknown. The Son has always been the One who makes Him
  known, to Hagar at a spring and to you in the gospel (John 1:18).
- **Attitude.** Hold reverence and peace together. The One who calls the ground holy (Exodus 3:5)
  is the One who says to a terrified man, "Peace be with you. Do not be afraid" (Judges 6:23, BSB).
- **Do.** Hagar named the LORD for what He had done for her, and Gideon built an altar and called it
  "The LORD Is Peace" (Genesis 16:13; Judges 6:24, BSB). This week, write down one time the Lord
  saw you and met you, give that memory a name from what He did, and tell one person.

## Messenger: the word

<span dir="rtl">מַלְאָךְ</span> (*mal'akh*, H4397) means "messenger": one sent with a message or a
task, a man (Genesis 32:3) or an angel (Genesis 28:12). The Greek
<span lang="grc">ἄγγελος</span> (*angelos*, G32) has the same range: "Behold, I will send My
messenger ahead of You" (Mark 1:2, BSB) is said of John the Baptist. The word names a role.
What kind of being fills it, the context says.

The phrase <span dir="rtl">מַלְאַךְ יְהוָה</span> (*mal'akh YHWH*, "the angel of the LORD") comes
58 times in the Hebrew Old Testament. Twice it names a man: "Haggai, the messenger of the LORD"
(Haggai 1:13, BSB) and the priest, who "is the messenger of the LORD of Hosts" (Malachi 2:7, BSB).
The other 56, in 52 verses, name an angel. Most are the figure this study weighs; a few are a
comparison (Zechariah 12:8) or an angel sent to strike, which the study sets aside (the full count
is in the [Annex](#annex-which-appearances-count)). Five more times He is called "the angel of God"
(Genesis 21:17; 31:11; Exodus 14:19; Judges 6:20; 13:9).

In Hebrew a noun joined to a name takes its definiteness from the name: "the absolute noun always
determines whether a construct chain is definite or indefinite" (unfoldingWord Hebrew Grammar,
"State Construct"). So the translations print "*the* angel of the LORD". The same rule gives Haggai
the title "*the* messenger of the LORD", so the grammar alone cannot prove that one being is meant.
What the passages say He does carries the argument.

## He speaks as God

The angel of the LORD speaks in God's own first person, and the people He speaks to say they have
met God. Several times the narrator says it himself (Genesis 16:13; Exodus 3:4; Judges 6:14, 16).

### Hagar at the spring

The angel says, "I will greatly multiply your offspring so that they will
be too numerous to count" (Genesis 16:10, BSB). That is a promise only God can make, and He makes it
as His own. Then the narrator names Him:

> ✝️ Genesis 16:13 (BSB)
>
> 13 So Hagar gave this name to the LORD who had spoken to her: "You are the God who sees me," for she said, "Here I have seen the One who sees me!"

The name is <span dir="rtl">אֵל רֳאִי</span> (*El Roi*, H7210), "the God who sees". [The Names of
God](../god/names-of-god.md#the-god-who-sees-and-the-lord-who-will-see-to-it) treats the name; the
point here is who gave Hagar cause to use it. Moses calls Him "the angel of the LORD" in Genesis
16:7, 9, 10 and 11, and "the LORD" in 16:13.

### Abraham on the mountain

The angel calls from heaven as the knife is raised: "now I know that
you fear God, since you have not withheld your only son from me" (Genesis 22:12, BSB). Abraham was
offering Isaac to God. The angel calls the offering His. Then He swears: "By Myself I have sworn,
declares the LORD" (Genesis 22:16, BSB).

### Jacob's dream

"the angel of God said to me, 'Jacob!' ... I am the God of Bethel, where you
anointed the pillar and made a solemn vow to Me" (Genesis 31:11, 13, BSB). At Bethel it was "the
LORD" who stood above the ladder and said, "I am the LORD, the God of your father Abraham"
(Genesis 28:13, BSB). The angel says He is that God.

### Moses at the bush

"There the angel of the LORD appeared to him in a blazing fire from within a
bush" (Exodus 3:2, BSB). Two verses later the narrator says "the LORD saw that he had gone over to
look, God called out to him from within the bush" (Exodus 3:4, BSB). The voice says, "I am the God
of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (Exodus 3:6, BSB), and
gives His name, I AM (Exodus 3:14). One figure is in the fire, and Exodus calls Him both the angel
and God.

### Israel at Bochim

"I brought you up out of Egypt and led you into the land that I had promised
to your fathers, and I said, 'I will never break My covenant with you'" (Judges 2:1, BSB). The
angel speaks as the God of the exodus and of the covenant, with no "thus says the LORD".

### Gideon in the winepress

"The angel of the LORD appeared to Gideon" (Judges 6:12, BSB), and in
the conversation that follows the narrator writes, "The LORD turned to him and said, 'Go in the
strength you have and save Israel ... Am I not sending you?'" (Judges 6:14, BSB). Then "'Surely I
will be with you,' the LORD replied" (Judges 6:16, BSB). The Hebrew is
<span dir="rtl">כִּי אֶהְיֶה עִמָּךְ</span> (*ki ehyeh immakh*), the same three words God spoke to
Moses from the bush, "I will surely be with you" (Exodus 3:12, BSB), and *ehyeh*
(<span dir="rtl">אֶהְיֶה</span>, H1961) is the "I AM" of His name (Exodus 3:14). When the fire
takes the offering, Gideon cries, "Oh no, Lord GOD! I have seen the angel of the LORD face to
face!" (Judges 6:22, BSB). He expects to die for having seen God, and the LORD answers, "Peace be
with you. Do not be afraid, for you will not die" (Judges 6:23, BSB).

### Jacob's wrestler, in Hosea

The "man" who wrestled Jacob until daybreak (Genesis 32:24) is the one with whom Jacob "wrestled
with God. Yes, he struggled with the angel and prevailed" (Hosea 12:3-4, BSB), and "the LORD God of
Hosts, the LORD is His name of renown" (Hosea 12:5, BSB). Genesis calls him a man, and Hosea calls
him the angel and God.

### Jacob's blessing

Blessing Joseph's sons, Jacob prays, "May the God before whom my
fathers Abraham and Isaac walked, the God who has been my shepherd all my life to this day, the
angel who has redeemed me from all harm—may He bless these boys" (Genesis 48:15-16, BSB). Three
lines name the God, the God and the angel, and the verb that follows is singular,
<span dir="rtl">יְבָרֵךְ</span> (*yevarekh*, H1288, "may He bless"). The angel is
<span dir="rtl">הַגֹּאֵל</span> (*hago'el*, H1350), "the one who redeems", the kinsman-redeemer's
word ([The Kinsman Redeemer](kinsman-redeemer.md)).

So one figure, from Hagar's day to Gideon's, speaks as the LORD, and Genesis, Exodus, Judges and
Hosea name Him as the LORD. This shows that God came to His people and spoke to them Himself.

## He takes what only God takes

### Holy ground

"Take off your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy ground"
(Exodus 3:5, BSB). Outside Jericho a man with a drawn sword, "Commander of the LORD's army", says
the same to Joshua: "Take off your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy" (Joshua
5:14-15, BSB), and next "the LORD said to Joshua" (Joshua 6:2, BSB). Joshua 5 never calls the
Commander the angel of the LORD, so the identification is likely rather than certain: His drawn
sword matches the angel's in Balaam's road (Numbers 22:23), and His words are the bush's.

### The bow that is not stopped

Balaam "bowed low and fell facedown" before the angel (Numbers 22:31, BSB), and Joshua falls
facedown before the Commander (Joshua 5:14). Neither is told to get up. When John falls at the feet of a created angel, he is stopped at once: "Do not do that! I
am a fellow servant with you ... Worship God!" (Revelation 22:9, BSB).

### The power to forgive

God tells Israel to obey the angel He sends, "for he will not forgive rebellion, since My Name is
in him" (Exodus 23:21, BSB). The verb is <span dir="rtl">נָשָׂא</span> (*nasa'*, H5375, "to lift,
to bear, to forgive") with <span dir="rtl">פֶּשַׁע</span> (*pesha'*, H6588, "rebellion"), the same
pair the LORD uses of Himself when He proclaims His name: "forgiving iniquity, transgression, and
sin" (Exodus 34:7, BSB). That name is in the angel: <span dir="rtl">בְּקִרְבּוֹ</span> (*beqirbo*,
H7130, "within him"). An angel who can withhold forgiveness can give it, and in Zechariah's vision
He does: "See, I have removed your iniquity, and I will clothe you with splendid robes" (Zechariah
3:4, BSB). "Who can forgive
sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7, BSB).

### A name that is wonderful

Manoah asks the angel's name. "Why do you ask my name," said the angel of the LORD, "since it is beyond comprehension?" (Judges 13:18, BSB). The Hebrew is
<span dir="rtl">פִּלְאִי</span> (*pil'i*, H6383), "wonderful". In the next verse Manoah offers a
sacrifice "to the LORD", and the Hebrew goes on with "one doing wonders",
<span dir="rtl">מַפְלִא</span> (*mafli'*, H6381), a participle with no stated subject. Most
translations take it as the LORD (BSB "the LORD did a marvelous thing"). The wonder is the angel
rising in the flame (Judges 13:19-20): the name He would not give is what the LORD does. Isaiah uses
the noun of the same root, <span dir="rtl">פֶּלֶא</span> (*pele*, H6382), for the child who
is born: "He will be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God" (Isaiah 9:6, BSB). Manoah's verdict
is plain: "We are going to die ... for we have seen God!" (Judges 13:22, BSB).

## He is distinguished from the LORD

If the angel only ever spoke as God, the simplest reading would be that God appeared and Scripture
used "angel" for the appearance. Scripture also does the opposite. It sets the angel beside the
LORD, as a second who is sent, who speaks to the LORD and of the LORD.

### Sent

"Behold, I am sending an angel before you ... My angel will go before you" (Exodus 23:20,
23, BSB). The One who sends and the One sent are two. The Gospels give the first words of 23:20 to
John the Baptist, joined to Malachi 3:1 (Matthew 11:10; Mark 1:2), so the study rests nothing on
them; what marks this angel out is the next verse, "My Name is in him".

### Interceding

In Zechariah's first vision the angel of the LORD prays to the LORD for His
people: "How long, O LORD of Hosts, will You withhold mercy from Jerusalem and the cities of Judah"
(Zechariah 1:12, BSB). "So the LORD spoke kind and comforting words" in answer (Zechariah 1:13,
BSB). The New Testament says the same of the risen Son: "He always lives to intercede for them"
(Hebrews 7:25, BSB).

### The LORD speaking of the LORD

Joshua the high priest stands "before the angel of the LORD,
with Satan standing at his right hand to accuse him. And the LORD said to Satan: 'The LORD rebukes
you, Satan!'" (Zechariah 3:1-2, BSB). The one before whom Joshua stands is called "the LORD", and
He speaks of "the LORD" as another. Genesis does the same over Sodom: "the LORD rained down sulfur
and fire on Sodom and Gomorrah—from the LORD out of the heavens" (Genesis 19:24, BSB).

### The Messenger of the covenant

Malachi's prophecy names two messengers. The first prepares the
way, and the New Testament says he is John the Baptist (Mark 1:2). The second is the Lord Himself:

> ✝️ Malachi 3:1 (BSB)
>
> 1 "Behold, I will send My messenger, who will prepare the way before Me. Then the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to His temple—the Messenger of the covenant, in whom you delight—see, He is coming," says the LORD of Hosts.

"The Lord", <span dir="rtl">הָאָדוֹן</span> (*ha'adon*, H113), comes to "His temple", and He is
"the Messenger of the covenant", <span dir="rtl">מַלְאַךְ הַבְּרִית</span> (*mal'akh habberit*,
H4397, H1285): the same word, *mal'akh*. The temple is His, and He is sent.

So the angel of the LORD is God, and He is distinguished from the LORD who sends Him. This shows that the one God of
Israel is one God in more than one Person. Before the New Testament names Father and Son, the Old
Testament shows the LORD sending the LORD.

## What the New Testament adds

The Old Testament gives the pattern; the New Testament names the Person who fits it.

### The Son makes the unseen God known

"No one can see Me and live," the LORD told Moses (Exodus
33:20, BSB). Yet Jacob, Gideon and Manoah said they had seen God, and lived (Genesis 32:30; Judges
6:22-23; 13:22-23). John holds both: "No one has ever seen God, but the one and only Son, who is Himself God and is at the
Father's side, has made Him known" (John 1:18, BSB). So whenever God was seen, the likeliest
reading is that the Son made Him known. (Some manuscripts read "the only begotten Son" here and others "the only begotten God";
either way John says the Son makes the Father known.)

### The Son was there

Jesus says, "before Abraham was born, I am!" (John 8:58, BSB). John says that
Isaiah, in the vision of the Lord on His throne (Isaiah 6:1), "saw Jesus' glory and spoke about Him"
(John 12:41, BSB). The Greek reads "his glory", and the verses on either side make "his" Jesus
(John 12:37, 42). Paul says the rock that followed Israel through the wilderness "was Christ"
(1 Corinthians 10:4, BSB) and that Israel tested Christ there (1 Corinthians 10:9). Jude writes,
"Jesus had delivered His people out of the land of Egypt" (Jude 5, BSB). The angel of God went
before Israel's camp at the sea (Exodus 14:19), and the angel was to bring them through the
wilderness into the land (Exodus 23:20). The New Testament puts the Son on that road.

At 1 Corinthians 10:9 and Jude 5 some editions read "the Lord" (BSB footnotes), so the case rests
on neither verse alone; John 1:18 and 12:41 carry it without a variant that touches the point.

### Stephen keeps both names

Telling the story of the bush, Stephen says "an angel appeared to
Moses in the flames of a burning bush", and then "the voice of the Lord came to him: 'I am the God
of your fathers'" (Acts 7:30-32, BSB). God sent Moses "through the angel who appeared to him in the
bush" (Acts 7:35, BSB). The New Testament keeps the angel and the Lord together as Exodus does.

### Angels of the Lord in the New Testament

In the Gospels and Acts, "an angel of the Lord" appears to Zechariah, to Joseph, to the shepherds
and to Peter (Luke 1:11; Matthew 1:20; Luke 2:9; Acts 12:7). One gives his name: "I am Gabriel ... I
stand in the presence of God" (Luke 1:19, BSB). None of them speaks as God or takes a bow. The title
is the same, and these are created angels doing the work the word names.

This shows that the God who came near in Genesis is the God who came near in Jesus. The Son who took
flesh at Bethlehem had, most likely, been coming to His people since Hagar.

## The case against, and its answers

The reading has real objections, and each needs an answer from the text.

### An envoy speaks in his master's voice

The Rabshakeh stood before Jerusalem and said, "Hear the
word of the great king, the king of Assyria! This is what the king says" (2 Kings 18:28-29, BSB),
and then spoke as the king. So, the argument runs, the angel may be a created angel speaking for
God, and "I am the God of Bethel" is God's word in his mouth. The *ESV Study Bible* gives this view
beside the other (note on Genesis 16:7). The *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* describes the
ancient envoy as "a fully vested representative" who spoke in his master's person (note on Exodus
3:2).

The way the angel speaks does not settle it. Sometimes He speaks as a herald, "declares the LORD"
(Genesis 22:16) and "This is what the LORD of Hosts says" (Zechariah 3:7; compare 2 Kings 1:3-4);
sometimes as God, with no marker (Genesis 16:10; 31:13; Judges 2:1; Zechariah 3:4). What settles it is the narrators. The same note says of the
envoy that "there was no confusion about the messenger's identity" (note on Exodus 3:2). Genesis,
Exodus and Judges do the opposite and call the angel the LORD in their own voice: "the LORD who had
spoken to her" (Genesis 16:13), "God called out to him from within the bush" (Exodus 3:4), "The LORD
turned to him" (Judges 6:14). The Rabshakeh never becomes "the king" in the narrator's mouth.

### The bow proves nothing

The verb for Balaam's bow, <span dir="rtl">שָׁחָה</span> (*shachah*, H7812), is used of Jacob
bowing to Esau (Genesis 33:3). So the bow counts only beside holy ground, the power to forgive, the
narrators' naming and Revelation 22:9.

### Hebrews sets the Son above the angels

"to which of the angels did God ever say: 'Sit at My
right hand'" (Hebrews 1:13, BSB); "Let all God's angels worship Him" (Hebrews 1:6, BSB). If the
Son is above the angels, how can He be one? *Mal'akh* names a task, as it does for Haggai and the
priest, so calling the Son "the angel of the LORD" says He was sent and leaves His nature to the
other texts. Malachi calls the Lord who owns the temple "the Messenger of the covenant"
(Malachi 3:1) without making Him a creature.

### The angel points away from Himself

Manoah offers food, and the angel replies, "Even if I stay,
I will not eat your food. But if you prepare a burnt offering, offer it to the LORD" (Judges 13:16,
BSB). This is the strongest objection, because it sounds like Revelation 22:9. But Judges does not
end the scene there. The angel rises in the flame of that offering (Judges 13:20), the narrator
says the LORD did a wonder, and Manoah concludes, "we have seen God!" (Judges 13:22, BSB). His
wife answers that the LORD "accepted the burnt offering" (Judges 13:23, BSB). The angel directs the
sacrifice to the LORD and goes up in it, as the Son directed all things to the Father.

### The angel God would send in His place

After the golden calf, God says, "I will send an angel before you ... But I will not go with you"
(Exodus 33:2-3, BSB). Here an angel is plainly less than God's own going, and Moses refuses him:
"If Your Presence does not go with us ... do not lead us up from here" (Exodus 33:15, BSB). God answers,
"My Presence will go with you" (Exodus 33:14, BSB). So not every angel God sends is God in person,
and Exodus marks the difference: the angel of 33:2 carries no word about God's Name, and the
Presence that goes with Moses is God Himself. The study therefore rests on what each passage says
the angel does, and sets the angel of 23:21, with God's Name in him, beside the Presence of 33:14.

### Is every "angel of the LORD" the same one?

Probably not. The angel sent to strike Jerusalem is commanded to withdraw (1 Chronicles 21:15, 27), and the angel who struck the Assyrian camp says
nothing (2 Kings 19:35). Those passages give no evidence either way, and this study does not count
them. Its case rests on the appearances where the angel speaks and is received.

### How sure, then?

Settled from the text: the angel of the LORD in Genesis 16, 22 and 31, Exodus 3,
Judges 2, 6 and 13 and Zechariah 3 speaks as God and is called God by the narrator or by those who
meet Him, and He is also distinguished from the LORD who sends Him. Likely, from the pattern and the
New Testament together: He is God the Son before His birth. No single verse says "the angel of the
LORD was Christ". The *ESV Study Bible* gives both views (note on Genesis 16:7), and neither touches
the deity of Christ, which the New Testament states outright (John 1:1; 20:28). The reading this
study holds is the one that explains all the evidence above, and the one the New Testament points
toward.

## Why He came

Look at whom He came to. The first person the angel of the LORD speaks to in Scripture is an
Egyptian slave woman, pregnant, running away, alone in the desert. He finds her by a spring and
calls her by name: "Hagar, servant of Sarai ... where have you come from, and where are you going?"
(Genesis 16:8, BSB). Then He came to a father with a knife over his son, a fugitive shepherd, a man
hiding grain in a winepress, a barren woman and a high priest in filthy clothes. Each time He came
with mercy: a promise to Hagar, a ram for Isaac (Genesis 22:13), a mission for Moses, peace for
Gideon, a son for Manoah's wife, clean robes for Joshua the high priest. Isaiah sums up the exodus
in one line: "In all their distress, He too was afflicted, and the Angel of His Presence saved them. In His love and compassion He redeemed
them" (Isaiah 63:9, BSB).

The form of those meetings belonged to their time: fire in a bush, a figure under an oak, an angel
rising in the flame of an altar. God has now spoken to us in His Son (Hebrews 1:1-2), and nothing
in Scripture tells you to look for an angel by a spring. What carries over is who came and why.

This shows that God has always come to the outcast, the frightened and the guilty, and come to save,
and the likeliest reading is that the One who came was His Son. What He did in the desert, in the winepress and in Zechariah's courtroom, He did for good
when "the Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us" (John 1:14, BSB). He has come looking for
you, and He sees you as He saw Hagar. The One who said to Joshua the high priest, "I
have removed your iniquity", went to the cross to remove yours, so that you may stand before God
clothed in His righteousness and hear Him say, "Peace be with you. Do not be afraid" (Judges 6:23,
BSB).

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** <span dir="rtl">מַלְאָךְ</span> (*mal'akh*) means "messenger", and the same
   phrase that names the angel of the LORD names Haggai and the priest (Haggai 1:13; Malachi 2:7).
   What does reading "the messenger of the LORD" in Genesis 16 or Exodus 3 change, and what does it
   leave untouched?
2. **The text in its context.** Read Genesis 16:7-13 and Exodus 3:2-6. Who does each narrator say
   was speaking, and how do the stories move from "the angel" to "the LORD"?
3. **Christ.** John 1:18 says no one has ever seen God, and Gideon and Manoah say they have seen God
   (Judges 6:22; 13:22). How does John's verse hold both together, and what does it tell you about
   who met them?
4. **The hard part.** In Judges 13:16 the angel tells Manoah to offer the sacrifice "to the LORD".
   Does that count against the angel being God, and what do Judges 13:19-23 add?
5. **Be transformed.** Hagar named the LORD "the God who sees me" (Genesis 16:13) when she was at her
   lowest. Where has He seen you, and what name for Him would you give that memory?

## Annex: Which appearances count

**The phrase.** <span dir="rtl">מַלְאַךְ יְהוָה</span> comes 58 times in the Hebrew Old Testament
(Westminster Leningrad Codex, OpenScriptures morphology, counted 2026-11-08). Two are men (Haggai
1:13; Malachi 2:7). Of the 56 that name an angel, Judges has 19, Numbers 10, Genesis 6, Zechariah
6, 1 Chronicles 5, 2 Kings 3 and the Psalms 3 (Psalm 34:7; 35:5-6), with one each in Exodus, 1
Kings, 2 Samuel and Isaiah. Among them are a comparison (Zechariah 12:8) and the angels sent to strike (2 Samuel 24:16; 2 Kings
19:35; 1 Chronicles 21), which the study sets aside. "The angel of God" (singular) is the same figure
at Genesis 21:17; 31:11; Exodus 14:19; Judges 6:20 and 13:9. Manoah's wife compares the man of God to
one, "like the angel of God" (Judges 13:6, BSB), and David is likened to "an angel of God" in 1 Samuel
29:9 and 2 Samuel 14:17, 20; 19:27.

**Three to keep apart.** Genesis 18 calls Abraham's visitors "three men" (Genesis 18:2) and says
"the LORD appeared to Abraham" (Genesis 18:1, BSB); two go on to Sodom and are called angels
(Genesis 19:1), and Abraham remains "before the LORD" (Genesis 18:22, BSB). Genesis never calls the
third "the angel of the LORD". With Jacob's wrestler (named an angel only in Hosea 12:4) and the
Commander of Joshua 5:14, it fits the pattern and is held with less confidence than the passages
that use the phrase.

**Where the texts differ.**

- **Judges 6:14, 16.** The Hebrew reads "the LORD turned to him"; the Septuagint (Brenton's text)
  reads "the angel of the Lord turned to him". At 6:16 the Hebrew has "Surely I will be with you,"
  the LORD replied, and the Greek has the angel of the Lord say, "The Lord will be with you". The
  Greek keeps the angel where the Hebrew names the LORD, and turns His first person into a herald's.
- **Isaiah 63:9.** The Hebrew is written <span dir="rtl">לא</span> ("not") and read
  <span dir="rtl">לוֹ</span> ("to Him"), so the line is "in all their distress He was distressed"
  (the reading most translations follow). The Septuagint divides the words differently and reads
  "not an envoy nor an angel, but He Himself saved them". The NLT follows the Greek in the second line ("he
  personally rescued them", footnoting the Hebrew); the ESV, NIV, NASB, LSB and CSB keep "the angel
  of his presence".
  The Isaiah scroll from Qumran (1QIsa<sup>a</sup>) has the written "not". Either reading says God
  Himself saved Israel, so this study quotes the verse without resting the argument on it.
- **John 1:18; 1 Corinthians 10:9; Jude 5.** See [What the New Testament adds](#what-the-new-testament-adds).
