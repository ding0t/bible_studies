# The Kinsman Redeemer: Boaz, Ruth, and the Right to Buy Back

A family leaves Bethlehem because there is no bread in the House of Bread. They go to Moab, which is
enemy country. Three men die there. Two widows come back with nothing, and one of them is a Moabite.
Four chapters later a genealogy arrives, and it ends with David.

Between the emptiness and the genealogy sits a legal transaction at a town gate. **The book of Ruth
is built on an Israelite institution called the <span dir="rtl">גֹּאֵל</span> (*goel*) — the
kinsman-redeemer — and it is the one place in Scripture where the office is shown working end to
end, in public, with a man who declines it and a man who pays.**

## Key Takeaways

### Types & Prophecy

**Type — the redeemer.** The *goel* is a family office with a statute behind it. Leviticus assigns it to the
nearest relative: "If your brother becomes poor and sells part of his property, then his nearest
redeemer shall come and redeem what his brother has sold" (Leviticus 25:25, ESV). Three things are
required of him and Ruth tests all three in public — he must be kin, he must be able to pay, and he
must be willing. Boaz is the only man in the story who is all three.

**Prophecy — the line.** The narrator's last word is a genealogy: "Obed fathered
Jesse, and Jesse fathered David" (Ruth 4:22, ESV). Matthew picks the same names up and puts the
Moabite in the Messiah's line by name: "Boaz the father of Obed by Ruth" (Matthew 1:5, ESV).

### Lessons about Jesus

- Redemption in Israel was never a transaction between strangers. It required a *kinsman* — which is
  why the incarnation is load-bearing.
- The office could be declined. A man with the legal right refused it because exercising it would
  cost him his own inheritance (Ruth 4:6). Ability and right are not the same as willingness.
- What Boaz buys, he takes as a wife. The redemption and the marriage are one witnessed act
  (Ruth 4:9-10), which is the shape the New Testament gives to Christ and the church — see
  [The Bride of Christ](../israel-and-church/bride-of-christ.md).
- Isaiah gives God the title outright: "your Redeemer the Holy One of Israel" (Isaiah 54:5, ESV).

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** Redemption in Scripture is a purchase with a named price and a named buyer. Where you
  have been treating it as a mood or a metaphor, put the transaction back in.
- **Attitude.** Ruth arrives with no claim — foreign, widowed, poor, gleaning at the edge of someone
  else's field. That is the posture the book gives its heroine, and it is the one it commends.
- **Do.** Boaz acts at the gate, in daylight, in front of witnesses. Find the obligation you have
  been handling privately because handling it publicly would cost you something.

## The world the book is set in

"In the days when the judges ruled there was a famine in the land, and a man of Bethlehem in Judah
went to sojourn in the country of Moab" (Ruth 1:1, ESV). That single verse carries the book's
problem. The judges period is the one Scripture sums up as a time when "everyone did what was right
in his own eyes" (Judges 17:6, ESV), and Bethlehem — *beth lechem*, house of bread — has no bread.

The book was written later. The narrator explains the sandal ceremony as a custom of "former times
in Israel" (Ruth 4:7), which puts distance between him and the events, and he names David at the
end, which places the writing after David's accession (*ESV Study Bible*, introduction to Ruth). The
author is never named; rabbinic tradition assigns it to Samuel (*b. Baba Bathra* 14a-15b), which the
same introduction doubts, since Samuel died before David reigned.

Three laws drive the plot, and the story is unreadable without them:

| Institution | Where it is given | What it provides |
|---|---|---|
| Gleaning | Leviticus 19:9-10 | The poor and the sojourner may gather what the harvesters leave |
| The *goel* | Leviticus 25:25 | The nearest relative may buy back land a kinsman has lost |
| Levirate duty | Deuteronomy 25:5-10 | A brother raises up offspring for a man who died without a son |

Ruth 4 is where the second and third meet, and the book spends its climax on legal procedure rather
than on the wedding.

## The office of the goel

<span dir="rtl">גֹּאֵל</span> (*goel*, H1350) is a participle — "the one redeeming" — from
<span dir="rtl">גָּאַל</span> (*gaal*, TWOT root 300), to redeem or buy back. It names a person
before it names an act. The redeemer is the nearest kinsman, and the office attaches to him by
blood: he has both the right to redeem and the obligation to.

The requirements surface as the story goes. He must be **kin**, which is why
the narrator introduces Boaz as "a relative of her husband's" before anything else happens (Ruth
2:1). He must be **able** — the redemption costs money, and a man without it cannot perform the
office. And he must be **willing**, which is the requirement Ruth 4 puts on trial.

### The nearer redeemer who says no

Boaz is not first in line, and he says so at the gate. The man with the prior claim is offered the
field, accepts, and then hears that Ruth comes with it. He withdraws:

> ✝️ Ruth 4:6 (ESV)
>
> 6 Then the redeemer said, "I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I impair my own inheritance. Take
> my right of redemption yourself, for I cannot redeem it."

He is kin and he is able. He weighs the redemption against his own estate and steps back, and the
narrator lets him go unnamed. Boaz greets him at 4:1 with
<span dir="rtl">פְּלֹנִי אַלְמֹנִי</span> (*peloni almoni*, H6423 + H492), a Hebrew idiom for
someone whose name is withheld; the ESV smooths it to "friend" and the interlinear glosses it "a
certain person". He is the only significant character in the book without a name. **This shows that a right to redeem and a willingness to pay for it are different
things, and that God's people were taught the difference by watching a man decline.**

### The wings, twice

Before the gate there is a threshing floor, and before the threshing floor there is a blessing. Boaz
meets Ruth gleaning and blesses her by the God "under whose wings you have come to take refuge"
(Ruth 2:12, ESV). The word is <span dir="rtl">כְּנָפָיו</span> (*kenafav*), from
<span dir="rtl">כָּנָף</span> (*kanaph*, H3671) — a wing, and also the corner or skirt of a garment.

A chapter later Ruth uses the same noun back at him:

> ✝️ Ruth 3:9 (WEB)
>
> 9 He said, "Who are you?" She answered, "I am Ruth your servant. Therefore spread the corner of
> your garment over your servant; for you are a near kinsman."

She asks Boaz to become the answer to the blessing he pronounced. The same gesture is God's own at
Ezekiel 16:8, where spreading the *kanaph* over a foundling is the act that makes a covenant — which
is why the same verse carries both the redeeming and the betrothing. **This shows that God's way of
covering the destitute has a shape, and He gave it to Israel as a law before He performed it
Himself.**

## The transaction at the gate

Boaz takes it up in public. Ten elders are seated as witnesses, the sandal changes hands, and Boaz
states what he has done in two parts:

> ✝️ Ruth 4:9-10 (ESV)
>
> 9 Then Boaz said to the elders and all the people, "You are witnesses this day that I have bought
> from the hand of Naomi all that belonged to Elimelech and all that belonged to Chilion and to
> Mahlon. 10 Also Ruth the Moabite, the widow of Mahlon, I have bought to be my wife, to perpetuate
> the name of the dead in his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among
> his brothers and from the gate of his native place. You are witnesses this day."

"I have bought" is <span dir="rtl">קָנִיתִי</span> (*qaniti*), from <span dir="rtl">קָנָה</span>
(*qanah*, H7069), to acquire. The land and the woman are acquired in one sentence, before the same
witnesses, on the same day — **the redemption and the marriage are a single legal act.** And the
purpose Boaz gives is not his own line but a dead man's: the name of Mahlon, raised up over his
inheritance.

The women of Bethlehem name what has happened, and they name God as the one who did it:
"Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a redeemer" (Ruth 4:14, ESV).

## The Moabite problem

Ruth is called "the Moabite" at the moment of acquisition, and the narrator has used that label
throughout. It is not incidental, because the law says this:

> ✝️ Deuteronomy 23:3 (ESV)
>
> 3 "No Ammonite or Moabite may enter the assembly of the LORD. Even to the tenth generation, none
> of them may enter the assembly of the LORD forever,

David is four generations from Boaz. **This is a genuine tension in the text and the readings of it
are contested.** Three lines are commonly taken, and this draft does not settle between them:

- The Hebrew of Deuteronomy 23:3 is masculine, and may bar Moabite *men* from the assembly rather
  than Moabite women who marry into Israel.
- The ban may concern the Moabite nation as a cultic body, leaving room for an individual who
  renounces it — which is what Ruth does in as many words: "Your people shall be my people, and your
  God my God" (Ruth 1:16, ESV).
- The narrative may be deliberately set against the statute, as a case the law's framers did not
  legislate for.

What the book itself does is put the confession before the redemption. Ruth says "your God my God"
in chapter 1; Boaz buys her in chapter 4. The *CSB Ancient Faith Study Bible* reads the book's
contribution along exactly this line, as a demonstration that those outside ethnic Israel could be
brought in through faith, and connects it forward to Isaiah 56:3-7.

## The genealogy is the point

The book does not end at the wedding. It ends with ten names, and the last three are the ones it was
travelling toward: "Obed fathered Jesse, and Jesse fathered David" (Ruth 4:22, ESV).

Matthew takes the same line up and does something the Old Testament genealogy does not — he names
the women. "Boaz the father of Obed by Ruth" (Matthew 1:5, ESV). The Moabite widow who gleaned at
the edge of a field stands in the ancestry of the Messiah, named, four verses into the New Testament.

**This shows that God was building the line of His Son out of the people the law had least room
for**, and that the redemption He would perform through that line was rehearsed first at a town gate
in Bethlehem, by a man who was kin, and able, and willing.

## Discussion Questions

1. The *goel* had to be kin, able and willing. Which of the three does the New Testament spend most
   effort establishing about Christ, and why that one?
2. The nearer redeemer is the only significant character in the book left unnamed, and his reason
   for declining is a sound financial one (Ruth 4:6). What is the narrator doing by leaving him
   anonymous?
3. Boaz blesses Ruth under God's wings (2:12) and is then asked to spread his own over her (3:9).
   What does it do to your reading of ordinary kindness that a blessing can be answered through the
   person who prayed it?
4. Deuteronomy 23:3 bars a Moabite from the assembly; Ruth 4:22 makes a Moabite David's
   great-grandmother. Read the two side by side. How would you explain the tension to someone who
   raised it as an objection, without flattening either text?
5. The book's climax is legal procedure at a gate rather than a wedding. Why might the narrator have
   chosen to spend his last chapter that way?
6. Ruth arrives with no standing — foreign, widowed, poor. Where has your own standing before God
   quietly become something you think you brought with you?
