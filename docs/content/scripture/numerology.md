# Biblical numerology

Psalm 119 has 176 verses. That is not a round number, and it is not an accident: it is 22 × 8 — one
stanza for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, eight verses to a stanza, every verse in a stanza
beginning with that stanza's letter. The poet built a structure you cannot see in English and cannot
miss in Hebrew, and he did it while writing about the sufficiency of God's word. The form *is* the
argument: God's instruction covers everything, A to Z.

That is biblical numerology at its most defensible — a numerical pattern the author deliberately
built, which a reader can verify letter by letter. It sits at one end of a spectrum whose other end
is a software industry selling hidden prophecies extracted from letter-skip sequences. This study is
about telling those apart.

### Lessons about Jesus

- The one number Scripture explicitly tells us to work out is the beast's, and it is given to a
  church under pressure so they can recognise the enemy — not to satisfy curiosity
  (Revelation 13:18).
- Matthew opens his Gospel by stating a numerical structure outright — three sets of fourteen
  generations (Matthew 1:17) — because he wants Jesus's descent from David to be impossible to miss.
  He explains the pattern rather than hiding it.
- Christ's own use of counting is soberly practical: the man who builds a tower first "counts the
  cost" (Luke 14:28). The only other place the New Testament uses that verb is Revelation 13:18.

>

### Be Transformed

- Apply one test to every numerical claim you meet, including the ones in this study: **did the
  author build this pattern, or did I?** A pattern you had to choose a counting method to produce is
  your pattern, not his.
- Notice how much of Scripture's craft you have been reading past. The point of learning that
  Lamentations is an alphabet running out of letters is not trivia — it is that the book was
  composed by someone containing grief inside a form on purpose.
- Be suspicious of any reading that makes you a member of a small circle who "sees" what ordinary
  readers miss. Scripture's structure is the opposite: publicly checkable, and pointing back to
  things already stated plainly.
- Watch for the tell in yourself. If a numerical argument arrives conveniently supporting what you
  already believed, that is precisely when to slow down.

## Why numbers and letters are the same thing in Hebrew and Greek

Neither biblical Hebrew nor Koine Greek had a separate set of digit symbols. There is no Hebrew "7"
the way there is an English 7. Each language used its own alphabet to count: א is 1, ב is 2, and so
on up through ת at 400. The full value table lives on the
[Hebrew alphabet](../resources/hebrew-alphabet.md#numbers) page.

This matters because it removes the mystery from the ground floor. When a Hebrew writer wrote a
number, he wrote letters — not because he was encoding a secret, but because that was the only way
to write a number. Every Hebrew word therefore *has* a numeric total, in the same unavoidable way
every English word has a length. The practice of comparing those totals is called **gematria**
(from Greek γεωμετρία, *geōmetria*), and the question is when, if ever, Scripture
intends you to do it.

You can watch the system operating in the text itself. Tischendorf's edition of the Greek New
Testament prints the number in Revelation 13:18 not as a word but as three numeral-letters — χξϛ',
that is *chi* (600) + *xi* (60) + *stigma* (6):

> ✝️ Revelation 13:18 (Tischendorf, Greek)
>
> 18 ὧδε ἡ σοφία ἐστίν· ὁ ἔχων νοῦν ψηφισάτω τὸν ἀριθμὸν τοῦ θηρίου, ἀριθμὸς γὰρ ἀνθρώπου ἐστίν· καὶ
> ὁ ἀριθμὸς αὐτοῦ χξϛ'.

## The acrostics: patterns you can verify

An acrostic poem runs the alphabet down the left margin. It is the clearest evidence in Scripture of
deliberate numerical composition, because it cannot happen by accident and it survives in the
consonantal text where anyone can check it.

Every entry in the table below was verified for this study by extracting the first consonant of each
verse from the Westminster Leningrad Codex and comparing the sequence against the 22-letter Hebrew
alphabet — not taken from a commentary's summary.

| Passage | Structure | Verified result |
|---|---|---|
| Psalm 119 | 22 stanzas × 8 verses = 176 | Perfect. Every 8th verse steps to the next letter, א through ת |
| Lamentations 1 | 22 verses, one per letter | Perfect, standard alphabet order |
| Lamentations 2 | 22 verses | Complete, but the order **inverts**: <span dir="rtl">פ</span> precedes <span dir="rtl">ע</span> |
| Lamentations 3 | 66 verses = 22 × 3 | Complete triple acrostic — three consecutive verses per letter — with the same inversion |
| Lamentations 4 | 22 verses | Complete, same inversion |
| Lamentations 5 | 22 verses | **Not an acrostic at all** — verified by control test |
| Proverbs 31:10-31 | 22 verses | Perfect, א through ת |
| Psalms 111, 112 | 22 lines each, over 10 verses | Acrostic works per poetic line, not per verse |
| Psalms 25, 34 | 22 verses each | Irregular — see below |
| Psalms 9-10 | Spans both psalms | Broken and partial — see below |

### What the irregularities are doing

**Lamentations breaks its own form on purpose.** Chapters 1-4 are alphabetic. Chapter 5 has exactly
22 verses — the right number — and no acrostic. The poet keeps the count and abandons the order. A
book that has spent four chapters containing catastrophe inside a disciplined form ends by letting
the form go while still holding the shape of it.

The <span dir="rtl">פ</span>/<span dir="rtl">ע</span> inversion is consistent: chapter 1 uses the familiar order, and chapters 2, 3
and 4 all put <span dir="rtl">פ</span> before <span dir="rtl">ע</span>. Three chapters agreeing against one is not a scribal
slip. Ancient Hebrew abecedaries show that letter order was not everywhere fixed the way it later
became.

**Psalms 25 and 34 share three identical irregularities.** In both, the superscription sits outside
the sequence, there is no waw verse, and an extra <span dir="rtl">פ</span> verse is appended after the <span dir="rtl">ת</span> verse has closed
the alphabet. Two psalms deviating in exactly the same three ways points to a shared convention
rather than two independent accidents.

**Psalms 9 and 10** carry a single acrostic between them, running roughly א through כ in Psalm 9 and
picking up again in Psalm 10, though too irregularly to tabulate honestly. This is one of the
standard arguments that they were originally one psalm — which is how the Septuagint and Vulgate
count them.

### A caution from a case that fails

Nahum 1:2-8 is often listed as a partial acrostic. Tested the same way as everything above — first
consonant per verse, and again per half-line — it does not hold up. The proposed sequence only
emerges if you emend the text to produce it. It is listed here as the honest negative result,
because a method that only ever confirms is not a method.

## When the acrostic catches a missing line

Psalm 145 is an alphabetic acrostic, and it has 21 verses. Twenty-one, not 22.

Running the check shows exactly where the gap is: verse 13 begins with <span dir="rtl">מ</span> (*mem*), and verse 14
begins with <span dir="rtl">ס</span> (*samek*). The <span dir="rtl">נ</span> (*nun*) line is simply absent from the Masoretic Text. The structure
tells you a line is missing in a way no amount of reading the English could.

And the missing line survives elsewhere. The Septuagint's Greek at this psalm (LXX Psalm 144:13)
carries a clause the Hebrew does not — verified directly against both texts for this study:

> ✝️ Psalm 144:13 LXX (Brenton, Greek — extra clause only)
>
> πιστὸς Κύριος ἐν τοῖς λόγοις αὐτοῦ, καὶ ὅσιος ἐν πᾶσι τοῖς ἔργοις αὐτοῦ.

"The LORD is faithful in all his words, and holy in all his works" — which in Hebrew would begin
<span dir="rtl">נֶאֱמָן</span> (*ne'eman*, "faithful"), a נ word, in exactly the slot the acrostic left empty. The CSB and
NLT translators' notes report the line as attested in the Dead Sea Scrolls and Syriac as well as the
Greek; the Septuagint half of that is what I checked myself.

### What the English versions do with it

This is the practically useful part of the finding, because a reader can see the editorial decision
on the page. Each of these was checked verse-by-verse rather than recalled:

| Version | Psalm 145:13 |
|---|---|
| Masoretic Text (WLC) | Absent |
| ASV | Absent |
| NASB 1995 | Absent |
| **ESV** | Present, **in square brackets** — "[The LORD is faithful in all his words and kind in all his works.]" |
| NIV | Present, unmarked |
| CSB | Present, unmarked |
| WEB | Present, unmarked |
| NASB 2020 | Present, unmarked — a reversal of NASB 1995 |

No version is simply right here; they are making different, defensible calls about a real textual
problem. The ESV is the most useful to a student because its brackets show the seam rather than
smoothing it over — the reader can tell something is being decided. The NASB's change between its
1995 and 2020 editions is a translation committee visibly changing its mind.

The wider point: the acrostic functions as a structural checksum. The poet's numerical design is
what makes a lost line detectable three thousand years later. That is what deliberate biblical
numerology is good for.

## The one number Scripture asks us to calculate

> ✝️ Revelation 13:18 (ESV)
>
> 18 This calls for wisdom: let the one who has understanding calculate the number of the beast, for
> it is the number of a man, and his number is 666.

The verb is **ψηφίζω** (*psēphizō*, G5585), "to count, reckon up, calculate." It comes from ψῆφος
(*psēphos*), the small pebble used both for tallying and for casting a vote — a physical, ordinary
word for working a sum.

It appears exactly twice in the Greek New Testament. The other occurrence is Luke 14:28, where a man
planning a tower first sits down and *counts the cost*. Both uses are about sober arithmetic done
before committing yourself. Neither carries any suggestion of mystical decoding; John is asking for
the same clear-headedness Jesus asked of a would-be builder.

### The arithmetic, and why the variant matters

Written in Hebrew letters, "Neron Caesar" — <span dir="rtl">נרון קסר</span> — totals 666:

| נ | ר | ו | ן | ק | ס | ר |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 200 | 6 | 50 | 100 | 60 | 200 |

The Hebrew transliteration of the Greek word for "beast" (θηρίον, *thērion*) — <span dir="rtl">תריון</span> — also totals
666, which the ESV Study Bible notes alongside the Nero calculation.

What makes the Nero result more than a coincidence is a textual variant. Some manuscripts of
Revelation read **616** rather than 666. And the Latin form of the same name, without the final nun —
<span dir="rtl">נרו קסר</span> — totals exactly 616. Drop one letter of spelling and the number moves by 50, which is that
letter's value. A scribe who understood the reference would adjust the total to match the spelling he
knew; a scribe who did not would have no reason to produce that particular number.

Irenaeus, writing around AD 180, describes precisely this, and treats it as a copyist's error:

> Some have erred following the ordinary mode of speech, and have vitiated the middle number in the
> name, deducting the amount of fifty from it.
>
> — Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.30.1

"Deducting the amount of fifty" is the missing nun, named by its value, seventeen centuries before
anyone ran the calculation on a computer.

### The earliest commentator's warning

Irenaeus is the earliest surviving witness on this verse, and he claims direct access to the chain of
transmission — 666 stands "in all the most approved and ancient copies," with "those men who saw John
face to face bearing their testimony to it" (5.30.1). He then does gematria himself, floating
*Lateinos* and *Teitan* as candidate names. And having done it, he refuses to conclude:

> It is therefore more certain, and less hazardous, to await the fulfilment of the prophecy, than to
> be making surmises, and casting about for any names that may present themselves, inasmuch as many
> names can be found possessing the number mentioned.
>
> — Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.30.3

That is the whole discipline in one sentence, from the second century. The method works — and
*because* it works too easily, on too many names, it cannot by itself identify anyone. Irenaeus even
names the pastoral danger: someone certain of the wrong name will fail to recognise the right man
when he comes.

Reading this dispensationally, Irenaeus's instinct is the sound one. The number was given to
first-century churches in a form that let them recognise the imperial power then persecuting them,
and it remains given to a future generation who will face a world ruler the text still expects. The
ESV Study Bible's note lands in the same place: it reports the Nero calculation and adds that many
interpreters "expect a future, greater fulfillment in a world ruler who is violently opposed to God
and his people." What the number does not do — for Irenaeus, or for us — is let anyone name a man in
advance.

### 666 as a symbol

Set the name calculations aside and the number still says something on the plain sense of the text,
because Revelation has spent twelve chapters establishing seven as the number of completeness. Six is
what falls short of seven. Man was made on the sixth day; the seventh is God's rest. A number
composed entirely of sixes, which John labels "the number of a man," is the creature straining after
divine completeness and missing by one, three times over. This reading requires no arithmetic and no
candidate name, only attention to Revelation's own established vocabulary.

## God's own numerical riddle — and who interprets it

The single clearest case of God using a number-based code is Daniel 5, and its most important feature
is usually skipped: **God supplies the interpretation Himself.**

> ✝️ Daniel 5:25 (ESV)
>
> 25 And this is the writing that was inscribed: MENE, MENE, TEKEL, and PARSIN.

The words on the wall — <span dir="rtl">מְנֵא מְנֵא תְּקֵל וּפַרְסִין</span> (*mene mene teqel u-pharsin*) — are Aramaic
**weights**: a mina, a mina, a shekel, and half-minas. A row of currency denominations. Belshazzar's
wise men could read them and still could not say what they meant, because as a list of weights they
mean nothing.

Daniel re-points each weight-name as a passive verb from the same root, and the wordplay is visible
in the morphology of the text:

- **MENE** — <span dir="rtl">מְנֵא</span>, a mina — read as <span dir="rtl">מְנָה</span> (*menah*):
  "God has numbered the days of your kingdom" (v26)
- **TEKEL** — <span dir="rtl">תְּקֵל</span>, a shekel — read as <span dir="rtl">תְּקִילְתָּה</span>
  (*teqiltah*): "you have been weighed in the balances and found wanting" (v27)
- **PARSIN** — <span dir="rtl">פַּרְסִין</span>, half-minas — read as
  <span dir="rtl">פְּרִיסַת</span> (*perisat*): "your kingdom is divided" (v28), then punned a second
  time against <span dir="rtl">פָּרָס</span> (*Paras*), Persia — "and given to the Medes and Persians"

Weights become verbs; a coin's name becomes an empire's name. It is as dense a piece of wordplay as
Scripture contains, and Belshazzar could not crack it. He needed a prophet, and the prophet was given
the meaning by God.

This is the hermeneutical key to the entire subject. Scripture *does* contain a divinely-intended
numerical code — exactly one clear instance — and the text itself immediately tells you what it says.
It does not leave the code to be extracted by later cleverness. Where God hides something in a number
and wants it known, He sends someone to say what it means.

## The responsible use of gematria: Matthew's genealogy

Matthew's genealogy is a good test case, because it shows what a defensible numerical argument looks
like and where the defensible part stops.

> ✝️ Matthew 1:17 (ESV)
>
> 17 So all the generations from Abraham to David were fourteen generations, and from David to the
> deportation to Babylon fourteen generations, and from the deportation to Babylon to the Christ
> fourteen generations.

**What is certain:** Matthew built his genealogy on a numerical structure, and he says so. He states
the 14 × 3 pattern in the text. He also achieved it by omission — comparing his list with 1 Chronicles
3:10-12 shows he skipped three generations between Joram and Uzziah. This is not a defect; ancient
genealogies routinely selected. But it does mean the fourteens are chosen, not merely counted, and
Matthew is content for you to know it.

**What is inference:** the usual explanation is that <span dir="rtl">דוד</span>, David's name, totals 14 (<span dir="rtl">ד</span> = 4, <span dir="rtl">ו</span> = 6, <span dir="rtl">ד</span> = 4), and that David is the 14th name in the list. That is arithmetically correct. But Matthew never
says it, and reputable commentators divide over it — the *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* notes
that some scholars attribute the correspondence to coincidence, and observes that Matthew's three
sets do not actually contain equal numbers of names once you count them.

The distinction is the thing to carry away. The numerical structure is *stated by the author*, and so
it is exegesis. The gematria explaining *why fourteen* is a modern reconstruction of an ancient
author's motive, and so it is inference — plausible, but not something to build doctrine on.

## Where this goes wrong

### Equidistant letter sequences and the "Bible code"

The claim, popularised by Michael Drosnin's 1997 bestseller and given academic footing by an earlier
paper, is that skipping every *n*th letter through the Hebrew text of Genesis reveals words and names
encoding future events. Witztum, Rips and Rosenberg published a statistical case in *Statistical
Science* in 1994, reporting that the names of famous rabbis appeared in close proximity to their birth
and death dates far more often than chance allowed.

It did not survive scrutiny. In 1999 the same journal published "Solving the Bible Code Puzzle" by
Brendan McKay, Dror Bar-Natan, Maya Bar-Hillel and Gil Kalai. Their central finding was that the
result was extremely sensitive to choices in how the rabbis' names and appellations were listed —
choices with enough latitude that a list could be tuned, consciously or not, toward a positive
result. Applying the same method to a Hebrew translation of *War and Peace* produced comparable
"codes." A method that finds prophecies in Tolstoy is not detecting prophecy.

**There is also a problem the statistics never reach**, and it is one this project can demonstrate
from its own data. ELS requires a fixed, exact letter sequence: insert or remove a single letter and
every skip-count downstream shifts, and every "code" past that point dissolves. No such single
sequence exists.

Comparing two respected digital editions of the Westminster Leningrad Codex — *the same manuscript* —
turns up 16 places in Genesis alone where their consonants differ. The differences are not errors.
They fall at the *ketiv/qere* points, where the Masoretic tradition deliberately transmits a word in
two forms at once: the consonants as written (*ketiv*) and the word as it is to be read aloud
(*qere*). At Genesis 8:17 the text as written is <span dir="rtl">הוצא</span>; the word to be read is <span dir="rtl">הַיְצֵא</span>. One edition
prints only the written form, the other prints both. Both are faithfully representing the same codex.

Widen the comparison and it gets worse for the method. Against the Samaritan Pentateuch, the
consonantal text of Genesis differs in 1,757 places. Around 72% of those are a single character, and
the commonest by far are an inserted <span dir="rtl">ו</span> (347 times) or <span dir="rtl">י</span> (178) — the *matres lectionis*, consonants
doing duty as vowel markers. This is spelling variation. It changes nothing whatever about what
Genesis *says*, and it annihilates every skip-distance in an ELS search.

So the code hunter must first decide which letter sequence is the real one. Scripture itself never
identifies one, and the doctrine of inspiration has never been a claim about a particular spelling
convention. A method whose results depend entirely on an editorial decision the Bible never makes is
not reading the Bible.

### Heptadic structures

Ivan Panin (1855-1942) claimed to have found thousands of patterns of seven throughout the Greek New
Testament — sevens in word counts, letter counts, vocabulary totals — and argued they proved divine
authorship beyond any possibility of human composition.

The weakness is structural rather than arithmetical. Panin worked from a text form he selected
himself, at a time when the Greek text was actively contested; and the number of things one *could*
count — words, letters, vocabulary items, words beginning with a vowel, nouns, words occurring only
once, and so on — is effectively unbounded. Given enough countable features and freedom over the
boundaries of the passage, sevens will appear. Without a pre-registered rule fixing what will be
counted before you look, a hit means very little.

**This study's own example makes the point.** Genesis 1:1 does contain seven words and 28 letters
(7 × 4) in the Masoretic text — I verified both:

| # | Word | Consonants |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | בראשית | 6 |
| 2 | ברא | 3 |
| 3 | אלהים | 5 |
| 4 | את | 2 |
| 5 | השמים | 5 |
| 6 | ואת | 3 |
| 7 | הארץ | 4 |
| | **Total** | **28** |

That is real. But "seven words" depends on the Masoretic convention of attaching prefixed
prepositions and articles to the following word. The MACULA linguistic database segments the identical
verse into **eleven** morphemes, because it counts <span dir="rtl">בְּ</span> ("in") and <span dir="rtl">הַ</span> ("the") separately. Both counts
are defensible; neither is wrong. The verse is "seven" or "eleven" depending on a decision about word
division that has nothing to do with inspiration.

That is the whole difficulty with heptadic arguments in one verse. Psalm 119's 22 × 8 does not have
this problem: the alphabet fixes the unit in advance, and the poet plainly chose it.

## A method for reading numbers in Scripture

Six working rules, drawn from everything above:

1. **Prefer patterns the author built and marked.** Psalm 119's acrostic and Matthew's stated
   fourteens are in a different category from anything requiring a technique to extract.
2. **Ask what unit is being counted, and who chose it.** If the pattern needs a particular counting
   convention, the pattern belongs partly to whoever chose the convention.
3. **Require the interpretation to come from Scripture.** Daniel 5 is the model: God gives a numerical
   riddle and then gives its meaning. A number whose significance is supplied entirely by the
   interpreter is the interpreter's.
4. **Test your method on something that should fail.** Nahum 1 fails the acrostic test in this study,
   and that is what makes the passing results worth anything.
5. **Never let a number carry doctrine alone.** Numbers reinforce meaning stated plainly elsewhere.
   If a numerical argument is the only support for a claim, the claim is unsupported.
6. **Check the reading against 2 Timothy 3:16-17.** The Scripture that is God-breathed and sufficient
   to equip "for every good work" is the text as it can be read. A message accessible only through
   computation is a message the ordinary reader — and every reader before computers — could not have
   had.

That last point is where Deuteronomy 29:29 does its work. Proverbs 25:2 invites investigation; God
does conceal things, and searching them out is honourable. But
Deuteronomy draws the line: revealed things are ours *so that we may do them*. The purpose of the
search is obedience. A numerical discovery that produces no obedience, and that no reader could reach
without a computer, has wandered out of what was revealed and into what belongs to the LORD.

## Symbolic numbers in Scripture

Distinct from gematria — which is about letters having values — is the plain observation that certain
numbers recur at meaningful moments. This needs no decoding; it is a pattern a careful reader notices,
the way one notices that "forty" keeps appearing at times of testing.

| Number | Common significance | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Unity — God's own oneness | "The LORD our God, the LORD is one" — Deuteronomy 6:4 |
| 2 | Testimony, witness | A matter established by two or three witnesses — Deuteronomy 19:15 |
| 3 | Divine completeness, resurrection | Jonah three days in the fish, which Jesus applies to his own resurrection — Jonah 1:17; 1 Corinthians 15:4 |
| 4 | Creation, the earth | Four rivers out of Eden — Genesis 2:10; four living creatures around the throne — Revelation 4:6-8 |
| 6 | Man, falling short | Man formed on the sixth day — Genesis 1:26-31; the beast's number, "the number of a man" — Revelation 13:18 |
| 7 | Completion, perfection, rest | God rests on the seventh day — Genesis 2:2-3; Israel's seven appointed feasts — Leviticus 23; the sevens structuring Revelation |
| 8 | New beginning | Circumcision on the eighth day — Genesis 17:12; eight people brought through the flood — 1 Peter 3:20 |
| 10 | Order, complete testimony | The Ten Commandments — Exodus 20 |
| 12 | Governmental completeness | The twelve tribes — Genesis 49; the twelve apostles — Matthew 10:2-4; the New Jerusalem's twelve gates and foundations — Revelation 21:12-14 |
| 40 | Testing, trial | Forty days of flood — Genesis 7:12; forty years in the wilderness — Numbers 14:33-34; Jesus fasting and tempted forty days — Matthew 4:2 |

Two cautions on using this table. First, these are observations about recurring usage, not a
dictionary — the number seven does not mean "perfection" every time it appears, and Scripture also
uses numbers to report plain quantities. Second, the direction of reasoning matters: notice the
pattern when the text puts it in front of you, rather than going looking for a number to make a
passage say something it does not.

Where a number carries real weight in a passage, this site tries to say so in the study of that
passage — see the [prophecy studies](../last-things/index.md), where sevens and forties recur — rather
than treating this table as a decoder ring.

## Discussion questions

1. Psalm 119's acrostic is invisible in English. What does it change about how you read the psalm to
   know that its form is a claim about the completeness of God's word?
2. Lamentations 5 keeps 22 verses but drops the alphabet. Why might a poet do that, and what does it
   suggest about the relationship between form and grief?
3. Irenaeus performed gematria on 666 and then refused to name anyone. What does his restraint model
   for handling prophecy today?
4. Daniel could not have decoded the writing on the wall by cleverness; he was given the meaning. How
   should that shape our expectations about "hidden" content in Scripture?
5. Matthew states his 14 × 3 structure openly. If a numerical pattern in Scripture is
   intended, would you expect the author to point at it? Are there reasons he might not?
6. Rule 6 above argues that a message reachable only by computer cannot be part of what Scripture was
   given to communicate. Is that argument sound? What would count against it?
