# Biblical numerology

Psalm 119 has 176 verses. It is 22 × 8: one stanza for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, eight
verses to a stanza, every verse in a stanza beginning with that stanza's letter. The poet built a
structure you cannot see in English and cannot miss in Hebrew, and he did it while writing about the
sufficiency of God's word. The form *is* the argument: God's instruction covers everything, A to Z.

That is biblical numerology at its most defensible: a pattern the author built, which a reader can
check letter by letter. At the other end of the spectrum is a software industry selling hidden
prophecies extracted from letter-skip sequences.

**In one sentence:** The numbers God put in Scripture were built and marked in plain view by its authors — acrostics you can check letter by letter, a genealogy that states its fourteens, a riddle God interpreted Himself — so you can read them with confidence and let them send you back to obeying what He has plainly revealed (Deuteronomy 29:29).

## Why numbers and letters are the same thing in Hebrew and Greek

Neither biblical Hebrew nor Koine Greek had a separate set of digit symbols. There is no Hebrew "7"
the way there is an English 7. Each language used its own alphabet to count: א is 1, ב is 2, and so
on up through ת at 400. The full value table lives on the
[Hebrew alphabet](../resources/hebrew-alphabet.md#numbers) page.

When a Hebrew writer wrote a number, he wrote letters, because that was the only way to write one.
Every Hebrew word therefore *has* a numeric total, in the same way every English word has a length.
Comparing those totals is called **gematria** (from Greek γεωμετρία, *geōmetria*), and the question
is when, if ever, Scripture intends you to do it.

The system shows in the text itself. Tischendorf's edition of the Greek New Testament prints the
number in Revelation 13:18 as three numeral-letters, χξϛ': *chi* (600) + *xi* (60) + *stigma* (6):

> ✝️ Revelation 13:18 (Tischendorf, Greek)
>
> 18 ὧδε ἡ σοφία ἐστίν· ὁ ἔχων νοῦν ψηφισάτω τὸν ἀριθμὸν τοῦ θηρίου, ἀριθμὸς γὰρ ἀνθρώπου ἐστίν· καὶ
> ὁ ἀριθμὸς αὐτοῦ χξϛ'.

## The acrostics: patterns you can verify

An acrostic poem runs the alphabet down the left margin. It cannot happen by accident, and it
survives in the consonantal text where anyone can check it.

Each entry in the table below comes from extracting the first consonant of each verse from the
Westminster Leningrad Codex and comparing the sequence against the 22-letter Hebrew alphabet.

| Passage | Structure | Verified result |
|---|---|---|
| Psalm 119 | 22 stanzas × 8 verses = 176 | Perfect. Every 8th verse steps to the next letter, א through ת |
| Lamentations 1 | 22 verses, one per letter | Perfect, standard alphabet order |
| Lamentations 2 | 22 verses | Complete, but the order **inverts**: <span dir="rtl">פ</span> precedes <span dir="rtl">ע</span> |
| Lamentations 3 | 66 verses = 22 × 3 | Complete triple acrostic — three consecutive verses per letter — with the same inversion |
| Lamentations 4 | 22 verses | Complete, same inversion |
| Lamentations 5 | 22 verses | **Not an acrostic at all** — verified by control test |
| Proverbs 31:10-31 | 22 verses | Perfect, א through ת |
| Psalms 111, 112 | 22 lines each, over 10 verses | Acrostic works per poetic line, not per verse |
| Psalms 25, 34 | 22 verses each | Irregular — see below |
| Psalms 9-10 | Spans both psalms | Broken and partial — see below |

### What the irregularities are doing

**Lamentations breaks its own form on purpose.** Chapters 1-4 are alphabetic. Chapter 5 has exactly
22 verses, the right number, and no acrostic. The poet keeps the count and lets the order go. A
book that has held catastrophe inside a disciplined form for four chapters ends by releasing the
form while keeping its shape.

The <span dir="rtl">פ</span>/<span dir="rtl">ע</span> inversion is consistent: chapter 1 uses the familiar order, and chapters 2, 3
and 4 all put <span dir="rtl">פ</span> before <span dir="rtl">ע</span>. Three chapters agree against one. Ancient Hebrew
abecedaries show that the order of these two letters varied before it settled.

**Psalms 25 and 34 share three irregularities.** In both, the superscription sits outside the
sequence, there is no waw verse, and an extra <span dir="rtl">פ</span> verse follows the <span dir="rtl">ת</span> verse that closes
the alphabet. Two psalms deviating in the same three ways points to a shared convention. (Psalm 25
also lacks a *qoph* line and has two *resh* lines.)

**Psalms 9 and 10** carry a single acrostic between them, running roughly א through כ in Psalm 9 and
picking up again in Psalm 10, too irregularly to set out in a table. This is one of the standard
arguments that they were originally one psalm, which is how the Septuagint and Vulgate count them.

### A caution from a case that fails

Nahum 1:2-8 is often listed as a partial acrostic. Tested the same way, by first consonant per verse
and again per half-line, it does not hold up. The proposed sequence only appears if you emend the
text to produce it. A test that can fail is what gives the passing results their weight.

## When the acrostic catches a missing line

Psalm 145 is an alphabetic acrostic with 21 verses, one short of the alphabet.

The check shows where the gap is: verse 13 begins with <span dir="rtl">מ</span> (*mem*), and verse 14
begins with <span dir="rtl">ס</span> (*samek*). The <span dir="rtl">נ</span> (*nun*) line is absent from the Masoretic Text.

The missing line survives elsewhere. The Septuagint (LXX Psalm 144:13) carries a clause the Hebrew
does not:

> ✝️ Psalm 144:13 LXX (Brenton, Greek — extra clause only)
>
> πιστὸς Κύριος ἐν τοῖς λόγοις αὐτοῦ, καὶ ὅσιος ἐν πᾶσι τοῖς ἔργοις αὐτοῦ.

"The LORD is faithful in all his words, and holy in all his works." In Hebrew that line would begin
<span dir="rtl">נֶאֱמָן</span> (*ne'eman*, H539, "faithful"), a נ word, in the slot the acrostic left empty. The Psalms
scroll from Qumran (11Q5) has it in Hebrew: <span dir="rtl">נאמן אלוהים בדבריו וחסיד בכול מעשיו</span>,
"God is faithful in his words and gracious in all his works." The CSB and NLT notes add the Syriac
as a further witness.

The line the Masoretic Text lost is a statement that God keeps His word. And He kept this one: the
acrostic marked the gap, and the Greek and the scroll held the words until a reader came looking.

### What the English versions do with it

| Version | Psalm 145:13 |
|---|---|
| Masoretic Text (WLC) | Absent |
| ASV | Absent |
| NASB 1995 | Absent |
| **ESV** | Present, **in square brackets** — "[The LORD is faithful in all his words and kind in all his works.]" |
| NIV | Present, unmarked |
| CSB | Present, unmarked |
| WEB | Present, unmarked |
| NASB 2020 | Present, unmarked — a reversal of NASB 1995 |

Each is a defensible call about a real textual problem. The ESV's brackets show the reader where
the decision was made. The NASB's change between its 1995 and 2020 editions is a translation
committee changing its mind in public.

The acrostic works as a checksum. The poet's numerical design is what makes a lost line detectable
three thousand years later.

## The one number Scripture asks us to calculate

> ✝️ Revelation 13:18 (ESV)
>
> 18 This calls for wisdom: let the one who has understanding calculate the number of the beast, for
> it is the number of a man, and his number is 666.

The verb is **ψηφίζω** (*psēphizō*, G5585), "to count, reckon up, calculate." It comes from ψῆφος
(*psēphos*, G5586), the small pebble used for tallying and for casting a vote: an ordinary word for
working a sum.

It appears twice in the Greek New Testament. The other occurrence is Luke 14:28, where a man
planning a tower first sits down and *counts the cost*. Both uses are about sober arithmetic done
before committing yourself. John asks for the same clear head Jesus asked of a would-be builder.

### The arithmetic, and why the variant matters

Written in Hebrew letters, "Neron Caesar" — <span dir="rtl">נרון קסר</span> — totals 666:

| נ | ר | ו | ן | ק | ס | ר |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 200 | 6 | 50 | 100 | 60 | 200 |

The Hebrew transliteration of the Greek word for "beast" (θηρίον, *thērion*), <span dir="rtl">תריון</span>, also
totals 666, and the ESV Study Bible notes it alongside the Nero calculation.

A textual variant strengthens the Nero reading. Some manuscripts of Revelation read **616**. The
Latin form of the same name, without the final nun, <span dir="rtl">נרו קסר</span>, totals 616. Drop one letter
of spelling and the number moves by 50, that letter's value. A scribe who knew the name in its Latin
spelling had a reason to produce that particular number.

The variant is old. Irenaeus, writing around AD 180, already knew it and called it a copyist's error:

> Some have erred following the ordinary mode of speech, and have vitiated the middle number in the
> name, deducting the amount of fifty from it.
>
> — Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.30.1

His own explanation was Greek. Numbers were written with letters, he says, so a copyist could
expand ξ (60) into ι (10). He says nothing of Nero or of a Hebrew nun; that explanation of the same
missing fifty came from modern scholars. What Irenaeus gives us is the witness that 616 was in
circulation within a century of John.

### The earliest commentator's warning

Irenaeus is the earliest surviving witness on this verse, and he claims direct access to the chain
of transmission. He says 666 stands "in all the most approved and ancient copies," with "those men
who saw John face to face bearing their testimony to it" (5.30.1). He then does gematria himself,
floating *Lateinos* and *Teitan* as candidate names. Having done it, he refuses to conclude:

> It is therefore more certain, and less hazardous, to await the fulfilment of the prophecy, than to
> be making surmises, and casting about for any names that may present themselves, inasmuch as many
> names can be found possessing the number mentioned.
>
> — Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.30.3

The method works, and *because* it works on too many names, it cannot by itself identify anyone.
Irenaeus also names the pastoral danger: someone certain of the wrong name will fail to recognise
the right man when he comes (5.30.1).

Read dispensationally, Irenaeus's instinct is sound. The number let first-century churches
recognise the imperial power then persecuting them, and it is still given to a future generation
who will face the world ruler the text expects. The ESV Study Bible's note reports the Nero
calculation and adds that many interpreters "expect a future, greater fulfillment in a world ruler
who is violently opposed to God and his people." The number will let God's people recognise that man
when he comes. It does not let anyone name him in advance.

### 666 as a symbol

Set the name calculations aside and the number still speaks on the plain sense of the text, because
Revelation has spent twelve chapters establishing seven as the number of completeness. Six falls
short of seven. Man was made on the sixth day; the seventh is God's rest. A number made entirely of
sixes, which John calls "the number of a man," is the creature straining after divine completeness
and missing by one, three times over. This reading needs no arithmetic and no candidate name, only
Revelation's own vocabulary.

## God's own numerical riddle — and who interprets it

In Daniel 5 God writes a riddle in weights on a palace wall, and **God supplies the
interpretation Himself.**

> ✝️ Daniel 5:25 (ESV)
>
> 25 And this is the writing that was inscribed: MENE, MENE, TEKEL, and PARSIN.

The words on the wall — <span dir="rtl">מְנֵא מְנֵא תְּקֵל וּפַרְסִין</span> (*mene mene teqel u-pharsin*) — are Aramaic
**weights**: a mina, a mina, a shekel, and half-minas. A row of currency denominations. Belshazzar's
wise men could read them and still could not say what they meant, because as a list of weights they
mean nothing.

Daniel re-points each weight-name as a passive verb from the same root:

- **MENE** — <span dir="rtl">מְנֵא</span>, a mina — read as <span dir="rtl">מְנָה</span> (*menah*, H4483):
  "God has numbered the days of your kingdom" (v26)
- **TEKEL** — <span dir="rtl">תְּקֵל</span>, a shekel — read as <span dir="rtl">תְּקִילְתָּה</span>
  (*teqiltah*, H8625): "you have been weighed in the balances and found wanting" (v27)
- **PARSIN** — <span dir="rtl">פַּרְסִין</span>, half-minas — read as
  <span dir="rtl">פְּרִיסַת</span> (*perisat*, H6537): "your kingdom is divided" (v28), then punned a second
  time against <span dir="rtl">פָּרָס</span> (*Paras*), Persia — "and given to the Medes and Persians"

Weights become verbs; a coin's name becomes an empire's name. Belshazzar could not read it. He
needed a prophet, and God gave the prophet the meaning.

God numbered Belshazzar's days, and then God told him what the numbers meant. Scripture contains
one clear divinely written numerical riddle, and the text explains it within three verses. Where God
hides something in a number and wants it known, He sends someone to say what it means.

## The responsible use of gematria: Matthew's genealogy

> ✝️ Matthew 1:17 (ESV)
>
> 17 So all the generations from Abraham to David were fourteen generations, and from David to the
> deportation to Babylon fourteen generations, and from the deportation to Babylon to the Christ
> fourteen generations.

**What is certain:** Matthew built his genealogy on a numerical structure, and he says so. He states
the 14 × 3 pattern in the text. He also achieved it by omission — comparing his list with 1 Chronicles
3:10-12 shows he skipped three generations between Joram and Uzziah. Ancient genealogies routinely
selected, so the fourteens are chosen, and Matthew is content for you to know it.

**What is inference:** the usual explanation is that <span dir="rtl">דוד</span>, David's name, totals 14 (<span dir="rtl">ד</span> = 4, <span dir="rtl">ו</span> = 6, <span dir="rtl">ד</span> = 4), and that David is the 14th name in the list. The arithmetic is correct. Matthew never
says it, and commentators divide over it. The *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* notes that some
scholars attribute the correspondence to coincidence, and that Matthew's three sets do not contain
equal numbers of names once you count them.

The structure Matthew *states* is exegesis. The gematria explaining *why fourteen* reconstructs an
ancient author's motive, so it is inference: plausible, and too light to build doctrine on.

## Where this goes wrong

### Equidistant letter sequences and the "Bible code"

The claim, popularised by Michael Drosnin's 1997 bestseller and given academic footing by an earlier
paper, is that skipping every *n*th letter through the Hebrew text of Genesis reveals words and names
encoding future events. Witztum, Rips and Rosenberg published a statistical case in *Statistical
Science* in 1994, reporting that the names of famous rabbis appeared in close proximity to their birth
and death dates far more often than chance allowed.

It did not survive scrutiny. In 1999 the same journal published "Solving the Bible Code Puzzle" by
Brendan McKay, Dror Bar-Natan, Maya Bar-Hillel and Gil Kalai. They found the result was extremely
sensitive to how the rabbis' names and appellations were listed, with enough latitude that a list
could be tuned, consciously or not, toward a positive result. The same method applied to a Hebrew
translation of *War and Peace* produced comparable "codes."

#### Which letter sequence: ketiv, qere and the Samaritan Pentateuch

**The statistics never reach a deeper problem.** ELS requires a fixed, exact letter sequence. Insert
or remove a single letter and every skip-count downstream shifts, and every "code" past that point
dissolves. No such single sequence exists.

Two respected digital editions of the Westminster Leningrad Codex, *the same manuscript*, differ in
their consonants at 16 places in Genesis alone. They fall at the *ketiv/qere* points, where the
Masoretic tradition transmits a word in two forms at once: the consonants as written (*ketiv*) and
the word as it is to be read aloud (*qere*). At Genesis 8:17 the text as written is <span dir="rtl">הוצא</span>; the word to be read is <span dir="rtl">הַיְצֵא</span>. One edition
prints only the written form, the other prints both. Both represent the codex faithfully.

Against the Samaritan Pentateuch, the consonantal text of Genesis differs in roughly 1,700 to 1,800
places, depending on which Masoretic edition you compare. About three quarters are a single
character, and the commonest by far are an inserted <span dir="rtl">ו</span> (about 350 times) or <span dir="rtl">י</span> (about 180),
the *matres lectionis*, consonants doing duty as vowel markers. This is spelling variation. It
changes nothing about what Genesis *says*, and it breaks every skip-distance in an ELS search.

So the code hunter must first decide which letter sequence is the real one. Scripture never
identifies one, and the doctrine of inspiration has never been a claim about a spelling convention.
A method whose results depend on an editorial decision the Bible never makes is reading the editor.

### Heptadic structures

Ivan Panin (1855-1942) claimed to have found thousands of patterns of seven throughout the Greek New
Testament — sevens in word counts, letter counts, vocabulary totals — and argued they proved divine
authorship beyond any possibility of human composition.

The weakness is in the method. Panin worked from a text form he selected himself, at a time when
the Greek text was actively contested. And the things one *could* count (words, letters, vocabulary
items, words beginning with a vowel, nouns, words occurring only once) are effectively unbounded.
Given enough countable features and freedom over where a passage begins and ends, sevens will
appear. Without a rule fixing what will be counted before you look, a hit means very little.

**Genesis 1:1 shows it.** The verse contains seven words and 28 letters (7 × 4) in the Masoretic
text:

| # | Word | Consonants |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | בראשית | 6 |
| 2 | ברא | 3 |
| 3 | אלהים | 5 |
| 4 | את | 2 |
| 5 | השמים | 5 |
| 6 | ואת | 3 |
| 7 | הארץ | 4 |
| | **Total** | **28** |

But "seven words" depends on the Masoretic convention of attaching prefixed prepositions and
articles to the following word. The MACULA linguistic database segments the same verse into
**eleven** morphemes, because it counts <span dir="rtl">בְּ</span> ("in") and <span dir="rtl">הַ</span> ("the") separately. Both counts are
defensible. The verse is "seven" or "eleven" depending on a decision about word division that has
nothing to do with inspiration.

Psalm 119's 22 × 8 has no such problem: the alphabet fixes the unit in advance, and the poet chose
it.

## A method for reading numbers in Scripture

1. **Prefer patterns the author built and marked.** Psalm 119's acrostic and Matthew's stated
   fourteens stand on firmer ground than anything that needs a technique to extract.
2. **Ask what unit is being counted, and who chose it.** If the pattern needs a particular counting
   convention, the pattern belongs partly to whoever chose the convention.
3. **Require the interpretation to come from Scripture.** Daniel 5 is the model: God gives a numerical
   riddle and then gives its meaning. A number whose significance is supplied entirely by the
   interpreter is the interpreter's.
4. **Test your method on something that should fail.** Nahum 1 fails the acrostic test, and that is
   what gives the passing results their weight.
5. **Never let a number carry doctrine alone.** Numbers reinforce meaning stated plainly elsewhere.
   If a numerical argument is the only support for a claim, the claim is unsupported.
6. **Check the reading against 2 Timothy 3:16-17.** The Scripture that is God-breathed and sufficient
   to equip "for every good work" is the text as it can be read. A message reachable only by
   computation is one the ordinary reader, and every reader before computers, could not have had.

Proverbs 25:2 invites investigation: God does conceal things, and searching them out is honourable.
Deuteronomy 29:29 draws the line: revealed things are ours *so that we may do them*. God has spoken
plainly so that you can obey Him. A numerical discovery that produces no obedience, and that no
reader could reach without a computer, has wandered out of what was revealed and into what belongs
to the LORD.

## Symbolic numbers in Scripture

Gematria is about letters having values. Separately, certain numbers recur at meaningful moments,
and a careful reader notices them the way one notices that "forty" keeps appearing at times of
testing.

| Number | Common significance | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Unity — God's own oneness | "The LORD our God, the LORD is one" — Deuteronomy 6:4 |
| 2 | Testimony, witness | A matter established by two or three witnesses — Deuteronomy 19:15 |
| 3 | Divine completeness, resurrection | Jonah three days in the fish, which Jesus applies to His own death and resurrection — Jonah 1:17; Matthew 12:40; raised "on the third day" — 1 Corinthians 15:4 |
| 4 | Creation, the earth | Four rivers out of Eden — Genesis 2:10; four living creatures around the throne — Revelation 4:6-8 |
| 6 | Man, falling short | Man formed on the sixth day — Genesis 1:26-31; the beast's number, "the number of a man" — Revelation 13:18 |
| 7 | Completion, perfection, rest | God rests on the seventh day — Genesis 2:2-3; Israel's seven appointed feasts — Leviticus 23; the sevens structuring Revelation |
| 8 | New beginning | Circumcision on the eighth day — Genesis 17:12; eight people brought through the flood — 1 Peter 3:20 |
| 10 | Order, complete testimony | The Ten Commandments — Exodus 20 |
| 12 | Governmental completeness | The twelve tribes — Genesis 49; the twelve apostles — Matthew 10:2-4; the New Jerusalem's twelve gates and foundations — Revelation 21:12-14 |
| 40 | Testing, trial | Forty days of flood — Genesis 7:12; forty years in the wilderness — Numbers 14:33-34; Jesus fasting and tempted forty days — Matthew 4:2 |

Two cautions on using this table. First, these are observations about recurring usage. Seven does
not mean "perfection" every time it appears, and Scripture also uses numbers to report plain
quantities. Second, notice the pattern when the text puts it in front of you; do not go looking for
a number to make a passage say something it does not.

Where a number carries real weight in a passage, the study of that passage says so. The
[prophecy studies](../last-things/index.md) are where sevens and forties recur most.

3. Irenaeus performed gematria on 666 and then refused to name anyone. What does his restraint model
   for handling prophecy today?
4. Daniel could not have decoded the writing on the wall by cleverness; he was given the meaning. How
   should that shape our expectations about "hidden" content in Scripture?
5. Matthew states his 14 × 3 structure openly. If a numerical pattern in Scripture is
   intended, would you expect the author to point at it? Are there reasons he might not?
6. Psalm 145's missing line says "The LORD is faithful in all his words." What does it mean to you
   that God preserved that sentence through a gap in the Hebrew text?
