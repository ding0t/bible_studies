# What Literary Criticism Declares: Many Writers, One Story

For two hundred years literary criticism has read the Bible the way a geologist reads a cliff: layer
by layer, source by source, hand by hand. It took Moses' five books apart into sources finished in
the fifth century BC. It gave Isaiah three authors and moved Daniel four centuries later. It said
Paul did not write three of the letters that carry his name. Each step multiplied the writers.

Scripture had already said there were many. "Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke
to our fathers by the prophets" (Hebrews 1:1, ESV). This study takes the critics' count at its
word, measures what those many writers produced, and asks what the result declares.

**In one sentence:** By literary criticism's own count the Bible is the work of many writers across
more than eight centuries, and the measurements show one connected story whose prophecies were
written, and survive in copies, before the events they name, which is what Scripture says of
itself: God spoke "at many times and in many ways", declaring the end from the beginning, and has
now spoken to us by His Son.

## Part one: what Scripture says

### Many times, many ways, one Speaker

Hebrews opens with two Greek words found nowhere else in the New Testament: πολυμερῶς
(*polymerōs*, pol-oo-meh-ROCE, G4181), "in many parts", and πολυτρόπως (*polytropōs*,
pol-oo-TROP-oce, G4187), "in many ways". The *ESV Study Bible* notes that "prophets" here takes in
the writers of the historical books as well, Moses and David among them (note on Hebrews 1:1).

The sentence has one subject. "God spoke." The parts were many and the ways were many: law,
history, song, proverb, vision. The Speaker was one, and the sentence runs on to its goal: "in these
last days he has spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:2, ESV). This shows that God has been saying
one thing through every writer, and that the thing He was saying is His Son.

### Writers who did not see the whole

The writers themselves did not always know where their words were going. Peter says the prophets
"searched and inquired carefully, inquiring what person or time the Spirit of Christ in them was
indicating when he predicted the sufferings of Christ and the subsequent glories" (1 Peter 1:10-11,
ESV). The verbs are ἐξεζήτησαν (*exezētēsan*, G1567), "sought out", and ἐξηραύνησαν
(*exēraunēsan*, G1830), "searched out". They studied their own prophecies. "It was revealed to them
that they were serving not themselves but you" (1 Peter 1:12, ESV).

Peter gives the reason in his second letter: "no prophecy was ever produced by the will of man, but
men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (2 Peter 1:21, ESV). That is
inspiration, and it explains a story whose writers could not have planned it between them. Paul
names it in one word: "All Scripture is breathed out by God" (2 Timothy 3:16, ESV).

### Declaring the end from the beginning

Isaiah 40-48 puts the LORD on trial against the gods of Babylon, and the test is prediction:

> ✝️ Isaiah 41:22-23 (ESV)
>
> 22 Let them bring them, and tell us what is to happen. Tell us the former things, what they are,
> that we may consider them, that we may know their outcome; or declare to us the things to come.
> 23 Tell us what is to come hereafter, that we may know that you are gods; do good, or do harm,
> that we may be dismayed and terrified.

The LORD answers with His own claim: "I am God, and there is none like me, declaring the end from
the beginning and from ancient times things not yet done" (Isaiah 46:9-10, ESV). "Declaring" is
<span dir="rtl">מַגִּיד</span> (*maggid*), from <span dir="rtl">נָגַד</span> (*nagad*, nah-GAHD,
H5046), "to tell, make known". Isaiah uses the verb 32 times. It is the word this series is named
for.

He gives the reason He spoke early: "I declared them to you from of old, before they came to pass I
announced them to you, lest you should say, 'My idol did them'" (Isaiah 48:5, ESV). The law gave
Israel the same test for any prophet: "if the word does not come to pass or come true, that is a
word that the LORD has not spoken" (Deuteronomy 18:22, ESV). Scripture invites the test that Part
four applies.

### One story, about one person

On the road to Emmaus, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the
Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27, ESV). That evening He named the three parts
of the Hebrew Bible: "everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the
Psalms must be fulfilled" (Luke 24:44, ESV). Jesus claimed the whole as one book about Him.
[What the Scripture Links Reveal](what-the-links-reveal.md) measured how the New Testament quotes
the Old. This study measures the claim behind it.

## Part two: what literary criticism says

### The method

Literary criticism, in the sense used here, is the study of how a biblical book came to be:
its sources, the forms its material took, and the editors who put it together. The *ESV Study
Bible*'s introductions report its main conclusions where they differ from the traditional view:

- **The Pentateuch** was "composed over a long period of time" and "reached their final form in the
  fifth century B.C." (introduction to Genesis). Deuteronomy belongs to the time of King Josiah or
  the exile.
- **Isaiah** is three books: First Isaiah (chapters 1-39) from the late eighth century, Second Isaiah
  (40-55) from the middle of the sixth, Third Isaiah (56-66) from the fifth.
- **Daniel** is "a second-century document" from the persecution under Antiochus IV (175-164 BC).
- **Several letters** were written in an apostle's name by a later hand: the Pastoral Letters,
  Ephesians, 2 Thessalonians and 1 Peter, by the reckoning of "many" or "some" scholars.

The same introductions argue for the traditional view in each case, and the study notes that "in
recent decades … scholars have become increasingly uncertain" about the Pentateuch's late sources
(introduction to Genesis). This study leaves those questions open. It takes the critical view at
its strongest and runs every measure on its dates as well.

### What its own count gives

The traditional view gives the Bible 38 writers: 27 named, 11 anonymous. The critical view gives
44, because Isaiah becomes three, Daniel passes to an unknown writer of the second century, and six
letters pass to four unnamed writers. The critical view adds writers. It removes none.

On dates, the critical view itself places First Isaiah in the late eighth century BC and its latest
New Testament date, for 1 Peter, under Trajan (to AD 117). Even using only the dates the critics
state, the writing spans at least **856 years**. On the traditional dates, from Moses to John, it
spans **1,545 years** (1446 BC to AD 100).

## Part three: what the measurements say

### Many hands

Every number here comes from a table of all 66 books, built for this study from the *ESV Study
Bible* and *CSB Ancient Faith Study Bible* introductions (the Annex has the details).

| | Traditional view | Critical view |
|---|---|---|
| Writers | 38 (27 named) | 44 (26 named) |
| Span of writing | 1,545 years | at least 856 years |
| Languages | Hebrew (39 books), Greek (27), Aramaic portions | the same |
| Places of writing | Asia, Europe, Africa | the same |

The Aramaic is counted from the Hebrew text's own tagging: 2,172 words of Daniel (2:4b-7:28), 1,149
of Ezra (4:8-6:18; 7:12-26), one verse of Jeremiah (10:11) and two words of Genesis (31:47). Africa
is Egypt, where Jeremiah gave his last oracles at Tahpanhes (Jeremiah 43:8-44:30). Europe is
Corinth, Macedonia and Rome, where Paul wrote nine of his thirteen letters and Peter wrote both of his.

The writers were a shepherd (Amos 7:14), kings, priests, a tax collector and fishermen. Several
never met another. None had the finished book.

### One web

OpenBible.info's cross-references record which verses readers find speak to each other, each with a
count of the people who endorsed it. Taking only links at least ten people endorsed, there are
**72,094** between different books.

- **Every book is joined to the whole.** The 66 books form one connected web. 1,431 of the 2,145
  possible pairs of books are linked directly; any book reaches any other in 1.33 steps on average.
  The least connected book, Philemon, still links to six others.
- **Every writer is joined to every other.** 519 of the 703 possible pairs of writers are linked
  directly, and the rest within two steps. On the critical view's 44 writers the figure is 697 of
  946, the same 74%.
- **The links cross between writers.** 86% join one writer to another (91% on the critical view).

The chart draws each of those links as an arc between the dates its two books were written. Red
arcs span a thousand years or more. On the critical view's dates the arcs shorten, and the web
holds.

|---|---|---|

| Quotations reaching back more than 1,000 years | 45% | 16% |

A writer who quotes an earlier one chooses to. What this measures is the length of the reach: New
Testament writers took their words from books written six to ten centuries before them, and found
those books saying what they had seen happen.

### The two ends meet

The Bible's first three chapters and its last three were written, on any dating, many centuries
apart. Readers have connected them 50 times on OpenBible, with 614 endorsements between them. The
wording shows why. Comparing the Greek of Genesis 1-3 (the Septuagint) with the Greek of
Revelation 20-22, these words occur in both, though each is rare in the New Testament:

|---|---|---|---|---|

| ποταμός (*potamos*, G4215) | river | 2:10, 13 | 22:1, 2 | 17 |
| ὄφις (*ophis*, G3789) | serpent | 3:1, 13 | 20:2 | 14 |
| πηγή (*pēgē*, G4077) | spring | 2:6 | 21:6 | 11 |
| χρυσίον (*chrysion*, G5553) | gold | 2:11, 12 | 21:18, 21 | 12 |
| φύλλον (*phyllon*, G5444) | leaf | 3:7 | 22:2 | 6 |
| φωστήρ (*phōstēr*, G5458) | light, radiance | 1:14, 16 | 21:11 | 2 |

The story opens with a river flowing out of Eden and "the tree of life … in the midst of the garden"
(Genesis 2:9-10, ESV). It closes with "the river of the water of life … flowing from the throne of
God and of the Lamb" and, on either side, "the tree of life" (Revelation 22:1-2, ESV). Between
them stands the curse: "cursed is the ground because of you" (Genesis 3:17, ESV). At the end, "No
longer will there be anything accursed" (Revelation 22:3, ESV), and "that ancient serpent" is bound
(Revelation 20:2, ESV). This shows that God finishes what He begins. The garden man was driven from,
guarded "to guard the way to the tree of life" (Genesis 3:24, ESV), is open again at the end, through
the Lamb.

### Different voices

Literary critics use a writer's habits with small words, "and", "for", "the", "not", to tell writers
apart, because those habits are hard to imitate. The same method, applied here to the 60 most common
words in each Testament, confirms that the Bible's writers are different people:

- In the New Testament, a 1,500-word passage's nearest match in another book is by the same writer
  44% of the time, against 17% by chance.
- In the Old Testament, with 2,500-word passages, the figure is 35% against 10%.

The method detects writers. The Bible's many hands are real, and the critics were right to see them.

The same measure, applied to Isaiah, does not divide it. The distance between Isaiah 1-39 and 40-66
is 0.71. The two halves of Exodus are 1.14 apart, of 2 Kings 1.12, of 1 Chronicles 0.97 and of 1
Samuel 0.77. Isaiah's halves sit in the middle of ordinary single books (fifth of thirteen splits
measured), and well below the 1.14 typical between two different books. This does not prove Isaiah
wrote both halves: a measure of common words is blunt, and genre moves it. It does show that on this
evidence the case for dividing Isaiah rests on its content, chiefly its predictions, and its style
gives that case nothing.

### A control: books outside the canon

The books outside the 66 that the Septuagint carries (Wisdom, Sirach, Tobit, Judith, the Maccabees
and others) come from the same people and the same centuries. If later writers simply quoted
whatever Jewish books they knew, the New Testament should reach them about as often.

Run the same test both ways, a run of five words in the same order with at least three content
words, and the New Testament reaches 101 of the 22,310 verses of the canonical Septuagint (4.5 per
thousand) and 6 of the 6,311 verses outside it (1.0 per thousand), all in 2 and 4 Maccabees. The
New Testament writers quote the books that are in the canon about four and a half times as densely
as the books from the same era that are not.

## Part four: prophecy, a test of its own

### The test

Cross-references show connection. Prophecy can show something the critics' model cannot absorb: a
text that names an event before it happens. The test has three dates.

1. **When it was written**, on the traditional view.
2. **The latest date any critic gives it.** This is the date chosen to make the prophecy as late as
   possible. Critics place Daniel in 167-164 BC and Isaiah 40-55 in the middle of the sixth century.
3. **The oldest surviving witness to the text**, a date no theory of authorship can move. The
   prologue to Sirach, written "in the thirty-eighth year of King Euergetes" (about 132 BC), says
   that "the law itself, and the prophecies, and the rest of the books" were already read in Greek
   (Sirach, prologue; this study's translation of Brenton's Greek, αὐτὸς ὁ νόμος, καὶ αἱ προφητεῖαι,
   καὶ τὰ λοιπὰ τῶν βιβλίων). Ben Sira himself, a generation or two earlier, names "the
   twelve prophets" as a set (Sirach 49:10). The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran was copied about
   125 BC, and every Qumran scroll was hidden before the site fell in AD 68.

Twenty prophecies that Scripture itself says were fulfilled go through all three. One more is set
aside before the test begins: 1 Kings 13:2 names Josiah three centuries ahead, but the book that
records it was finished after Josiah lived, so it cannot count here.

All thirteen were written before the event on the critics' own dates. All thirteen were in Greek
before Jesus was born, on the prologue's witness. Three can be held in the hand: the Great Isaiah
Scroll contains Isaiah 7:14, 9:1-2 and the whole of Isaiah 53, copied more than a century and a half
before the cross. "He was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities" (Isaiah
53:5, ESV) was on that scroll in Qumran's caves when Jesus was born.

Three readings are contested, and the case on each side belongs on the page.

- **Isaiah 7:14.** <span dir="rtl">עַלְמָה</span> (*almah*, H5959) means a young woman of marriageable
  age; the Septuagint, in Greek before Jesus was born, renders it παρθένος (*parthenos*, G3933),
  "virgin", the word Matthew quotes (Matthew 1:23). Those who read Isaiah 7:14 of a birth in Ahaz's own day point to the sign's
  timing in 7:15-16; those who read it of Jesus point to 9:6-7, where the child carries the names of
  God.
- **Psalm 22:16.** The Masoretic Text reads <span dir="rtl">כָּאֲרִי</span> (*ka'ari*), "like a
  lion", my hands and feet. The Septuagint reads ὤρυξαν, "they dug", and a psalms scroll from Nahal
  Hever reads a verb from <span dir="rtl">כרה</span>, "to dig", with five words of the verse
  surviving. The ESV, NASB, CSB and NIV all print "pierced". No New Testament writer quotes this
  line, so the table counts it last.
- **Daniel 9:25-26.** The text sets out weeks of years, but where they begin and how they are counted
  is disputed. The site's chronology follows Harold Hoehner, counting from Artaxerxes' decree in
  444 BC to AD 33; others count from 538 or 457 BC. Even on the critics' date of 164 BC, the anointed
  one is "cut off" two centuries later.

This group is where the critics' dates do their work. Second Isaiah, on their reckoning, wrote as
Cyrus was rising, two years before his decree. Daniel, on theirs, wrote after Alexander. That is the
reason for those dates: the ESV Study Bible's introduction to Daniel notes that the late date is
argued "because of its detailed prophecies" of the second century. A prophecy fulfilled before its
oldest surviving copy cannot settle the argument, because the date of writing is the thing in
dispute.

Micah 3:12 stands on any date. Jeremiah 26:18 quotes it by name, a century after Micah and before
Jerusalem fell, so a second book attests the prophecy before the event. The Medes are named against
Babylon in Isaiah 13, inside First Isaiah, which the critical view describes as "largely the work
of Isaiah" (introduction to Isaiah, *ESV Study Bible*); on that view it stands too, though "largely"
leaves room to date this oracle later.

### After the Old Testament closed

Daniel 9:26 says that after the anointed one is cut off, "the people of the prince who is to come
shall destroy the city and the sanctuary" (ESV). On the critics' own date of 164 BC that is 234
years before Rome burned the temple in AD 70. The reading is contested: critics take the verse of
the high priest Onias III, deposed in 171 BC and later killed (2 Maccabees 4:33-34), and of
Antiochus IV. Antiochus desecrated the temple;
he destroyed neither the city nor the sanctuary. Jesus applied Daniel's desolation to Jerusalem's
coming fall (Luke 21:20-24; Matthew 24:15).

Jesus' own word, "There will not be left here one stone upon another" (Mark 13:2, ESV), was fulfilled
in AD 70 too. It cannot count against a critic, because critics who date the Gospels after AD 70
do so partly because of it (introduction to Matthew, *ESV Study Bible*). It is listed and not
counted.

Israel's return to the land in 1948 is left out of the test. Ezekiel 36:24 promises "I will take
you from the nations and gather you from all the countries and bring you into your own land"
(ESV), and the date it was written is not in doubt. What no text supplies is the year of its
fulfilment. [Israel's Regathering and Refining](../israel-and-church/israels-regathering-and-refining.md#about-1948)
weighs 1948 as a stage toward that promise and gives the views on both sides.

### What the test shows

The test asks one thing: was it written down, and does it survive, before it happened? For the
thirteen prophecies of Jesus, yes, on every date anyone gives, and for three of them a manuscript
you can stand in front of says so. This shows that God does what He said in Isaiah 46:10. He
declared the end from the beginning, and He declared it in public, in writing, centuries ahead, so
that no one could say "My idol did them".

## Where this argument is weak

- **Writers chose to quote.** The New Testament's reach back into the Old is deliberate. Part three's
  quotation figures measure how far the later writers reached, and the prophecy test is the measure
  that does not depend on their choices.
- **The canon was chosen.** The church kept the books that fit. The control in Part three tests
  this, and the canon wins by about four and a half to one, but the numbers are small (101 verses
  against 6) and a stricter or looser test would move them.
- **Editors explain some unity.** A redactor can make one book consistent. No editor joined Genesis
  to Revelation, and no editor could place Isaiah 53 in a scroll before the events it describes.
- **Fulfilment is the New Testament's claim.** A critic can say the Gospel writers shaped their
  stories to match the prophecies. Several of these events were in other hands: Jesus did not choose
  where He was born, the priests set the price of thirty pieces of silver, and Roman soldiers cast
  lots for His clothes and left His legs unbroken (John 19:23-24, 32-36).
- **No number measures a miracle.** This study gives no odds. Probabilities multiplied across
  prophecies assume the prophecies are independent and that only fulfilled ones were counted, and
  neither holds. What can be measured is structure and dates, and the inference to an Author is a
  conclusion from them, which Part five draws.

## Part five: what literary criticism declares

Literary criticism set out to show that the Bible came from many hands, and it did. It found more
writers than the church had counted, writing in three languages on three continents, across more
than eight centuries on the critics' own dates. Its own tools confirm that those writers wrote
differently.

Then the measurements show what those writers made. Their books form one web in which every writer
is joined to every other. The story ends where it began, with a river and the tree of life, the curse
lifted and the serpent bound. Its prophecies of Jesus were written before He came, translated into
Greek before He was born, and copied onto a scroll that still survives.

Many writers who did not know each other, did not see the whole and did not finish the story could
not have planned this between them. Scripture names the One who did: "God spoke to our fathers by
the prophets" (Hebrews 1:1, ESV), and "men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy
Spirit" (2 Peter 1:21, ESV). This is the doctrine of inspiration, and the data fit it. One Author
wrote through many hands, and He wrote about His Son.

So you may read the Bible as one book. When you read Isaiah, you are reading about the same Jesus
the Gospels show you. When you wonder whether God will keep His word to you, look at the record: He
declared the end from the beginning, and He has never yet failed to do what He said.

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** Hebrews 1:1 uses πολυμερῶς and πολυτρόπως, "many parts" and "many ways", words
   found nowhere else in the New Testament. What does it add that the writer stresses the variety of
   God's speaking before he names the Son?
2. **The text in its context.** Isaiah 41-48 sets the LORD against Babylon's gods, and the test is
   prediction (Isaiah 41:22-23; 48:5). Why would that test matter to exiles living among those gods?
3. **Christ.** Jesus said that "everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and
   the Psalms must be fulfilled" (Luke 24:44, ESV). Which Old Testament passage would you most like
   to have heard Him explain on the road to Emmaus, and why?
4. **The hard part.** Critics date Daniel to 164 BC partly because its prophecies of the second
   century are so exact (Daniel 8:20-22). How much weight should a dating argument carry when it
   rests partly on the view that detailed prediction cannot happen?
5. **The text in its context.** Peter says the prophets "searched and inquired carefully" about what their own
   words meant (1 Peter 1:10-11, ESV). What does that tell you about how much one writer of
   Scripture understood of the whole?
6. **Be transformed.** God declared His acts "before they came to pass … lest you should say, 'My
   idol did them'" (Isaiah 48:5, ESV). What promise of His are you finding it hard to trust this
   week, and what in His record would you set against your doubt?

## Annex: how the measurements were made

- **Authorship table.** `docs/data/book-authorship.toml` gives each book's writer, date, place and
  language, with the traditional date from the *ESV Study Bible* introductions (book and section
  introductions; Psalms, Song of Songs and Revelation from its section introductions, with the *CSB
  Ancient Faith Study Bible* for Psalms' date range). A critical date is entered only where the
  source states one. Anonymous books count as one writer each, the choice that gives fewer writers.
- **Cross-references.** OpenBible.info, 865,898 links, endorsed by at least ten readers for the
  network figures. Until 2026-11-03 the site's copy had silently dropped Revelation's 35,032 links;
  the figures here include them.
- **Quotations.** The site's derived links (`quotation-greek`, 5,407 links): Greek New Testament
  against the Septuagint, same words in the same language.
- **Bookends.** Septuagint lemmas (Open Scriptures) for Genesis 1-3 against MACULA Greek lemmas for
  Revelation 20-22; New Testament counts from MACULA.
- **Style.** Burrows' Delta on the 60 most common lemmas per Testament (MACULA Greek and Hebrew),
  in 1,500-word (Greek) and 2,500-word (Hebrew) passages. Isaiah's halves are compared with the
  halves of twelve other long books.
- **Control.** Five-lemma runs with three or more content words, NT against the Septuagint, canon and
  non-canon scored by the same rule. Duplicate recensions (Old Greek Daniel, Tobit's second text)
  are left out.
- **Prophecy.** `docs/data/fulfilled-prophecy.toml`; fulfilment years from the site's chronology
  (`docs/data/chronology.json`); Qumran copies from the Dead Sea Scrolls morphology (caves 1-11
  only). The Great Isaiah Scroll's date, about 125 BC, is the one recorded on
  [Ancient Texts and Manuscripts](ancient-texts-manuscripts.md).
- **Re-running.** `uv run python unity_measures.py` in `references/build/` writes every figure to
  `docs/data/unity-measures.json`.
