# How to read the Bible

The single most useful idea I know for reading Scripture well comes from Gordon Fee and Douglas Stuart's *How to Read the Bible for All Its Worth*: every passage has two separate questions attached to it, and they have to be asked in order.

1. **Exegesis** — what did this mean to the people who first read or heard it?
2. **Hermeneutics** — what does it mean for us, now?

Almost every bad reading of the Bible I've come across skipped straight to question 2. The fix isn't complicated, just disciplined: don't answer "what does this mean for me" until you've answered "what did this mean to them." A verse ripped straight out of its own moment and applied directly to yours will usually mean something the author never intended — sometimes harmlessly, sometimes not.

## Ask these questions, in order

**1. What kind of writing is this?**
A psalm, a letter, a law, a parable, and a vision are read differently, on purpose — see [Reading by genre](#reading-by-genre) below. Knowing the genre before you start tells you what kind of questions are even fair to ask of the text.

**2. Who wrote it, to whom, and why?**
The historical occasion — author, audience, date, and the actual circumstance that called the passage into being. A letter answering a specific controversy in a specific church reads differently once you know what the controversy was.

**3. What comes immediately before and after?**
Don't exegete a verse in isolation from its paragraph. Ask what the author's own train of thought is — what's the point of this unit, in the author's own argument, not a point you already had in mind before you opened the book.

**4. What do the words actually say?**
Most of the time the words are plain enough in translation. When a specific word is doing real theological work, or two good translations render it noticeably differently, check the underlying Hebrew or Greek instead of guessing — see [Hebrew Alphabet](../resources/hebrew-alphabet.md) for a start on the original languages this site leans on.

**5. What's tied to that moment, and what carries over?**
Some things in a passage are bound to its original cultural setting; some things transcend it. This is the actual hinge between exegesis and hermeneutics — get honest about which is which before you draw a principle out of the text.

**6. Does this hold up elsewhere in Scripture?**
Don't build a doctrine on one obscure narrative detail or an incidental remark. A narrative usually *illustrates* a principle taught plainly somewhere else; it rarely *establishes* one by itself. If you can't find the principle stated directly anywhere else, hold the conclusion loosely.

## Beware eisegesis

**Eisegesis is reading into a passage a meaning its author never put there.** The name is built as the mirror of exegesis. *Exegesis* comes from ἐξηγέομαι (*exēgeomai*, ex-ay-GEH-oh-my, G1834), "to lead out" or "to tell", the verb John uses of Jesus: "he has made him known" (John 1:18, ESV). *Eisegesis* swaps the prefix for εἰς (*eis*), "into". The word was coined by later scholarship. The practice it names is in Scripture, and Scripture warns against it.

### How to recognise it

Eisegesis rarely feels like eisegesis from the inside. It feels like finding something. These are the signs to check for:

- **The conclusion came first.** You opened the text looking for support for something you already held, and found it. Ask whether you could have reached this meaning if nobody had told you to look for it.
- **The verse only works on its own.** Put the paragraph back around it (question 3 above). If the meaning falls apart, it came from you.
- **The first readers could not have understood it.** A reading that depends on a modern invention, a modern event or an English pun was closed to the church at Corinth and the exiles in Babylon. Question 2 catches this.
- **One word carries the whole doctrine.** A single word, loaded with a dictionary definition or an etymology the author was not using, is asked to hold more than its sentence gives it (question 4).
- **The reading suits the reader.** When an interpretation licenses what you already wanted to do, or rebukes only other people, give it a second look. Paul warned of hearers who gather teachers "to suit their own passions" (2 Timothy 4:3, ESV).
- **The rest of Scripture never says it.** A doctrine found in one verse and contradicted by the plain teaching of others has failed question 6.

### Scripture's own example: the temptation

Satan quoted the Bible to Jesus, and quoted it accurately.

> ✝️ Matthew 4:6-7 (ESV)
>
> "If you are the Son of God, throw yourself down, for it is written, 'He will command his angels concerning you,' and 'On their hands they will bear you up, lest you strike your foot against a stone.'" Jesus said to him, "Again it is written, 'You shall not put the Lord your God to the test.'"

Every word from Psalm 91:11-12 was correct. The meaning was Satan's. Psalm 91 promises God's keeping to the one who "dwells in the shelter of the Most High" (Psalm 91:1, ESV), and Satan used it as a dare to force God's hand. Jesus did not dispute the quotation. He set it beside Deuteronomy 6:16, letting Scripture govern how Scripture is read. That is question 6, and your Lord Jesus used it first.

### Why it is dangerous

**It puts your voice where God's belongs.** Peter grounds the authority of Scripture in where it came from:

> ✝️ 2 Peter 1:20-21 (ESV)
>
> no prophecy of Scripture comes from someone's own interpretation. For no prophecy was ever produced by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit.

What "own interpretation" refers to is contested. The *ESV Study Bible* sets out both views and prefers the first: verse 20 describes where prophecy came from, since verse 21 explains it by how prophecy was produced. The second reads it as a rule for readers, who must interpret the prophets as the apostles did. On either reading the meaning of Scripture originates with God, who spoke through its human authors by the Holy Spirit. A reader who supplies his own meaning has put himself in the place the Holy Spirit occupied.

**It ends in ruin.** Peter says of Paul's letters that some things in them "are hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction, as they do the other Scriptures" (2 Peter 3:16, ESV). "Twist" is στρεβλόω (*strebloō*, streb-LOH-oh, G4761), "to distort". "Destruction" is ἀπώλεια (*apōleia*, ap-OH-lie-ah, G684), the word Peter has already used for the "destruction of the ungodly" at the judgment (2 Peter 3:7, ESV) and for the end of the false teachers (2 Peter 2:1, 3). Peter puts the twisting of Scripture on the road that ends at the judgment.

**It adds to what God said.** "Do not add to his words, lest he rebuke you and you be found a liar" (Proverbs 30:6, ESV). Jesus charged the Pharisees with "making void the word of God by your tradition" (Mark 7:13, ESV): their reading of the Corban vow cancelled the fifth commandment it claimed to serve.

**It misleads others.** Paul refused "to tamper with God's word" (2 Corinthians 4:2, ESV), and the verb, δολόω (*doloō*, dol-OH-oh, G1389), means to distort or falsify. A teacher who reads a meaning in passes it on to everyone who trusts him.

### The remedy is to let God speak

Paul told Timothy to be "a worker who has no need to be ashamed, rightly handling the word of truth" (2 Timothy 2:15, ESV). "Rightly handling" is ὀρθοτομέω (*orthotomeō*, or-tho-tom-EH-oh, G3718), literally "to cut straight". The Levites in Ezra's day "gave the sense, so that the people understood the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8, ESV). The Bereans "received the word with all eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so" (Acts 17:11, ESV). Each of them drew the meaning out and checked it against the text.

This shows that God speaks in order to be understood. He meant something by every word He gave, and He gave His Son to make Him known (John 1:18). On the road to Emmaus the risen Jesus "interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27, ESV). The Author was showing what He had meant all along. So you may open the Bible expecting to hear God's voice in it. What He meant is better than anything you could bring to it.

## Reading by genre

Different kinds of biblical writing were written to work differently, and reading all of them the same way is where a lot of bad interpretation starts.

| Genre | Read for |
| --- | --- |
| **Epistles** (letters) | What problem in the original church occasioned this passage? Does the author himself mark a claim as universal ("in all the churches"), or as local practice? |
| **Old Testament narrative** | Narrative *records* what happened — it doesn't automatically endorse it. Does the narrator, or the rest of Scripture, evaluate this as good or bad? Or is that left for you to notice? |
| **Acts / historical precedent** | Was the story's own point to establish a repeatable pattern, or is the detail incidental to something else the author's actually making a point about? A pattern isn't automatically a command. |
| **Gospels** | Which Gospel, and what's that author's own emphasis and audience? Read each Gospel on its own terms before harmonizing it with the others. |
| **Parables** | What's the one main point the original hearers would have grasped? Who is Jesus needling, and what situation provoked the story? Resist reading meaning into every incidental detail. |
| **Law** | Moral, civil, or ceremonial? Even where a specific rule doesn't carry over today, what does it reveal about God's own character? |
| **Prophets** | Read first as God's people being held to an existing covenant — blessing for faithfulness, judgment for breach — in the prophet's own time, before reading it forward to any other time. |
| **Psalms** | What kind of psalm — lament, praise, thanksgiving, a royal psalm, an imprecatory one? A psalm is usually a prayer addressed to God, not a proposition addressed to you. Let its form shape what you expect from it. |
| **Wisdom literature** | Proverbs are generally-true observations, not unconditional promises. Hold that hopeful realism against Job and Ecclesiastes' more honest counterpoint, rather than flattening one against the other. |
| **Revelation / apocalyptic** | Find the Old Testament imagery a vision is drawing on before assigning it a novel, modern meaning. Remember it was written to seven real churches first — that pastoral purpose sits right alongside the future-oriented vision, not instead of it. |

## A few habits worth keeping

- **Compare translations.** Where two solid translations render a verse noticeably differently, slow down there.
- **Use commentaries last, not first.** Read the text yourself before you read what someone else concluded about it — a commentary should check your reading, not form it for you.
- **Name your sources.** If a conclusion came from a commentary, a lexicon, or someone else's teaching, say so. It's not a weakness to cite where an idea came from; it's how a reader can go verify it themselves.
