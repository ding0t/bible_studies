# Frequently asked questions

Short answers, each pointing at the page that works the question out properly. If what you came for
is a word — *lemma*, *MACULA*, *pericope* — the [Glossary](glossary.md) is the faster stop.

## About this site

### Who writes this, and is it authoritative?

One person, as personal study notes — written to make sure I learn, and published in case they help
someone else. They carry no authority beyond the Scripture they point at, and you are meant to test
them the way the Bereans tested Paul, "examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were
so" (Acts 17:11). If a study and your Bible disagree, your Bible wins.

### Is this written by AI?

The research is AI-assisted; the positions, the arguments and the errors are mine. AI does the
fetching, collating and checking — pulling every occurrence of a word, catching a quotation I have
misremembered, arguing back when a reading is weak. It has no spiritual authority, it does not pray,
and it cannot be taught by God. If something here is wrong, I published it.
→ [Why AI-assisted study](about/why-ai-assisted-study.md), and
[where it stops](about/why-ai-assisted-study.md#where-it-stops) in particular

### How do I check a claim for myself?

Every study ends with its sources, naming the dataset or work each claim came out of rather than
gesturing at "scholars". Most of that data is openly licensed and published alongside this site, so
you can go and re-run the query rather than take the result on trust.
→ [About our datasets](about/about-our-datasets.md#three-tiers-one-rule)

### I think something here is wrong.

Then please say so — [open an issue](https://github.com/ding0t/bible_studies/issues). Correction is
the point of publishing rather than keeping notes in a drawer.

### Is any of this finished?

Studies are revised as I learn more; [Recently updated](about/recent-updates.md) shows what has
changed lately, and the [Backlog](about/backlog.md) is the public list of what is still to be
written. Unfinished pages are held back rather than published half-done.

## What I believe

### What religion are you? What denomination?

Christian. That is the whole answer, and its not being a denomination is the point of it.

The question is harder to answer than it looks, because the categories the asker has in mind are
often not the ones the responder lives by. Australia's 2026 Census is a clean example. Question 24
offers ten tick-boxes — no religion, Catholic, Anglican, Islam, Hinduism, Uniting Church, Buddhism,
Presbyterian, Greek Orthodox, Baptist — and the word *Christian* is not among them. To give it as
your answer you have to leave the list entirely and write it into the "other" box, where the Bureau
records it as *Christianity, not further defined*.

Two things need saying plainly about that, because a false version of it went around in 2026.
"Christian" was never removed from the Census — it was never a tick-box in the first place — and it
is recorded when you write it in.[^abs] The Bureau's stated reason is that a single Christianity box
would lose the denominational detail, which is a fair thing for a statistician to want. The effect
on the person filling in the form is still that the list they are handed is a list of the things
that divide Christians, and the name they actually go by is not on it.

Paul met the same instinct in Corinth, and did not treat it as harmless:

> ✝️ 1 Corinthians 1:12-13 (ESV)
>
> What I mean is that each one of you says, "I follow Paul," or "I follow Apollos," or "I follow
> Cephas," or "I follow Christ." Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized
> in the name of Paul?

Where I gather is a separate question, and a real one: I fellowship at a Calvary Chapel. Being
planted in a local congregation matters, and this is not an argument for drifting between churches
on principle. But it answers *where I fellowship*, not *what I am*, and collapsing the second
question into the first is how the word Christian goes missing off a form.

[^abs]: Australian Bureau of Statistics, [Religious affiliation
    (RELP)](https://www.abs.gov.au/census/guide-census-data/census-dictionary/2026/variables-topic/cultural-diversity/religious-affiliation-relp),
    2026 Census dictionary; AAP FactCheck, [Falsehoods spread about census "Christian"
    category](https://www.aap.com.au/factcheck/falsehoods-spread-about-census-christian-category/),
    August 2026.

### What is your statement of faith?

Set out in full on its own page rather than summarised here, because a summary is where these things

### Where do you land on end times?

Dispensational and pretribulational: the church caught up to meet Christ before a literal seven-year
tribulation, then his visible return, a literal thousand-year reign, final judgement, and the new
heaven and new earth. → [End times](about/statement-of-faith.md#end-times) and the
[Last Things studies](last-things/index.md)

### What does "dispensational" mean here, in practice?

The word itself is Paul's — **οἰκονομία** (*oikonomia*), the administration of a household, used at
Ephesians 3:9 of the arrangement now in force; see [dispensationalism](glossary.md#dispensationalism)
for that. In practice, though, it rests on one commitment above all: that the Bible is the
authoritative Word of God, over tradition and over human leaders. It is to be read first as written and in context — not as symbolic poetry, and not as
verses cherry-picked to suit a belief already held.

The distinctive outcomes follow from that:

- Less focus on me, and more on "your will be done" — reading the Psalms, for instance, by first
  asking what they show us about Jesus as Messiah, before asking what they show me about myself.
- Creation is a literal six-day event.
  → [Creation](about/statement-of-faith.md#creation-is-6-days)
- End-times prophecy becomes simpler to read, because it is read the same way as everything else.
- The promises God made to Israel are not quietly transferred to the Church. The two are kept
  distinct, which changes how a great deal of prophecy reads.
  → [Israel and the Church](israel-and-church/israel-and-the-church.md)

### Is this a "Hebrew Roots" site?

No. Two different things share that phrase. Studying the Old Testament's types, its Torah, its
feasts and its prophecy is what Jesus and the apostles did, and this site does a lot of it. The
*movement* called Hebrew Roots teaches something else — that the Church simply is Israel, or that
Gentile believers are a scattered tribe recovering their identity — and this site does not hold
that. → [Cautions](israel-and-church/hebrew-roots.md#cautions)

### Do you think Christians have to keep the Torah or the feasts?

No, and Paul's warning against it is his sharpest language anywhere (Galatians 5:2-4). Studying what
the Torah reveals about God's character, and what it foreshadows in Christ, is not the same as being
bound to keep it. → [Cautions](israel-and-church/hebrew-roots.md#cautions)

### Do you treat 1 Enoch, the Apocrypha or the Dead Sea Scrolls as Scripture?

No. The canon here is the 66 books of the Old and New Testament, closed.
([What is Scripture](about/statement-of-faith.md#what-is-scripture)) Those other texts are used as
historical witnesses — evidence for how a passage was read, what a word meant to its first hearers,
and what the text said before the medieval manuscripts. The Dead Sea Scrolls in particular are a
thousand years older than the oldest complete Hebrew Bible, and this site checks disputed readings
against them. → [Ancient texts and manuscripts](scripture/ancient-texts-manuscripts.md)

## Bible text and translations

### Which translation do you use, and why?

The ESV for quotation, cross-checked against the NASB, NIV and NLT wherever a verse is disputed or
ambiguous — differently weighted translations disagreeing is itself information. The Masoretic Text
and the Septuagint anchor the original-language work.
→ [Translation philosophy](scripture/translations.md#translation-philosophy)

### Why not the KJV or the NKJV?

Not a matter of taste. The KJV rests on a late Greek text that had accumulated copying errors, and
the NKJV modernised the language while keeping that same underlying text. For study, almost any
modern translation is a better starting point. The KJV remains a landmark of English and is quoted
here where its wording is the point. → [English translations](scripture/translations.md#english-translations)

### Why does the New Testament sometimes quote the Old Testament differently from my Bible?

Usually because the New Testament author is quoting the Greek Old Testament, and it reads
differently from the Hebrew. Hebrews 10:5 quotes "a body you have prepared for me" where Psalm 40:6
in Hebrew has "ears". Neither is a mistake, and the [Scripture Links](references.md) tool shows both
verses in the original with the shared words highlighted, so you can see exactly what the quotation
rests on. → [Septuagint](scripture/translations.md#septuagint-greek-old-testament)

### How do we know the text hasn't been changed?

By comparing the manuscripts that carry it and saying openly where they disagree — which is what
textual criticism is, and it is a strength of the case rather than an embarrassment to it. This site
works the disputed cases rather than asserting that there are none.
→ [Textual criticism](scripture/ancient-texts-manuscripts.md#textual-criticism-how-we-actually-know)

## Dates and chronology

### When was Jesus crucified?

**Friday, 3 April AD 33.** The day of the week is the firm part: Mark calls it "the day of
Preparation, that is, the day before the Sabbath" (15:42), and Luke and John say the same. Only two
years in the plausible range put Passover on a Friday — AD 30 (7 April) and AD 33 (3 April).

The year rests mainly on Luke 3:1 read naturally, with Sejanus's fall behind the pressure on Pilate
at John 19:12, Daniel 9 and Paul's chronology all leaning the same way. AD 30 stays defensible for
anyone who dates Tiberius's fifteenth year from the co-regency. The lunar-eclipse argument that
often accompanies this date is **not** used here — it needs both a contested observation and a
strained reading of Peter's Joel quotation.
→ [Chronology Anchors](last-things/chronology-anchors.md#settling-the-crucifixion-year)

### Doesn't "three days and three nights" rule out a Friday?

It is the strongest objection to it. Friday afternoon to Sunday dawn gives two nights, not three.
The short answer is that the phrase occurs once (Matthew 12:40, quoting Jonah), the New Testament
describes the same interval "on the third day" eight times, and Mark and Matthew report one
prediction with "after three days" and "on the third day" respectively — so the Gospels treat the
two as equivalent, which only works if part of a day counts as a day. The Friday reading is the
stronger one and still spends that verse on credit.
→ [Three Days and Three Nights](jesus/three-days-and-three-nights.md)

## How the studies are made

### Why do the studies keep going into Hebrew and Greek?

Because most study questions start from an English word, and therefore from a translator's decision.
Going back to the word underneath shows what that decision was, and what it cost.
→ [Reading the original-language data](scripture/original-language-data.md), and the
[Glossary](glossary.md) for the vocabulary

### Why don't you just quote commentaries?

Commentaries last, not first: they are used to test a reading already reached from the text, never
to form one. Reaching for a commentary first is how you end up defending someone else's conclusion
without having examined the passage.
→ [What each tier is for](about/about-our-datasets.md#what-each-tier-is-for)

### Your cross-references don't match my study Bible's. Why?

Because these are derived from the biblical texts themselves rather than inherited from an existing
list — the site detects quotations and allusions by measuring the actual shared wording, and shows
you that wording so you can judge each link. It finds connections the traditional lists miss, and it
misses some they carry. → [How we cross-reference](about/how-we-cross-reference.md), and
[what it does not do](about/how-we-cross-reference.md#what-it-does-not-do)

## Using the site

### Where should I start?

Three doors, depending on what you came for.
[How to read the Bible](scripture/how-to-read-the-bible.md) is the method everything else here
follows — the questions to ask of a passage, in order. [Key takeaways](about/key-takeaways.md)
explains what every study is trying to hand you, so the studies read faster. And if you would rather
start with the thing itself, [The Way](jesus/the-way.md) is a word study that traces one Greek word,
ὁδός (*hodos*), from Isaiah through John 14:6 into the book of Acts, and shows what this site does
with a passage.

### How is the site organised, and how do I find a passage?

Directories carry the subject and tags carry everything else — method, language, audience. Search
(top of every page) covers the whole site, [Tags](tags.md) slices it across subjects, and
[Scripture Links](references.md) goes the other way, from a verse to what connects to it.
→ [Our taxonomy](about/our-taxonomy.md)

### What are the timeline and genealogy pages?

Two interactive charts rather than pages of prose. The
[Timeline](timeline.md) sets creation through to the millennial reign against the millennial-week
framework, showing the Masoretic, Septuagint and Samaritan chronologies side by side so you can see
where they disagree. The [Genealogy](genealogy.md) is a family tree from Adam to Jesus — lifespans,
Hebrew name meanings, and who was alive at the same time as whom, which is more surprising than it
sounds.
