# Why Not 4004 BC? Where Ussher's Forty-Six Years Came From

**For about 250 years, English Bibles printed "4004 BC" beside Genesis 1:1.** The date was James
Ussher's, worked out in his *Annales Veteris Testamenti* of 1650 and set in the margin of the
Authorised Version from 1701. This site counted from it until 1 October 2026. It now counts from
3959 BC, forty-five years later.

Ussher's arithmetic from Adam to the Exodus is exactly this site's arithmetic. So is the
Babylonian date he started from. All but one year of the difference lies in one stretch: the four
centuries of kings between Solomon's temple and the exile.

**In one sentence:** Ussher's count from Adam to the Exodus and his Babylonian anchor both stand;
his forty-six-year error came from laying the kings' reigns end to end as full years, and the
book of Kings itself, checked by an Assyrian eclipse, shows how Israel's scribes actually counted.

### Lessons about Jesus

Ussher's scheme put Christ's birth four thousand years after creation, in 5 BC, just before
Herod's death in 4 BC. That round number was partly expected before he counted: four thousand
years from Adam to Christ was a widely held scheme in his day. On the corrected line Jesus is born
about AM 3954 and crucified in AM 3991, dates that rest on Herod's death, Daniel's seventy weeks
and Luke's Roman regnal year (see [Chronology Anchors](chronology-anchors.md)). God sent His Son "when the fullness of time had come"
(Galatians 4:4, ESV), and Scripture's own evidence is what places that time.

## What Ussher did

Ussher worked backward from a date he could fix outside the Bible. Second Kings ends with
Jehoiachin released from prison by "Evil-merodach king of Babylon, in the year that he began to
reign" (2 Kings 25:27, ESV). Babylonian, Greek and Roman sources put that accession, Amel-Marduk's,
in 562 BC. From there Ussher had three steps to take:

1. **The kings, from the exile back to the temple.** He added up the reign lengths of the kings of
   Judah and reached the fourth year of Solomon at 1012 BC.
2. **The temple back to the Exodus.** First Kings 6:1 dates the temple "in the four hundred and
   eightieth year after the people of Israel came out of the land of Egypt" (ESV). That gave the
   Exodus at 1491 BC.
3. **The Exodus back to Adam.** The Masoretic ages in Genesis 5 and 11, Abram's 75 years at the
   call (Genesis 12:4) and the 430 years of Galatians 3:17 put the Exodus at Anno Mundi 2513.
   1491 + 2513 gives creation in 4004 BC.

In 1701 William Lloyd, Bishop of Worcester, set these dates in the margin of a folio Authorised
Version. Bibles went on printing them for about two and a half centuries, and many readers took
them for part of the text.

## Two of his three steps still stand

**Step 3 is this site's own count.** The Masoretic chain from Adam to the Exodus gives AM 2513
here too, and [Genealogy and Times](genealogy-times.md) works through why the site follows the
Masoretic numbers. It takes one contested fork, the length of Israel's stay in Egypt, which
[How long was Israel in Egypt?](#how-long-was-israel-in-egypt) sets out below. **The starting date
stands as well**: 562 BC is still the accepted year of
Amel-Marduk's accession.

Step 2 depends on reading 1 Kings 6:1's 480 years as elapsed years. That is contested (see below),
but this site reads it as a real interval, as Ussher did. The two differ by one year in the
counting: Ussher took "the four hundred and eightieth year" as 479 years elapsed (1012 to 1491 BC),
and this site counts 480 (966 to 1446 BC).

That leaves step 1. Ussher's fourth year of Solomon is 1012 BC. The reconstruction this site
follows puts it at 966 BC, forty-six years later. Less the one year of step 2, everything above
the temple moves by forty-five: the Exodus from 1491 to 1446 BC and creation from 4004 to 3959 BC.

## Scripture counts the same years twice

Kings tells the history of two kingdoms side by side. That builds a check into the text itself:
the same stretch of time is counted twice, once in each kingdom's list.

The cleanest stretch runs from the division of the kingdom to Jehu's revolt. It opens with
Jeroboam and Rehoboam taking their thrones in the same year. It closes in one chariot chase,
when Jehu shoots Joram of Israel and has Ahaziah of Judah shot as he flees (2 Kings 9:24, 27). Both
lists cover exactly the same years.

| Israel | Years | | Judah | Years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jeroboam (1 Kings 14:20) | 22 | | Rehoboam (1 Kings 14:21) | 17 |
| Nadab (1 Kings 15:25) | 2 | | Abijam (1 Kings 15:2) | 3 |
| Baasha (1 Kings 15:33) | 24 | | Asa (1 Kings 15:10) | 41 |
| Elah (1 Kings 16:8) | 2 | | Jehoshaphat (1 Kings 22:42) | 25 |
| Zimri (1 Kings 16:15) | 7 days | | Jehoram (2 Kings 8:17) | 8 |
| Omri (1 Kings 16:23) | 12 | | Ahaziah (2 Kings 8:26) | 1 |
| Ahab (1 Kings 16:29) | 22 | | | |
| Ahaziah (1 Kings 22:51) | 2 | | | |
| Joram (2 Kings 3:1) | 12 | | | |
| **Total** | **98** | | **Total** | **95** |

Laid end to end, Israel's reigns give 98 years for this stretch and Judah's give 95. Both cannot be
the elapsed time. In Edwin Thiele's reconstruction it is 90 years, from 931 to 841 BC. The book of
Kings records two habits that account for the difference.

### A part-year counted whole

> ✝️ 1 Kings 15:25 (ESV)
>
> 25 Nadab the son of Jeroboam began to reign over Israel in the second year of Asa king of Judah,
> and he reigned over Israel two years.

Three verses later Baasha kills him "in the third year of Asa king of Judah" (1 Kings 15:28, ESV).
Nadab's "two years" fit between Asa's second year and Asa's third. The Hebrew is
<span dir="rtl">שְׁנָתָיִם</span> (*shenatayim*, H8141), the dual of *shanah*, "year": a pair of
years, counted as two. Both "began to reign" and "reigned" render one verb,
<span dir="rtl">מָלַךְ</span> (*malakh*, H4427), "to be king". The verse counts any part of a
calendar year on the throne as a year of the reign.

Elah shows the same thing. He "began to reign over Israel in Tirzah, and he reigned two years" from
Asa's twenty-sixth year (1 Kings 16:8, ESV), and Zimri killed him "in the twenty-seventh year of
Asa" (1 Kings 16:10, ESV). Historians call this non-accession reckoning. The year a king dies is
counted twice, as his last year and as his successor's first, so every change of king adds a year
on paper that never happened. Nine kings reign in Israel's column. Laid end to end, their numbers
run long by most of those changeovers.

### Sons beside their fathers

Judah's column runs long for a different reason. Some sons began to reign while their fathers were
still alive. Joram of Israel took the throne "in the second year of Jehoram the son of
Jehoshaphat, king of Judah" (2 Kings 1:17, ESV), and also "in the eighteenth year of Jehoshaphat
king of Judah" (2 Kings 3:1, ESV). Jehoshaphat's eighteenth year was his son's second, so the son
was already counting years of his own reign while his father still reigned. Of Uzziah, 2 Kings says, "he was a leper to the day of his death, and he lived in a
separate house. And Jotham the king's son was over the household, governing the people of the land"
(2 Kings 15:5, ESV). When father and son both count those shared years, the sum double-counts them.

### Ussher's 424 years against Thiele's 380

Ussher knew this. Added straight through, the kings of Judah from the temple to the fall of
Jerusalem total about 430 years. He allowed for a few overlapping reigns and brought it down to
424, from 1012 to 588 BC. Thiele's reconstruction gives 380, from 966 to 586 BC, so forty-four of
Ussher's years are overlaps and part-years that he still counted as elapsed time. The other two of
the forty-six sit at the far end, where Ussher put Jerusalem's fall in 588 BC and the
reconstruction puts it in 586.

## The Assyrian check

Ussher had no outside record of the kings to test his total against. Assyrian cuneiform was
deciphered only in the 1850s, two centuries after he wrote.

The Assyrians named each year after an official, the eponym, and kept the list. One entry, in the
eponymy of Bur-Sagale, records a solar eclipse. Astronomers can date it to 15 June 763 BC, and that
one fixed point turns the whole list into a calendar of known years. Two of its kings then meet
Israel's:

- **853 BC.** Shalmaneser III's Kurkh Monolith lists "Ahab the Israelite" among the kings who
  fought him at Qarqar. Ahab is alive in 853.
- **841 BC.** His Black Obelisk shows Jehu, or his envoy, bowing with tribute. Jehu is on the
  throne by 841.

That leaves twelve years between Ahab's last year and Jehu's first. Scripture gives the two kings
in between 2 and 12 years (1 Kings 22:51; 2 Kings 3:1), fourteen on paper. Count them the way
Nadab was counted and they come to twelve. Ahaziah "began to reign over Israel in Samaria in the
seventeenth year of Jehoshaphat ... and he reigned two years" (1 Kings 22:51, ESV), and Joram
followed him "in the eighteenth year of Jehoshaphat" (2 Kings 3:1, ESV). Ahaziah's two years
cover one calendar year, the same pattern as Nadab's. Ahaziah's first year was also Ahab's last,
and Joram's first was also Ahaziah's last. Take out those two shared years and fourteen becomes
twelve, the interval Assyria gives.

Thiele published this reconstruction in *The Mysterious Numbers of the Hebrew Kings* (1951, third
edition 1983). Its fixed points carry the kings back to Solomon's fourth year at 966 BC. The
synchronisms Kings gives fit Assyria's independent record once they are counted the way Kings
counts them.

## Others have reached the same answer

Rodger Young, who has carried Thiele's work forward with Leslie McFall, followed Ussher step by step
in "Ussher Explained and Corrected" (*Bible and Spade* 31.2, 2018). He reaches this study's verdict
by the same route. He accepts Ussher's count from Adam to the Exodus, AM 2513. He notes that
Ussher's BC dates "are measured upward from the chronology of the divided kingdom," so an error
there moves every date above it. He puts the start of the temple in spring 967 BC, "45 years later
than Ussher's date". And he explains the gap by the Nadab pattern: Israel's scribes counted a new
king's first part-year twice, a method Valerius Coucke identified in the 1920s and Thiele found
independently.

Young's 967 BC and this site's 966 differ by a year, and so do his 479 elapsed years and this
site's 480. The two differences cancel, and both put the Exodus in 1446 BC. Young prints no creation
date, but his figures with Ussher's AM 2513 give 3959 BC, the date this site uses. He closes by
asking Ussher's defenders "to accept these corrections to his otherwise magnificent work."

Not all of them have. Floyd Nolen Jones's *Chronology of the Old Testament* keeps 4004 BC, though
Young notes that Jones already follows Thiele in telling accession-year counting from
non-accession counting. Danny Faulkner of Answers in Genesis doubts Ussher's exact day and holds
that "Ussher's approximate date of creation still is 4000 BC". He names "regnal dating of monarchs"
and a longer sojourn in Egypt as open questions (*Answers Research Journal* 9, 2016). Outside specialist work on the kings, 4004 BC is still
widely taken as "the" Bible date, which is the confusion Lloyd's margins began.

## What is still contested

Three questions remain open, and none of them brings back 1012 BC.

- **Thiele's system has loose ends.** He could not reconcile the synchronisms of Hezekiah's reign
  in 2 Kings 18 with the rest, and later work by Leslie McFall and Rodger Young has adjusted
  several of his dates by a year or two. Jerusalem's fall is still argued between 587 and 586 BC.
  These move dates by a year or two. None moves the temple by forty.
- **The 480 years may be schematic.** The *ESV Study Bible* reads 1 Kings 6:1 at face value as
  supporting an Exodus about 1446 BC. It also sets out the case for twelve generations of a
  symbolic forty years, which gives a "late" Exodus around 1260 BC. The *NIV Biblical Theology
  Study Bible* adds a third option: a total of periods that partly overlapped. This site reads the
  480 as elapsed years.
- **The Septuagint reads 440.** Greek 1 Kings (3 Kingdoms) 6:1 has the "four hundred and fortieth
  year". This site follows the Masoretic 480.

Each of these bears on the Exodus and creation dates. None bears on the temple, which the Assyrian
record fixes on its own. A fourth question sits inside step 3, the part of Ussher's chain this site
shares, and it moves creation further than any of them.

## How long was Israel in Egypt?

Exodus gives the stay as 430 years, and the ancient texts attach that number to different things.

> ✝️ Exodus 12:40 (ESV)
>
> 40 The time that the people of Israel lived in Egypt was 430 years.

The Septuagint reads "in the land of Egypt and in the land of Canaan". The Samaritan Pentateuch
reads "the dwelling of the sons of Israel and their fathers" in "the land of Canaan and the land of
Egypt". Paul counts the 430 years from the promise to Abraham: "the law, which came 430 years
afterward" (Galatians 3:17, ESV). Josephus gives 430 years from Abraham's arrival in Canaan and 215
from Jacob's move to Egypt (*Antiquities* 2.15.2). On that reading Israel was in Egypt 215 years.
This is the **short sojourn**, taken by Ussher and by this site. The **long sojourn** reads the
Hebrew of Exodus 12:40 as 430 years in Egypt itself, and Genesis 15:13 and Acts 7:6 speak of four
hundred years of affliction in a land not theirs.

Two kinds of evidence pull opposite ways.

- **The family line favours the short stay.** Kohath went down to Egypt with his father Levi
  (Genesis 46:11). Kohath lived 133 years, his son Amram 137, and Amram's son Moses was 80 at the
  Exodus (Exodus 6:18, 20; 7:7). Even if each son was born in his father's last year, that allows
  at most 350 years from the move to Egypt to the Exodus. Moses' mother was "Jochebed the daughter
  of Levi, who was born to Levi in Egypt" (Numbers 26:59, ESV). A 430-year stay needs this line to
  skip generations, as [Four Hundred and Eighty Years](four-hundred-and-eighty-years.md) shows
  Ruth's and Ezra's lists do.
- **Population favours the long stay.** [What World Population
  Declares](../god/world-population-declares-gods-creation-and-biblical-truth.md#the-rate-the-census-implies)
  works out the growth from seventy people to the two million or so implied by the census of
  Numbers 1:46. Over 430 years that takes 2.39% a year, the population doubling every 29 years.
  Over 215 years it takes 4.77% a year, a doubling every 15 years. Read the census as clans or
  military units, as some do, and seventy reaching 20,000 in 215 years takes 2.63% a year. Exodus
  presents the growth as extraordinary in itself: Israel "multiplied and grew exceedingly strong"
  (Exodus 1:7, ESV), and "the more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied" (Exodus 1:12,
  ESV).

Population makes the short stay harder without ruling it out, and the family line does the same to
the long stay. This site follows the short sojourn because two of the three ancient texts state it
outright and Paul counts that way. It remains contested.

The choice moves dates more than anything else in this study. On the long sojourn the Exodus falls
in AM 2728, Abram is born in 2166 BC, and creation moves to 4174 BC, which puts the six-thousandth
year in AD 1827.

## What moved when the site corrected it

Moving the temple from 1012 to 966 BC moved every date counted back from it.

| | Ussher | This site since 2026-10-01 |
|---|---|---|
| Solomon's temple begun | 1012 BC | 966 BC |
| The Exodus | 1491 BC | 1446 BC |
| Creation | 4004 BC | 3959 BC |
| AD 2026 | AM 6029 | AM 5984 |
| AM 6000 | AD 1997 | AD 2042 |

The last row matters most for the rest of the site. On Ussher's epoch the six-thousandth year has
already passed. On the corrected line it is still future, which keeps the millennial-week reading
open; [A Day Is a Thousand Years](../last-things/day-is-a-thousand-years.md) weighs that reading, and [The Zadok
Calendar](../feasts/zadok-calendar.md#where-year-0-sits) sets out the alternate epochs the site
still tracks.

## The sum of your word is truth

The kings' numbers in Kings were right all along. Nadab's two years and Ahaziah's two years say
what Israel's court scribes meant by them, and the synchronisms beside them tell any reader how to
count. The error belonged to a reader who added them up under a convention the writers never used.

This shows that God gave His word through real people writing in the conventions of their own
courts and calendars, and that what He gave is true in what it affirms. The statement of faith this
site holds says Scripture "in its original writing, is without error and fully trustworthy in
everything it affirms", and starts from Psalm 119:160:

> ✝️ Psalm 119:160 (ESV)
>
> 160 The sum of your word is truth, and every one of your righteous rules endures forever.

Ussher's sum was a man's sum. The sum of God's word, read the way it was written, comes out right.
So you may read Kings with confidence, check one number against another as Ussher tried to, and
expect the text to hold. It held for him everywhere but the kings' reigns, where he counted his
own way.

## Discussion questions

1. Nadab "reigned two years" but died the year after he took the throne. Before this study, would
   you have called that a contradiction? What changed?
2. Kings gives every king a reign length and a synchronism with the other kingdom. Why might the
   writer have supplied both, when either alone would place the king?
3. Ussher's dates sat in Bible margins for 250 years and many readers took them as Scripture. What
   else might you have absorbed from a study Bible's apparatus as if it were the text?
4. The correction came from an Assyrian eclipse record kept by scribes who served other gods. What do you make of God's providence running through a record like that?
5. Where in your own reading of Scripture have you been adding up the numbers your way rather than
   the writer's?
6. Moses' family line points to a short stay in Egypt, and Israel's growth points to a long one.
   How do you hold a question Scripture leaves open without forcing an answer?
