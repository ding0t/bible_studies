# Friday, 3 April AD 33: Dating the Cross

Paul says that "when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under
the law, to redeem those who were under the law" (Galatians 4:4-5, ESV). He says again that "at the
right time Christ died for the ungodly" (Romans 5:6, ESV). John marks that time by the feast: "before
the Feast of the Passover, when Jesus knew that his hour had come to depart out of this world to the
Father" (John 13:1, ESV).

That time can be found. The Law fixes the Passover to a day of the month. The moon fixes that day to
a day of the week in each year. The Gospels then give the facts that choose the year: a Friday at
Passover, under Pontius Pilate (Mark 15:42; Luke 23:54; John 19:31; Matthew 27:62), after John began
to preach "in the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar" (Luke 3:1, ESV).

**In one sentence:** God sent His Son to the cross at the time He had set, and when each Passover
under Pilate is reckoned by the Law's rules, the Gospels' details place it on Friday, 3 April AD 33,
with AD 30 the one serious rival.

### Types & Prophecy

**Type.** Jesus died on "the day of Preparation of the Passover" (John 19:14, ESV), the day the
lambs were killed (Exodus 12:6), and Paul names what that pattern meant: "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been
sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7, ESV).

**Prophecy.** Daniel 9:25 counts sixty-nine weeks "from the going out of the word to restore and
build Jerusalem to the coming of an anointed one, a prince", and after them "an anointed one shall be
cut off" (Daniel 9:26, ESV). Counted from Nehemiah's decree in 444 BC, in years of 360 days, the
sixty-nine weeks reach the Triumphal Entry of AD 33, days before the cross. The decree and the year
length are both readings, and the [Daniel 9:25 section](#daniel-925-and-the-decree-it-counts-from)
gives the case for each.

### Lessons about Jesus

- His disciples counted the days from His death. On the Sunday two of them called it "the third day
  since these things happened" (Luke 24:21), and Mark sets His death on the day before the Sabbath
  (Mark 15:42).
- Luke dates the ministry by five rulers (Luke 3:1-2). God worked our redemption inside public
  history, under officials anyone could name.
- He went to the cross knowing "his hour had come" (John 13:1), at the Passover His death fulfilled,
  on Friday, 3 April AD 33.

## How the Passover is dated

Before we weigh any fact about the crucifixion, we need the Passover of each year it might have
happened. That depends on the Law's rules and on the sky. It can be settled before the Gospels are
opened.

### What the Law fixes

God gave Israel its calendar at the Exodus: "This month shall be for you the beginning of months. It
shall be the first month of the year for you" (Exodus 12:2, ESV). The lamb was kept "until the
fourteenth day of this month, when the whole assembly of the congregation of Israel shall kill their
lambs at twilight" (Exodus 12:6, ESV). Leviticus repeats the date and sets the Feast of Unleavened
Bread on the fifteenth (Leviticus 23:5-6). Moses calls the month Abib and ties it to the season of
the Exodus (Deuteronomy 16:1). After the exile it is called Nisan (Nehemiah 2:1).

So the Passover is a fixed day of a lunar month. God made the moon "to mark the seasons" (Psalm
104:19, ESV). A lunar month does not keep step with the seven-day week, and so 14 Nisan falls on a
different weekday from year to year.

### The rules this reckoning assumes

To find 14 Nisan in a given year, we reconstruct the calendar in common Jewish use at the time. That
was the Pharisaic-Rabbinic lunar-solar calendar, and four rules govern it.

1. **The day begins at sunset.** "There was evening and there was morning, the first day" (Genesis
   1:5), and "from evening to evening shall you keep your Sabbath" (Leviticus 23:32). Each day
   carries its night at the front.
2. **The month begins with the first crescent seen from Jerusalem**, on the evening after the new
   moon. 14 Nisan is counted from that evening.
3. **Nisan is the first month whose fifteenth day falls on or after the spring equinox.** A year
   that would put the feast too early received a second month of Adar. That kept the Passover in
   the spring, as Deuteronomy 16:1 requires.
4. **The cross fell on 14 Nisan**, which John calls "the day of Preparation of the Passover" (John
   19:14, ESV), the day the lambs were killed. Whether the Synoptic Gospels place it on 15 Nisan is
   a long-standing question of its own. The dates below follow John.

Rules 2 and 3 are where the reckoning can err. A thin crescent might have been seen a night sooner or
later than we can now say, and that moves the feast by a day. So the table gives the other date
wherever a year's crescent was marginal.

### The Passovers of Pilate's prefecture, AD 26 to 36

Pontius Pilate governed Judea from AD 26 to 36 (so the *ESV Study Bible* at Luke 3:1). All four
Gospels put the cross under him, so the Passover we seek is one of these eleven. This site worked
out the new moon for each by the rules above (`utils/compute_nisan_14.py`):

| Year | 14 Nisan | Weekday | If the crescent was marginal |
|---|---|---|---|

| AD 27 | 10 April | Thursday | **Friday** 11 April if seen a night late |
| AD 28 | 30 March | Tuesday | |
| AD 29 | 18 April | Monday | Sunday 17 April if seen a night early |
| AD 30 | 7 April | **Friday** | Thursday 6 April if seen a night early |
| AD 31 | 27 March | Tuesday | Monday 26 March if seen a night early |
| AD 32 | 14 April | Monday | Sunday 13 April if seen a night early |
| AD 33 | 3 April | **Friday** | none: the crescent was well clear |
| AD 34 | 24 March | Wednesday | |
| AD 35 | 12 April | Tuesday | |
| AD 36 | 31 March | Saturday | Sunday 1 April if seen a night late |

Four of the eleven can give a Friday Passover. AD 30 and AD 33 do so on the usual reckoning, and
AD 26 and AD 27 only on a marginal crescent. The *ESV Study Bible*'s article on the question names
the same two: "the only plausible years for such a Friday corresponding to Nisan 14 are A.D. 30 or
33." AD 33 keeps its Friday on every reckoning. AD 30 keeps it only if the crescent was not seen a
night early.

## The facts that bear on the date

With every Passover in hand, we can gather the facts. Each is set out here as the text or the record
gives it. They are weighed together in the next section.

### The day of the week

Mark calls the day of the cross "the day of Preparation, that is, the day before the Sabbath" (Mark
15:42, ESV). His word is **προσάββατον** (*prosabbaton*, G4315, "fore-sabbath"), and it occurs
nowhere else in the New Testament. He explains it for his readers, as he explains other Jewish
customs (Mark 7:3-4). It fixes the first of the three days, the day of the crucifixion and burial.
Luke says the same without the term (Luke 23:54). **παρασκευή** (*paraskeuē*, G3904,
"Preparation") is used of that day six times across the four Gospels.

Luke also counts from the other end. On the Sunday itself, two disciples on the road to Emmaus call
it "the third day since these things happened" (Luke 24:21, ESV). Counted inclusively from a
Friday, that Sunday is the third day. Counted from a Wednesday, it is the fifth.

### How Scripture counts three days

The phrase "three days and three nights" appears in one New Testament verse. There Jesus takes it
from Jonah (Jonah 1:17):

> ✝️ Matthew 12:40 (ESV)
>
> 40 For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the
> Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.

Read as a measurement, that requires about seventy-two hours. A death on Friday afternoon and an
empty tomb at dawn on Sunday spans two nights and parts of three days. The New Testament states the
same interval nineteen other times, counted from the Greek text:

| Phrasing | Occurrences | Where |
|---|---|---|
| "on the third day" | **9** | Matthew 16:21; 17:23; 20:19; Luke 9:22; 18:33; 24:7; 24:46; Acts 10:40; 1 Corinthians 15:4 |
| "after three days" | **4** | Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:34; Matthew 27:63 |
| "in three days," of the temple He would raise (John 2:21) | **6** | John 2:19, 20; Matthew 26:61; 27:40; Mark 14:58; 15:29 |
| "three days and three nights" | **1** | Matthew 12:40 |

Two of these phrasings report one prediction. After Peter's confession, Mark 8:31 has "after three
days rise again" and Matthew 16:21 has "on the third day be raised". That works only if part of a
day counted as a day. Read strictly, "after three days" would put the resurrection on the fourth.
The *ESV Study Bible* calls this "inclusive" reckoning at Matthew 12:40: "the combination of any part
of three separate days."

The chief priests use both phrasings a sentence apart (Matthew 27:63-64). They quote Jesus as saying
"after three days I will rise". Then they ask for a guard "until the third day". If "after three
days" had meant seventy-two hours, their guard would have lapsed before the moment they feared. They
were hostile witnesses with no stake in the convention. Their usage shows it was the ordinary way of
speaking.

Jesus was alive through the night that began the Preparation, so that night never counts, whichever
day the cross is placed on. The Sabbath is the only complete day in the tomb. Every reckoning
therefore leans on the two partial days at either end.

### The case for a Wednesday

Those who place the cross on a Wednesday rest on three things.

The first is the "high day". John says "that Sabbath was a high day" (John 19:31, ESV), using
**μεγάλη**. If that names a festival Sabbath, two Sabbaths fell in that week, and the third night
becomes available. The phrase can also mean the weekly Sabbath, made great by the feast that fell on
it. The words alone do not decide.

The second is the spices. Luke has the women prepare spices and then rest on the Sabbath (Luke
23:56). Mark has them buy spices "when the Sabbath was past" (Mark 16:1). On a single Sabbath the
order is awkward. They would have bought on Saturday evening what they had prepared on Friday
evening, in a burial window of minutes. Two Sabbaths remove the difficulty. A Wednesday crucifixion
gives a festival Sabbath on Thursday, an ordinary Friday to buy and prepare, and the weekly Sabbath
to rest. The single-Sabbath answer is that Greek aorists do not order events as sharply as an
English reader hears them. That is a fair reading of the grammar, and it is also a harmonisation.

The third is the count itself. On sunset reckoning, a Wednesday crucifixion with the resurrection at
the close of the Sabbath gives three literal nights and days. The Gospels narrate the empty tomb at
dawn and never the moment of rising, so the texts do not exclude it.

### Luke 3:1 and the fifteenth year of Tiberius

Luke dates John's preaching "in the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar" (Luke 3:1, ESV).
He names five officials with their titles. Tiberius succeeded Augustus on 19 August AD 14, so his
fifteenth year runs from August AD 28 to August AD 29. The *ESV Study Bible* puts it at "probably
A.D. 29 (plus or minus a year)". The *NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible* puts it at AD 28-29. Jesus
was baptised after John began to preach (John 1:29-34), so the ministry begins no earlier than late
AD 28.

A second reckoning counts Tiberius's years from AD 13, when he gained authority alongside Augustus.
The *Cultural Backgrounds* volumes report that scholars often date the fifteenth year that way, to
AD 27-28.

### The Passovers in John

John names three Passovers in the ministry (John 2:13; 6:4; 11:55). There are four if the unnamed
feast of John 5:1 was one. Take the earliest start Luke 3:1 allows, late AD 28. The first named
Passover then falls in AD 29, the second in AD 30, and the third no earlier than AD 31. From a start
in late AD 29, reaching AD 33 takes four Passovers: AD 30, 31, 32 and 33. So that year needs the
feast of John 5:1 to be a Passover, or one Passover to pass unmentioned. The *ESV Study Bible* runs
the same count and reaches AD 33.

One view holds that John records only two Passovers in the ministry. On that view his temple
cleansing (John 2:13-22) is the Synoptics' one at the end (Mark 11:15-17; Luke 19:45-46), moved
forward for thematic reasons. John's own time markers place it near the start. "After this" Jesus
went to Capernaum "for a few days", and then "the Passover of the Jews was at hand" (John 2:12-13).

### Sejanus's fall and the "friend of Caesar" charge

The crowd told Pilate, "If you release this man, you are not Caesar's friend" (John 19:12, ESV).
That is the language of *amicus Caesaris*. Pilate's patron Sejanus fell in October AD 31, and after
that any link with him was dangerous. The threat would have weighed on Pilate far more in AD 33 than
in AD 30.

### Daniel 9:25 and the decree it counts from

Daniel's count runs "from the going out of the word to restore and build Jerusalem" (Daniel 9:25,
ESV). Four Persian decrees touch Jerusalem: Cyrus's (538 BC), Darius's (about 520), Artaxerxes' to
Ezra in his seventh year (457, or 458 on a spring-to-spring count), and Artaxerxes' to Nehemiah in
his twentieth (444). [Prophecy: Events and Times](../last-things/prophecy-events-times.md#which-decree)
tabulates them. The reckoning this site follows makes two choices:

- **The decree is Nehemiah's.** Nehemiah asks to be sent "to the city of my fathers' graves, that I
  may rebuild it" (Nehemiah 2:5, ESV) and is given timber "for the gates of the fortress of the
  temple, and for the wall of the city" (Nehemiah 2:8, ESV). Ezra's commission funds the temple's
  worship, and Ezra blesses God for putting it in the king's heart "to beautify the house of the LORD
  that is in Jerusalem" (Ezra 7:27, ESV). Daniel 9:25 speaks of the city, built again "with squares
  and moat".
- **The years are 360 days long**, as Revelation 11:2-3 counts them ([How the tiers
  work](chronology-anchors.md#how-the-tiers-work)). Sixty-nine weeks of them is 69 × 7 × 360 =
  173,880 days, or 476 solar years. From Artaxerxes' twentieth year that reaches AD 32 if the decree
  is counted at 445 BC, and AD 33 if it is counted at 444 BC.

Sir Robert Anderson ran the 173,880 days from 14 March 445 BC (*The Coming Prince*, 1894). He reached
10 Nisan, which he calculated as Sunday 6 April AD 32, and took it for the Triumphal Entry. His
calculation is why AD 32 is still proposed. Harold Hoehner moved the end of the count a year later.
On Anderson's own numbers, an AD 32 crucifixion falls on the wrong weekday for a Friday Passover, as
the table above shows. Hoehner counted from Nehemiah's Nisan in 444 BC to 10 Nisan, Monday 30 March
AD 33. [Prophecy: Events and Times](../last-things/prophecy-events-times.md#the-arithmetic) sets out
both sums and the two-to-four-day slack in each.

The other classic reckoning counts 483 solar years from Ezra's decree in 457 BC to AD 27. Its
advocates take AD 27 for Jesus' baptism. They place the cross three and a half years later, in AD 30
or 31, reading "for half of the week he shall put an end to sacrifice and offering" (Daniel 9:27,
ESV) of it. The *ESV Study Bible* counts the same way from 458 BC to AD 26, and allows either AD 30
or AD 33.

### John 2:20 and the forty-six years

At the first Passover of the ministry, the Jews say *Τεσσεράκοντα καὶ ἓξ ἔτεσιν οἰκοδομήθη ὁ ναὸς
οὗτος*. The verb οἰκοδομήθη ("was built", G3618) is an aorist. ναός (*naos*, G3485) is the sanctuary
proper, within the whole ἱερόν (*hieron*, G2411) complex. Josephus has Herod build the naos in
eighteen months from his eighteenth year, finishing around 18/17 BC. The wider precinct stood
unfinished until the AD 60s. So the dative ἔτεσιν (*etesin*, G2094) carries two defensible senses.
Each pairs with one candidate year to give a ministry of about three years:

| Reading | Counts from | First Passover | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| "has taken forty-six years to build" | Herod's start, 20/19 BC | AD 27 | AD 30 |
| "was built forty-six years ago" | the naos completed, 18/17 BC | AD 29/30 | AD 33 |

The *ESV Study Bible* calls the second reading "much more likely," on the grammatical grounds just
given. The grammar leans that way without settling it.

### Paul's three years and fourteen years

The Gallio inscription puts Gallio's proconsulship of Achaia (Acts 18:12) at AD 51/52. Galatians
1:18 and 2:1 give three years and then fourteen after Paul's conversion. Where that lands depends on
which Jerusalem visit Galatians 2:1 describes, and on whether the two spans overlap. The *ESV Study
Bible* puts the conversion about AD 33, "an approximate date", and the Galatians 1:18 visit about
AD 36. That leaves little room after an AD 33 crucifixion for the events of Acts 1-8, and advocates
of AD 30 press the point. Other reconstructions place the conversion a year or two later.

### The sky that day

The darkness at the crucifixion (Matthew 27:45; Mark 15:33; Luke 23:44-45) is popularly called an
eclipse. A solar eclipse needs the moon between the earth and the sun, which happens only at new
moon. The Passover falls at full moon, in the middle of the month. Whatever darkened the land for
three hours that Friday, the moon could not have done it. The Gospels narrate it as a sign at Jesus'
death, with the torn veil and the earthquake (Matthew 27:51). The critical Greek text of Luke 23:45
(NA28) uses a verb (*eklipontos*) related to our word "eclipse", which fed the popular confusion. The
later Byzantine text behind the KJV reads "the sun was darkened".

A lunar eclipse did fall that week, on the evening of the crucifixion in AD 33. Colin Humphreys and
Graeme Waddington calculated every lunar eclipse visible from Jerusalem at Passover in AD 26-36
(*Nature*, 1983). They found exactly one, on **Friday, 3 April AD 33**. A partial eclipse was
already in progress as the moon rose over the Mount of Olives around 6:20pm. That was hours after
the daytime darkness, as 14 Nisan ended and the Sabbath began. They linked it to Peter's Pentecost
sermon, which quotes Joel 2:31: "the sun shall be turned to darkness, and the moon to blood" (Acts
2:20, ESV). Peter's hearers, they proposed, had that eclipse in living memory seven weeks later.

Bradley Schaefer replied in the *Quarterly Journal of the Royal Astronomical Society* (1990). He
recalculated how much of the moon's disc was shadowed as it cleared the horizon. He found very
little: at most a partial dimming of a fraction of the disc, largely gone before the moon stood high
enough to see clearly in the twilight. He likened it to spotting an eight-watt red bulb beside a
searchlight. Humphreys' reply has not settled the question.

## Weighing the evidence

### The day: a Friday

The weekday is settled by the statements that say which day it was, and they favour a Friday.
Mark's προσάββατον and Luke's "third day" on the Sunday both name the day. The costs on the Friday
side concern only how the days are counted.

| | Takes at face value | Has to absorb |
|---|---|---|
| **Wednesday** | Matthew 12:40, which on sunset reckoning gives three nights; the Luke and Mark spice sequence, without harmonising | Mark's **προσάββατον**; the nine "on the third day" statements; a Sunday its own count makes the fifth day (Luke 24:21) |
| **Friday** | προσάββατον; Luke 24:21; the chief priests using both phrasings a sentence apart | one missing night; a harmonisation of Luke's "prepared" against Mark's "bought" |

The Friday reading absorbs one night of Matthew 12:40 by inclusive reckoning. It may fairly do so,
because the same convention appears on its own in Matthew 27:63-64, from men with no reason to
defend it. The Wednesday reading must set aside Mark's word for the day and the disciples' own
count. Whichever you hold, you should be able to name both entries in its row.

### Settling the crucifixion year

A Friday Passover under Pilate leaves four years. AD 26 and AD 27 fall before John began to preach
(Luke 3:1). The choice therefore lies between Friday 7 April AD 30 and Friday 3 April AD 33. Both
are 14 Nisan, so the question of 14 or 15 Nisan does not decide between them.

Luke 3:1 is the primary evidence, and it favours AD 33. Read naturally, Tiberius's fifteenth year
begins the ministry in AD 28-29, and John's Passovers carry it to AD 33. To reach AD 30, Tiberius's
years must be counted from the co-regency of AD 13. That reckoning has real support. It sits
awkwardly beside the care Luke takes to name five officials with their exact titles.

The Passovers in John make AD 30 hard to hold. From any start Luke 3:1 allows, AD 30 is at most the
second Passover of the ministry, and John names three. AD 33 has its own cost: it needs John 5:1 to
be a Passover, or one Passover to go unmentioned. AD 30 escapes only if John moved the temple
cleansing forward. Most AD 30 reconstructions rest on that assumption, so the year of the cross and
the number of temple cleansings are one question. John's time markers at 2:12-13 tell against it.

Sejanus supports AD 33. A Pilate who yields to the charge of being no friend of Caesar fits the
years after Sejanus fell. Daniel 9:25 supports AD 33 as well. On the reckoning this site follows it
excludes AD 30, whichever date Nehemiah's decree is given. AD 30 is reached only from Ezra's temple
decree, counted in solar years. This site holds Nehemiah's decree and the 360-day year for the
reasons given above, and marks both as readings.

The remaining three lines decide nothing between the years. John 2:20 fits either, and the year does
not need it. Paul's chronology turns on which visit Galatians 2:1 describes, and the verdict does
not lean on it. The eclipse bears on the year only through Acts 2:20. That link needs a contested
observation and a strained citation, for Peter is quoting Joel about what *will* happen before the
day of the Lord. So the eclipse is left out of the case.

## Verdict: Friday, 3 April AD 33

Reckoned by the Law's rules, a Friday 14 Nisan under Pilate falls in AD 30 and AD 33. Once the year
is chosen, the date is exact. The year rests on Luke 3:1 read naturally and on the Passovers John
records. Sejanus supports it, and so does Daniel 9, which excludes AD 30 on either date for
Nehemiah's decree.

AD 30 remains defensible in three ways. You may count Tiberius's fifteenth year from the co-regency,
hold that John's temple cleansing is the Synoptics' one, or count Daniel's weeks from Ezra in solar
years. Together these are the strongest case against. Entry 20 of [Chronology
Anchors](chronology-anchors.md#the-444-bc-decree-behind-daniels-sixty-nine-weeks), 444 BC, rests on
Nehemiah's own two dates (Nehemiah 1:1; 2:1). So Daniel 9 reaches AD 33 from a starting year fixed
on its own grounds.

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** Mark explains "the day of Preparation" for his readers as **προσάββατον**
   (*prosabbaton*, G4315), "the day before the Sabbath" (Mark 15:42), as he explains other Jewish
   customs elsewhere (Mark 7:3-4). What does that one word settle about the day Jesus died, and what
   does it leave for the other evidence to decide?
2. **The text in its context.** Luke names an emperor, a governor, three tetrarchs and two high
   priests before he says "the word of God came to John" (Luke 3:1-2). Why would Luke date the start
   of the gospel this carefully, and what kind of events is he claiming to report?
3. **Christ.** Pilate brought Jesus out on "the day of Preparation of the Passover" (John 19:14), and
   Paul calls Christ "our Passover lamb" (1 Corinthians 5:7). What does the timing of the cross say
   about what His death was?
4. **The hard part.** The Friday reading has to absorb one night of Matthew 12:40, and AD 33 needs
   John 5:1 to be a Passover or one Passover to go unmentioned. Which cost seems heavier to you, and
   what evidence would change your mind?
5. **Be transformed.** Christ died "at the right time" (Romans 5:6), at the Passover His Father had
   set. Where are you waiting on God's timing now, and what does a cross dated to the day say to you
   about it?
