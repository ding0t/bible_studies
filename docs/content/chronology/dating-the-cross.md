# Friday, 3 April AD 33: Dating the Cross

Paul says that "when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under
the law, to redeem those who were under the law" (Galatians 4:4-5, ESV), and that "at the right time
Christ died for the ungodly" (Romans 5:6, ESV). John marks that time by the feast: "before the Feast
of the Passover, when Jesus knew that his hour had come to depart out of this world to the Father"
(John 13:1, ESV). The Gospels give the time as a day and a year. All four put the cross on the day of Preparation before a Sabbath, at Passover,
under Pontius Pilate (Mark 15:42; Luke 23:54; John 19:31; Matthew 27:62). Luke dates the ministry by
the emperor's reign: John began to preach "in the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar,
Pontius Pilate being governor of Judea" (Luke 3:1, ESV). Those details fix the day of the week,
narrow the year to two, and then decide between them.

**In one sentence:** God sent His Son to the cross at the time He had set, and the Gospels' own
details, a Friday Passover under Pilate, Luke's fifteenth year of Tiberius and the Passovers John
records, place it on Friday, 3 April AD 33, with AD 30 the one serious rival.

### Types & Prophecy

**Type.** Jesus died on "the day of Preparation of the Passover" (John 19:14, ESV), the day the
lambs were killed (Exodus 12:6), and Paul names what that pattern meant: "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been
sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7, ESV).

**Prophecy.** Daniel 9:25 counts sixty-nine weeks "from the going out of the word to restore and
build Jerusalem to the coming of an anointed one, a prince", and after them "an anointed one shall be
cut off" (Daniel 9:26, ESV). Counted from Nehemiah's decree in 444 BC, in years of 360 days, the
sixty-nine weeks reach the Triumphal Entry of AD 33, days before the cross. The decree and the year
length are both readings, and the [Daniel 9:25 section](#daniel-925-and-the-decree-it-counts-from)
gives the case for each.

### Lessons about Jesus

- He died on a day His own followers could count from. Two of them call the Sunday "the third day
  since these things happened" (Luke 24:21), and Mark names the first of the three as the day before
  the Sabbath (Mark 15:42).
- He died in a year Rome kept records of. Luke dates the ministry by five rulers (Luke 3:1-2), and
  this shows that God worked our redemption inside public history, under officials anyone could name.
- He went to the cross knowing "his hour had come" (John 13:1): the Passover His death fulfilled, in
  the year the evidence points to, Friday, 3 April AD 33.

## The day: a Friday

Mark's word for the day and Luke's count from it put the cross on a Friday, and the cost is one night
of Matthew 12:40. The phrase "three days and three nights" is usually raised to ask which day of the week the cross fell on.

> ✝️ Matthew 12:40 (ESV)
>
> 40 For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the
> Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.

Read as a measurement, that requires roughly seventy-two hours. A crucifixion on Friday afternoon
and an empty tomb at dawn on Sunday spans two periods of darkness and parts of three days. The gap
is one night, and it is the whole of the case for moving the crucifixion to a Wednesday or a
Thursday.

### The phrase is quoted once; the interval is stated nineteen other times

"Three days and three nights" appears in exactly one New Testament verse, and there Jesus is quoting
Jonah (Jonah 1:17). Counted from the Greek text:

| Phrasing | Occurrences | Where |
|---|---|---|
| "on the third day" | **9** | Matthew 16:21; 17:23; 20:19; Luke 9:22; 18:33; 24:7; 24:46; Acts 10:40; 1 Corinthians 15:4 |
| "after three days" | **4** | Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:34; Matthew 27:63 |
| "in three days," of the temple He would raise (John 2:21) | **6** | John 2:19, 20; Matthew 26:61; 27:40; Mark 14:58; 15:29 |
| "three days and three nights" | **1** | Matthew 12:40 |

**The two phrasings are used of the same prediction.** Mark 8:31 has "after three days rise again"
where Matthew 16:21 has "on the third day be raised" — one occasion, both following Peter's
confession. That is only possible if part of a day counts as a day, since "after three days" read
strictly would put the resurrection on the fourth. The convention has a name: the *ESV Study Bible* states it at Matthew 12:40 as "inclusive" reckoning, "the
combination of any part of three separate days."

**The chief priests use both phrasings one sentence apart** (Matthew 27:63-64), quoting Jesus as
saying "after three days I will rise" and then asking for a guard "until the third day." If "after
three days" meant seventy-two hours, a guard expiring on the third day would lapse before the moment
they were guarding against. That is the sharpest form of the argument, because it sits inside one
paragraph by one author, from hostile witnesses with no stake in the convention.

### Two statements about which day

**Mark names it.** "The day of Preparation, that is, the day before the Sabbath" (Mark 15:42)
translates **προσάββατον** (*prosabbaton*, G4315, "fore-sabbath"), which occurs once in the New Testament,
defining the day Jesus died. It anchors the *first* of the three days, the day of the crucifixion
and burial. Luke says the same without the
term (Luke 23:54), and **παρασκευή** (*paraskeuē*, G3904, "Preparation") is used of that day six times across
the four Gospels.

**Luke counts from the other end.** On the road to Emmaus, on the Sunday itself, two disciples call
that day "the third day since these things happened" (Luke 24:21). Counting inclusively from a
Friday: Friday one, Saturday two, Sunday three. From a Wednesday, that Sunday is the fifth day. It is two
ordinary people counting, in passing, on the day in question.

### The week as the Hebrew calendar counts it

The Hebrew day runs evening to evening — "there was evening and there was morning, the first day"
(Genesis 1:5), and "from evening to evening shall you keep your Sabbath" (Leviticus 23:32). Each day
carries its night at the front.

The night of the Preparation is the one He was
*alive* for, so it never counts, whichever day the crucifixion is placed on. And the Sabbath is the
only complete day in the tomb, which is why every reckoning of this has to lean on the two partial
days at either end.

**Sunset reckoning does not add a night to the Friday view.** It does strengthen the other side: on
sunset reckoning a
Wednesday crucifixion with the resurrection at the end of the Sabbath gives a literal three nights
and three days, and since the Gospels never narrate the moment of the resurrection — only the
discovery of an empty tomb at dawn — that placement is not excluded by the texts. What it does not escape is Luke 24:21.

### The counter-case, at its strongest

John 19:31 calls that Sabbath "a high day," **μεγάλη**. If that names a *festival* Sabbath distinct
from the weekly one, two Sabbaths fell in that week and the extra night becomes available. The
phrase is open between that and "the weekly Sabbath, made great by a feast falling on it."

**The spices are the better version of the argument.** Luke has the women *prepare* spices and then
rest on the Sabbath (Luke 23:56); Mark has them *buy* spices "when the Sabbath was past" (Mark 16:1). On a
single Sabbath those sit awkwardly — why buy on Saturday evening what was prepared on Friday evening,
in a burial window measured in minutes? Two Sabbaths dissolve it: a Wednesday crucifixion gives a
festival Sabbath on Thursday, an ordinary Friday on which the women could both buy and prepare, and
the weekly Sabbath to rest. The single-Sabbath answer is that Greek aorists do not order these
events as sharply as an English reader hears them. That is a real reading, and a
harmonisation.

### Friday, and what it costs

The arithmetic still has an answer, and the weight is with a Friday. The Friday reading buys Matthew 12:40 on credit: it needs
inclusive reckoning to absorb the missing night. What keeps that from being special pleading is that
the convention is visible independently, in Matthew 27:63-64, used by people with no stake in
defending it.

| | Takes at face value | Has to absorb |
|---|---|---|
| **Wednesday** | Matthew 12:40 — and on sunset reckoning it does deliver three nights; the Luke/Mark spice sequence, without harmonising | Mark's **προσάββατον**; the nine "on the third day" statements; a Sunday its own count makes the fifth day (Luke 24:21) |
| **Friday** | προσάββατον; Luke 24:21; the chief priests using both phrasings a sentence apart | one missing night; a harmonisation of Luke's "prepared" against Mark's "bought" |

This study holds Friday, because προσάββατον and Luke 24:21 are explicit statements about *which
day* while both costs on the Friday side are about *how to count*. Anyone holding the date should be
able to name both entries in their own column.

## Settling the crucifixion year

Two dates satisfy the Gospels' own constraints once the years before John began to preach are set aside. The crucifixion was on a Friday at Passover, under Pilate, who was prefect from AD 26 to 36 (so the *ESV Study Bible* at Luke 3:1). Reconstructing the first-century Jewish calendar from the first visibility of the lunar crescent, Nisan 14 fell on a Friday twice in the years after John began to preach:

| Candidate | Date |
|---|---|
| **AD 30** | Friday 7 April |
| **AD 33** | Friday 3 April |

### The astronomical filter, and why AD 32 persists

**Why the other nine years drop out, and why AD 32 keeps coming back.** The filter is a single
astronomical fact, and it carries a stated assumption: on the Pharisaic-Rabbinic lunar-solar
calendar in common Jewish use at the time, Nisan 14 landed on a Friday only twice in Pilate's
eleven-year prefecture once the years before John began to preach are set aside. The *ESV Study Bible*'s own article on the question puts it the same way —
"the only plausible years for such a Friday corresponding to Nisan 14 are A.D. 30 or 33."
Every year from AD 28 except AD 30 and AD 33 fails on the weekday alone, before any of the historical arguments
below are reached, which is why a candidate list is always short and never includes AD 31, AD 34, or
any of the rest.

**This study's own computation agrees, and adds detail.** Working the new moons for every Passover
from AD 26 to 36, with the month begun at the first crescent seen from Jerusalem and the feast on or
after the spring equinox, gives a Friday 14 Nisan in AD 30 and AD 33. Two earlier years can give one
on a marginal crescent: AD 26 (Friday 22 March, on the eve of the equinox, or Saturday if the thin
crescent was missed) and AD 27 (Thursday 10 April, or Friday 11 April if the crescent was seen a
night late). Both come before John began to preach (Luke 3:1), so neither is a candidate. AD 30's
Friday also depends on the crescent: seen a night early, 14 Nisan that year is Thursday 6 April.
AD 33's crescent was well clear, and its 14 Nisan is Friday 3 April on every reckoning.

#### AD 32: Anderson's count and Hoehner's correction

**AD 32 is the one that keeps being proposed anyway.** It is a genuine candidate on *chronological*
grounds and not on astronomical ones. Sir Robert Anderson's
reckoning of Daniel's sixty-nine weeks (*The Coming Prince*, 1894) runs 173,880 days from 14 March
445 BC to 10 Nisan, which he calculated as Sunday 6 April AD 32 and identified as the Triumphal Entry
itself, and that calculation is the reason the year circulates. Harold Hoehner moved the terminus a
year later, because on Anderson's own numbers an AD 32 crucifixion falls on the wrong day of the week
for a Friday Passover. He counted from Nehemiah's Nisan in 444 BC to 10 Nisan, Monday 30 March AD 33. See
[Prophecy: Events and Times](../last-things/prophecy-events-times.md#the-arithmetic) for both versions of that sum
and the two-to-four-day slack in each.

### Six lines of evidence between AD 30 and AD 33

Both are Nisan 14, so the long-standing question of whether the Synoptics and John place the crucifixion on Nisan 14 or 15 does not decide between them. Four other lines do. Two more, John 2:20 and Paul's chronology, are weighed after them and decide nothing.

[![The year of the cross, drawn after Clarence Larkin: the eleven Passovers of Pilate's prefecture, AD 26 to 36, each with the weekday of 14 Nisan. Below, each line of evidence is placed under the year it favours: Luke 3:1, the Passovers in John (with AD 33's own cost), Sejanus and Daniel 9:25 under AD 33, with what AD 30 needs to answer each; John 2:20 and Paul's chronology between the two, Paul dashed; the eclipse of Acts 2:20 dashed, weighed and not used. #### Luke 3:1 and the fifteenth year of Tiberius

**Luke 3:1 is the primary evidence, and it favours AD 33.** Tiberius succeeded Augustus on 19 August AD 14, so his fifteenth year runs from August AD 28 to August AD 29. The *ESV Study Bible* puts it at "probably A.D. 29 (plus or minus a year)" and the *NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible* at AD 28-29. John's ministry begins there, Jesus is baptised after it, and the Passovers John records carry the ministry across three and a half years to AD 33 — set out below. Reaching AD 30 instead requires counting Tiberius's fifteenth year from AD 13, when he gained authority alongside Augustus. The *Cultural Backgrounds* volumes report that scholars often date the fifteenth year that way, to AD 27-28, so the reckoning has real support; it sits awkwardly beside Luke's evident care in naming five officials with their exact titles.

#### The Passovers in John

**The Passovers in John make AD 30 hard to hold.** John marks at least three Passovers across the
ministry (John 2:13; 6:4; 11:55), possibly four if the unnamed feast at 5:1 is one. Take the earliest
start Luke 3:1 allows, late AD 28: the first of those Passovers falls in AD 29, the second in AD 30,
the third no earlier than AD 31 — so the crucifixion cannot be at the second. Reaching AD 33 from a start in late AD 29 takes four Passovers (AD 30, 31, 32 and 33), so that date also needs the unnamed feast of John 5:1 to be a Passover, or one Passover to pass unmentioned. The *ESV Study Bible*
runs the same count and reaches AD 33 by it, and treats it as the argument that makes AD 30
difficult.

The escape from that is to say John records only two Passovers, on the view that his temple cleansing
(John 2:13-22) is the Synoptics' one at the end of the ministry (Mark 11:15-17; Luke 19:45-46), moved forward for thematic reasons. That
is a real position, and it is the load-bearing assumption under most AD 30 reconstructions, so the crucifixion year and the number of temple cleansings are the same
question wearing two hats. John's own time markers resist it: "after this" He went to Capernaum "for
a few days," and *then* "the Passover of the Jews was at hand" (John 2:12-13).

#### Sejanus's fall and the "friend of Caesar" charge

**Sejanus favours AD 33.** "If you release this man, you are not Caesar's friend" (John 19:12, ESV) uses the language of *amicus Caesaris*, and it lands on Pilate with real force only after his patron Sejanus fell in October AD 31 and association with him became dangerous. A Pilate who caves to that threat fits AD 33 better than AD 30.

#### Daniel 9:25 and the decree it counts from

**Daniel 9:25 excludes AD 30 on the reckoning this site follows, and that reckoning makes two choices.** The count runs "from the going out of the word to restore and build Jerusalem" (Daniel 9:25, ESV), and four Persian decrees touch Jerusalem: Cyrus's (538 BC), Darius's (about 520), Artaxerxes' to Ezra in his seventh year (457, or 458 on a spring-to-spring count) and Artaxerxes' to Nehemiah in his twentieth (444); [Prophecy: Events and Times](../last-things/prophecy-events-times.md#which-decree) tabulates them.

- **The decree is Nehemiah's.** Nehemiah asks to be sent "to the city of my fathers' graves, that I may rebuild it" (Nehemiah 2:5, ESV) and is given timber "for the gates of the fortress of the temple, and for the wall of the city" (Nehemiah 2:8, ESV). Ezra's commission funds the temple's worship, and Ezra blesses God for putting it in the king's heart "to beautify the house of the LORD that is in Jerusalem" (Ezra 7:27, ESV). Daniel 9:25 is about the city, built again "with squares and moat".
- **The years are 360 days long**, as Revelation 11:2-3 counts them ([How the tiers work](chronology-anchors.md#how-the-tiers-work)). Sixty-nine weeks of them is 69 × 7 × 360 = 173,880 days, or 476 solar years. From Artaxerxes' twentieth year that reaches AD 32 counting the decree at 445 BC, and AD 33 counting it at 444 BC.

Neither route produces AD 30. The other classic reckoning can. Counting 483 solar years from Ezra's decree in 457 BC reaches AD 27, which its advocates take for Jesus' baptism, with the cross three and a half years later, reading "for half of the week he shall put an end to sacrifice and offering" (Daniel 9:27, ESV) of it, in AD 30 or 31. The *ESV Study Bible* counts from 458 BC to AD 26 the same way and allows for either AD 30 or AD 33. That reading has to treat Ezra 7's temple decree as the word to rebuild the city, and Daniel's years as ordinary ones. This study holds Nehemiah's decree and the 360-day year for the reasons above, and marks both as readings.

#### John 2:20: neutral between both years

**John 2:20 turns out to be neutral.** The Greek reads *Τεσσεράκοντα καὶ ἓξ ἔτεσιν οἰκοδομήθη ὁ ναὸς οὗτος* — an aorist, οἰκοδομήθη ("was built", G3618), and ναός (*naos*, G3485), the sanctuary proper rather than the whole ἱερόν (*hieron*, G2411) complex. Josephus has Herod building the naos in eighteen months from his eighteenth year, finishing around 18/17 BC, while the wider precinct was unfinished until the AD 60s. So the dative ἔτεσιν (*etesin*, G2094) carries two defensible senses, and each pairs with one candidate to give a ministry of about three years:

| Reading | Counts from | First Passover | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| "has taken forty-six years to build" | Herod's start, 20/19 BC | AD 27 | AD 30 |
| "was built forty-six years ago" | the naos completed, 18/17 BC | AD 29/30 | AD 33 |

The verse is compatible with either year and selects neither. The *ESV Study Bible* goes further and
calls the second reading "much more likely," on the same grammatical grounds set out
above. This study stops short of that: the grammar leans that way without settling it,
and the year does not need John 2:20 to carry weight it cannot.

#### Paul's chronology, which cuts both ways

**Paul's chronology cuts both ways.** The Gallio inscription puts his proconsulship of Achaia (Acts 18:12) at AD 51/52, and Galatians 1:18 and 2:1 give three years and then fourteen after Paul's conversion. Where that lands depends on which Jerusalem visit Galatians 2:1 describes, and on whether the two spans overlap. The *ESV Study Bible* puts the conversion about AD 33, "an approximate date", and the Galatians 1:18 visit about AD 36, which leaves little room after an AD 33 crucifixion for the events of Acts 1-8; advocates of AD 30 press exactly that point. Other reconstructions place the conversion a year or two later. This line is contested, and the verdict below does not lean on it.

## The sky that day

Two things in the sky that day are often brought into the dating. This study weighs both and uses neither.

### The darkness at full moon

The darkness at the crucifixion (Matthew 27:45;
Mark 15:33;
Luke 23:44-45) is popularly called an eclipse. That is
astronomically impossible: a solar eclipse requires the moon to sit between the earth and sun, which only happens
at new moon, and the crucifixion took place at Passover, which by definition falls at **full**
moon (14 Nisan, the middle of the lunar month). Whatever caused three hours of midday darkness that
Friday, the moon could not have been the cause. The Gospels themselves don't call it an eclipse.
They narrate it as a supernatural sign accompanying Jesus's death, alongside the temple veil tearing
and an earthquake (Matthew 27:51). The critical Greek text of Luke 23:45 (NA28) does use a verb
(*eklipontos*) related to our word "eclipse," which fed the popular confusion; the later Byzantine
text behind the KJV reads "the sun was darkened" instead. The astronomy
rules it out regardless of that wording.

### The lunar eclipse of 3 April AD 33

An independently computable eclipse *is* connected to this week, on the evening of the crucifixion. Colin Humphreys and Graeme Waddington calculated every lunar eclipse
visible from Jerusalem at Passover across every candidate crucifixion year, AD 26-36 (*Nature*,
1983). They found exactly one: **Friday, 3 April AD 33**.

A partial lunar eclipse was already in progress as the moon rose over the Mount of Olives around
6:20pm, hours *after* the daytime darkness of the crucifixion, at the moment 14 Nisan was ending
and 15 Nisan, the Sabbath, was beginning.

They connected this to Peter's Pentecost sermon, which quotes Joel 2:31
(ESV) ("the sun shall be turned to darkness, and the
moon to blood") as Acts 2:20 (ESV). Their proposal
is that Peter's audience had this very eclipse, seven weeks earlier, in living memory.

#### Schaefer's reply and Acts 2:20

**That claim has been directly challenged.** Bradley Schaefer replied in the *Quarterly Journal of
the Royal Astronomical Society* (1990). He recalculated how much of the moon's disc would actually
have been shadowed as it cleared the horizon and became visible to the naked eye.

The answer was small. By his estimate, at most a partial dimming of a fraction of the disc, with the
eclipsed portion largely gone by the time the moon was high enough to see clearly against the
twilight sky. His comparison: trying to spot an eight-watt red bulb next to a searchlight.

So a "blood moon" vivid enough to be *remarked on* seven weeks later overstates what was visible.
Humphreys' reply has not settled it.

The eclipse is a hard astronomical fact. It bears on the crucifixion year only through Acts 2:20: if Peter was
pointing at that eclipse, it favours AD 33. That link is the contested part. Schaefer's critique
leaves the eclipse standing and the vivid "blood moon" doubtful, and Peter is quoting Joel about
what *will* happen before the day of the Lord.

An argument that needs both a contested observation and a strained citation should not be load-bearing.
So this site does not count the eclipse as evidence for the year.
[The verdict](#verdict-friday-3-april-ad-33) settles AD 33 on Luke 3:1 and the
Passovers in John, with Sejanus and Daniel 9 in support, and leaves the eclipse out of the case.

## Verdict: Friday, 3 April AD 33

The date itself is astronomically exact once the year is chosen; the year rests on Luke 3:1 read naturally and the Passovers John records, supported by Sejanus and by Daniel 9, which excludes AD 30 on either date for Nehemiah's decree. AD 30 stays defensible for anyone who takes Tiberius's fifteenth year from the co-regency, holds that John's temple cleansing is the Synoptics' one, or counts Daniel's weeks from Ezra in solar years, and this study records both as the strongest case against. Entry 20 of [Chronology Anchors](chronology-anchors.md#the-444-bc-decree-behind-daniels-sixty-nine-weeks), 444 BC, rests on Nehemiah's own two dates (Nehemiah 1:1; 2:1), so Daniel 9 reaches AD 33 from a starting year fixed on its own grounds.

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** Mark explains "the day of Preparation" for his readers as **προσάββατον**
   (*prosabbaton*, G4315), "the day before the Sabbath" (Mark 15:42), as he explains other Jewish
   customs elsewhere (Mark 7:3-4). What does that one word settle about the day Jesus died, and what
   does it leave for the other evidence to decide?
2. **The text in its context.** Luke names an emperor, a governor, three tetrarchs and two high
   priests before he says "the word of God came to John" (Luke 3:1-2). Why would Luke date the start
   of the gospel this carefully, and what kind of events is he claiming to report?
3. **Christ.** Pilate brought Jesus out on "the day of Preparation of the Passover" (John 19:14), and
   Paul calls Christ "our Passover lamb" (1 Corinthians 5:7). What does the timing of the cross say
   about what His death was?
4. **The hard part.** The Friday reading has to absorb one night of Matthew 12:40, and AD 33 needs
   John 5:1 to be a Passover or one Passover to go unmentioned. Which cost seems heavier to you, and
   what evidence would change your mind?
5. **Be transformed.** Christ died "at the right time" (Romans 5:6), at the Passover His Father had
   set. Where are you waiting on God's timing now, and what does a cross dated to the day say to you
   about it?
