# Reading the Song of Songs

The Song of Songs is eight chapters of love poetry between a man and a woman. God made that love at
the beginning: He "brought her to the man," and "they shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:22, 24,
ESV). The Song most likely names Him once, at its climax, and calls the fire of their love "the very
flame of the LORD" (Song of Songs 8:6, ESV).

**In one sentence:** The Song celebrates the love of a betrothed man and woman, most likely calls
that love's fire the flame of the LORD, and so treats married love as His gift; Paul names what
marriage itself points to: Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:32).

## Key Takeaways

### Types & Prophecy

Paul supplies the key the Song never states. He quotes Genesis 2:24 and says of it, "This mystery
is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32, ESV). The
Song makes no prediction, and Paul does not quote it. The line from the Song to Christ runs through
Genesis 2:24, which Paul does quote.

### Lessons about Jesus

- **Jesus loves His church with a love as strong as death.** "Love is strong as death" (Song of
  Songs 8:6, ESV), and Christ "loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25, ESV).
- **Jesus burns with holy zeal.** The Song's word for jealousy, <span dir="rtl">קִנְאָה</span>
  (*qinʾah*, H7068), is the word of Psalm 69:9, "zeal for your house has consumed me" (ESV), which
  the disciples remembered when Jesus cleared the temple (John 2:17).

## What the Song is

The Song is a sequence of love poems between a man and a woman, "which is Solomon's" (1:1, ESV).
The church long took that title at face value, and modern critics dispute it (*CSB Ancient Faith
Study Bible*, introduction to the Song).
Three times the woman charges her friends "that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases"
(2:7; 3:5; 8:4, ESV). Love has a time, and the Song waits for it. By chapter 8 the waiting is over:
the *ESV Study Bible* heads 8:5-14 "The Lovers Join in Marriage," the anxious waiting giving way to
relaxed enjoyment (note on 8:5-14).

Contemporary evangelical scholarship reads the Song as what it appears to be — love poetry
celebrating marriage between a man and a woman, with the couple "apparently betrothed" (*ESV Study
Bible*, note on Song 1:2-2:17), its wine imagery comparable to Egyptian love poetry (*NIV Cultural
Backgrounds Study Bible*, note on Song 1:2).

## How it has been read

The Song's place in Scripture was argued over early. The Mishnah records that "there is a dispute
about the Song of Songs," and then Rabbi Akiva's answer: "the whole world is not as worthy as the day
on which the Song of Songs was given to Israel; for all the writings are holy but the Song of Songs
is the holy of holies" (*m. Yadayim* 3:5). The church gave it the same weight. Eusebius records that
Origen "commenced his Commentaries on the Song of Songs" while in Athens and "completed these also,
ten books in number" after returning to Caesarea (*Ecclesiastical History* 6.32). Over the centuries
Jewish readers took the book of God and Israel, and Christian readers of Christ and the church.

## The flame of the LORD

The Song never mentions God, with a single exception, at the book's climax:

> ✝️ Song of Songs 8:6-7 (ESV)
>
> 6 Set me as a seal upon your heart, as a seal upon your arm, for love is strong as death, jealousy
> is fierce as the grave. Its flashes are flashes of fire, the very flame of the LORD. 7 Many waters
> cannot quench love, neither can floods drown it. If a man offered for love all the wealth of his
> house, he would be utterly despised.

The Hebrew behind "the very flame of the LORD" is <span dir="rtl">שַׁלְהֶבֶתְיָה</span>
(*shalhevetyah*) — <span dir="rtl">שַׁלְהֶבֶת</span> (*shalhevet*, "flame," H7957) with
<span dir="rtl">יָה</span> (*Yah*, H3050) attached, the short form of the divine name. Translations
divide on it. The ESV, NASB and LSB take it as the name ("the very flame of the LORD"; the LSB has
"The very flame of Yah"). The NIV ("a mighty flame"), CSB ("an almighty flame!") and NKJV ("A most
vehement flame") read the ending as an intensifier. Both are defensible, and the disagreement is
about Hebrew idiom.

The translators' own footnotes record the fork. The *Legacy Standard Bible* offers "A vehement flame"
as an alternative, and explains the ending as "the shortened form of Yahweh, found in poetry and
praise (e.g. Hallelujah)…". The CSB, which prints the intensifier, footnotes "the blaze of the Lord".

If the name is there, then the one time God is named in the Song, it is to identify the fire in human
love as His own. That is a stronger warrant for reading the book theologically than any allegory of
its individual images.

The *ESV Study Bible* draws the same conclusion from the name: "both love and a jealousy to protect
marriage are given by God" (note on 8:6).

### Jealousy, and the seal

The poem defines the jealousy by pairing it with love, line for line: "love is strong as death,
jealousy is fierce as the grave." That parallelism makes it resolute devotion, which is how the
*ESV Study Bible* reads it (note on 8:6). The word is <span dir="rtl">קִנְאָה</span> (*qinʾah*,
H7068). It is used of a husband's jealousy (Proverbs 6:34), and it is also the word Scripture uses
for the LORD's own zeal: "I am jealous for Zion with great jealousy" (Zechariah 8:2, ESV); "The zeal
of the LORD of hosts will do this" (Isaiah 9:7, ESV).

"A seal" is <span dir="rtl">חוֹתָם</span> (*chotam*, H2368), the signet a man pressed into clay to
mark something as his. Judah handed his over as a pledge (Genesis 38:18). The bride asks to be that
mark on her husband's heart and arm. Paul uses the picture the other way round: God "has also put
his seal on us and given us his Spirit in our hearts as a guarantee" (2 Corinthians 1:22, ESV). The
direction is reversed and the mark of belonging is the same.

**This shows that God is the author of married love, and He guards it with His own zeal.** He
made it at the beginning (Genesis 2:24), and many waters cannot put it out (8:7).

## Reading it toward Christ

Reading the Song toward Christ needs a discipline. The *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* warns, on
the foxes of 2:15, that the image "has given rise to an enormous number of speculative
interpretations" and "is not meant to be grist for imaginative interpreters." A book read
allegorically line-by-line will yield whatever the reader brought to it, which is why the
identification in Ephesians 5:32 carries weight the Song cannot supply on its own.

Paul holds that key. He quotes the first marriage — "the two shall become one flesh" — and says, "This
mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31-32,
ESV). Paul applies Genesis 2:24 to Christ; the step to every faithful marriage, the Song's included, is an
inference from that. The Song preaches Christ by
being what it is: a love as strong as death, which is the love with which Christ "loved the church
and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25, ESV).

## Arise, come away

> ✝️ Song of Songs 2:10-13 (ESV)
>
> 10 My beloved speaks and says to me: "Arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come away, 11 for
> behold, the winter is past; the rain is over and gone. 12 The flowers appear on the earth, the time
> of singing has come... 13 The fig tree ripens its figs, and the vines are in blossom; they give
> forth fragrance. Arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come away."

Winter past, the fig tree budding, and on that evidence: *arise and come away*. The inference runs
the same way as the Olivet Discourse's fig tree — read the season off the tree, and know He is near
(Matthew 24:32-33). OpenBible's cross-reference list includes Matthew 24:32 for Song 2:13.

It is still a resonance. Matthew does not quote the Song, and συκῆ (*sykē*) is simply the ordinary word for a
fig tree, so shared vocabulary proves nothing on its own. What the two passages share is a structure
of inference: the season tells you the time.

## Discussion Questions

1. If <span dir="rtl">שַׁלְהֶבֶתְיָה</span> (*shalhevetyah*) in Song of Songs 8:6 does name God, what
   does it mean that the book's only mention of Him identifies the fire in human love as His?
2. Three times the Song says "that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases" (2:7; 3:5; 8:4,
   ESV). Why
   would a book of love poetry keep telling its readers to wait?
3. Paul says marriage "refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). What changes when you read
   the Song after Ephesians 5, and what stays the same?
4. Origen wrote ten books on the Song; a modern study Bible warns that its foxes are "not meant to be
   grist for imaginative interpreters." Where is the line between reading the Song toward Christ and
   decoding it?
5. "Many waters cannot quench love" (8:7). Where do you need to believe that about Christ's love for
   you this week?
