# Learn Greek

**In one sentence:** the New Testament was written in Greek, and its 24 letters are enough to start
reading the names it gives the Holy Spirit.

The apostles wrote in *Koine*, the common Greek of the first-century Roman world. Many of its letters
are already familiar from mathematics and from the capitals of the English alphabet. Learn the
table below, then read the [Names of the Holy Spirit word cards](names-of-the-holy-spirit-cards.md),
which add the letters a few at a time. [Learn Hebrew](../hebrew/index.md) does the same for the Old
Testament.

## The alphabet

The sounds are the ones taught for reading the New Testament, which this site's transliterations
follow (the *SBL Handbook of Style*).

| Capital | Small | Name | Written | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Α | α | alpha | a | *a* in *father* |
| Β | β | beta | b | *b* |
| Γ | γ | gamma | g | hard *g* in *go*; before γ, κ, ξ or χ it is *n*: ἄγγελος, *angelos* |
| Δ | δ | delta | d | *d* |
| Ε | ε | epsilon | e | *e* in *get* |
| Ζ | ζ | zeta | z | *z*, or *dz* |
| Η | η | eta | ē | *e* in *they* |
| Θ | θ | theta | th | *th* in *thin* |
| Ι | ι | iota | i | *i* in *machine*, or in *sit* |
| Κ | κ | kappa | k | *k* |
| Λ | λ | lambda | l | *l* |
| Μ | μ | mu | m | *m* |
| Ν | ν | nu | n | *n* |
| Ξ | ξ | xi | x | *x* in *box* |
| Ο | ο | omicron | o | *o* in *not* |
| Π | π | pi | p | *p* |
| Ρ | ρ | rho | r | *r*; at the start of a word *rh* |
| Σ | σ, ς | sigma | s | *s*; written ς at the end of a word |
| Τ | τ | tau | t | *t* |
| Υ | υ | upsilon | y | French *u*; in a diphthong *u* |
| Φ | φ | phi | ph | *f* |
| Χ | χ | chi | ch | *ch* in Scottish *loch* |
| Ψ | ψ | psi | ps | *ps* in *lapse* |
| Ω | ω | omega | ō | *o* in *tone* |

Two vowels together often make one sound, a **diphthong**: αι (*ai*, as in *aisle*), ει (*ei*, as
in *eight*), οι (*oi*, as in *oil*), ου (*ou*, as in *soup*), αυ (*au*, as in *house*) and ευ
(*eu*). A mark like a reversed apostrophe over an opening vowel, the **rough breathing**, adds an
*h*: ἅγιον is *hagion*, "holy". The accents (´ ` ˜) mark the stressed syllable.

Modern Greek is pronounced differently: β is *v*, δ is *th* as in *this*, and η, ι, υ, ει and οι
are all *ee*. So πνεῦμα is *pneuma* in the classroom and *pnevma* in Athens. The cards give the
classroom reading, and a line on each says what the word means in Greek today.

## The capitals of the manuscripts

The oldest copies of the New Testament were written in capitals only, with no spaces between words
and no accents: the **uncial** script. Their sigma was the round *lunate* sigma, Ϲ. So John 3:8's
πνεῦμα appears as ΠΝΕΥΜΑ. The scribes also wrote sacred names short, with a line over them: ΘϹ for
θεός (God), ΙϹ for Ἰησοῦς (Jesus), and ΠΝΑ for πνεῦμα when it means the Spirit. The word cards show
each word in uncial capitals beneath the modern print.
