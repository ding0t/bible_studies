# In Humility

Two women in the church at Philippi could not get along. Paul names them in his letter: "I entreat
Euodia and I entreat Syntyche to agree in the Lord" (Philippians 4:2, ESV). Some who preached Christ
in his own city did it "out of selfish ambition" (Philippians 1:17, ESV). Paul was in prison, and
the church he loved was pulling apart.

He could have given them a rule. He gave them a person. "Have this mind among yourselves, which is
yours in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 2:5, ESV), and then he told them what Jesus did.

**In one sentence:** Humility is the mind of Christ Jesus, who was God and humbled Himself to death
on a cross, and whom the Father exalted; so you are to see yourself truly before God, count others
more significant than yourself, and leave your lifting up to Him, who dwells with the lowly, gives
grace to the humble, and sees through the humility people put on for show.

## Key Takeaways

*(This section follows the [Key Takeaways](../about/key-takeaways.md) format — see that page for what each part is for.)*

### Types & Prophecy

- **Prophecy fulfilled.** Zechariah saw Israel's king coming "humble and mounted on a donkey"
  (Zechariah 9:9, ESV). Matthew says that was fulfilled when Jesus rode into Jerusalem (Matthew
  21:5).
- **Prophecy applied to Jesus.** The LORD swore, "To me every knee shall bow" (Isaiah 45:23, ESV).
  Paul says every knee will bow at the name of Jesus (Philippians 2:10).

### Lessons about Jesus

- **Jesus humbled Himself.** He was in the form of God, took the form of a servant, and "humbled
  himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross" (Philippians 2:8, ESV).
- **Jesus is gentle and lowly in heart.** He says so Himself, and He says it as the reason to come
  to Him (Matthew 11:29).
- **Jesus washed feet.** The Lord and Teacher took a slave's task at His last supper and said, "I have
  given you an example" (John 13:15, ESV).
- **The Father exalted Him.** Because He went all the way down, God gave Him "the name that is above
  every name" (Philippians 2:9, ESV).

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** Think of yourself truly. Everything you have, you received (1 Corinthians 4:7). That
  settles both boasting and despair.
- **Attitude.** Leave your lifting up to God. Moses did not answer the people who spoke against him,
  and God answered for him (Numbers 12:1-8).
- **Do.** This week, take one task nobody wants, for someone who cannot repay you, and tell no one.
  That is what Jesus did with a towel (John 13:4-5).

## What the words mean

### A slave's word

The Greek adjective is ταπεινός (*tapeinos*, tah-pay-NOSS, G5011). Its first sense is "low-lying,"
like ground. Used of people, it meant low in status. In Greek writers before the New Testament it was
usually an insult, the word for a servile, abject person. Abbott-Smith's lexicon puts it plainly: in
classical Greek "usually in a slighting sense," in the New Testament "in an honourable sense."

The noun Paul uses in Philippians 2:3 is ταπεινοφροσύνη (*tapeinophrosynē*, G5012), "lowliness of
mind." It joins *tapeinos* to φρήν (*phrēn*, G5424), the mind. Abbott-Smith notes it is rare outside the New
Testament, and where Josephus and Epictetus use it, it is a fault.

It occurs seven times in the New Testament. Five name the real thing (Acts 20:19; Ephesians 4:2;
Philippians 2:3; Colossians 3:12; 1 Peter 5:5). Two name a counterfeit (Colossians 2:18, 23), which
this study comes to [below](#humility-for-show).

So the apostles took a word for the bottom of society and made it a virtue. They could do it because
the Lord had gone there first.

### The poor who depend on God

The Old Testament's word is <span dir="rtl">עָנָו</span> (*ʿanav*, ah-NAHV, H6035). It sits beside
<span dir="rtl">עָנִי</span> (*ʿani*, H6041), "poor, afflicted." The two overlap so closely that the
Hebrew text of Proverbs 3:34 is written with one and read aloud with the other. The *ʿanav* is someone
low enough to have only God to rely on. The anointed one of Isaiah 61:1 brings them good news, and Jesus read that verse as His own (Luke
4:18), and
the psalms are full of their prayers.

### Moses, who did not answer

The word's most famous use is about a man. Miriam and Aaron spoke against Moses: "Has the LORD indeed
spoken only through Moses?" (Numbers 12:2, ESV). Then the narrator stops the story:

> ✝️ Numbers 12:3 (ESV)
>
> 3 Now the man Moses was very meek, more than all people who were on the face of the earth.

"Meek" is *ʿanav*. What follows shows what it means. Moses says nothing in his own defence. The LORD
calls the three of them out, and the LORD answers: "Why then were you not afraid to speak against my
servant Moses?" (Numbers 12:8, ESV). The *ESV Study Bible* says the word's focus here is on Moses'
attitude (note on Numbers 12:3-4).

When the Septuagint translated *ʿanav* in Numbers 12:3, it chose πραΰς (*praus*, G4239), "gentle."
That is the word Jesus used of Himself.

## The mind of Christ

### The command

Paul opens with an appeal to what the Philippians already have in Christ: encouragement, love, the
fellowship of the Holy Spirit (Philippians 2:1). Then he asks for one mind.

> ✝️ Philippians 2:3-4 (ESV)
>
> 3 Do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility count others more significant than
> yourselves. 4 Let each of you look not only to his own interests, but also to the interests of
> others.

"Conceit" is κενοδοξία (*kenodoxia*, G2754), literally "empty glory." It is found only here in the
New Testament. "Selfish ambition" is ἐριθεία (*eritheia*, G2052), the word Paul has just used of the
rival preachers (Philippians 1:17).

### The same words, in Jesus

Then Paul tells the story of Christ, and he tells it in the words of the command.

> ✝️ Philippians 2:5-11 (ESV)
>
> 5 Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, 6 who, though he was in the form
> of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, 7 but emptied himself, by taking the
> form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. 8 And being found in human form, he humbled
> himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. 9 Therefore God has
> highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, 10 so that at the name of
> Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, 11 and every tongue confess
> that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.

Set the two passages side by side in Greek, and three of the command's words come back:

| The command (Philippians 2:3) | What Jesus did (Philippians 2:6-8) |
|---|---|
| "count" others more significant, ἡγούμενοι (*hēgeomai*, G2233) | did not "count" equality with God a thing to be grasped, ἡγήσατο |
| not from "empty glory," *keno*-doxia | "emptied" himself, ἐκένωσεν (*ekenōsen*, G2758) |
| in "humility," *tapeino*-phrosynē | "humbled" himself, ἐταπείνωσεν (*etapeinōsen*, G5013) |

The Philippians' command is what Jesus did first, and He did it at the greatest possible cost. He had the highest claim anyone has ever
had, and He counted it as something not to hold on to for His own advantage. He was in the form of
God and took "the form of a servant," δοῦλος (*doulos*, G1401), a slave. The *NIV Cultural
Backgrounds Study Bible* observes that people of status in the ancient world barely ate with their
servants, much less acted like one (note on Philippians 2:7).

"Emptied himself" has been read to mean that the Son gave up His divine nature or some of its powers.
The context points elsewhere. Verse 7 says how He emptied Himself: "by taking the form of a servant."
The *ESV Study Bible* says Paul is not saying Christ became less than God (note on Philippians 2:7).
He added a human nature and laid aside His rights. This site's [statement of
faith](../about/statement-of-faith.md) holds the same: "He emptied Himself to take on humanity while
remaining fully God."

### Therefore God exalted Him

The hinge of the passage is "therefore" (Philippians 2:9). Jesus went down, and the Father lifted Him
up.

In Isaiah 45 the LORD says, "I am God, and
there is no other," and swears, "To me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear allegiance"
(Isaiah 45:22-23, ESV). Paul quotes that oath elsewhere of God (Romans 14:11). Here he gives the
bowing knee and the confessing tongue to Jesus. The one who humbled Himself furthest receives the
worship the LORD swore would be His alone.

So this shows that God exalts the lowly, and He did it first and supremely for His Son. For Jesus,
the way down was the road to glory. Paul draws that conclusion for the
Philippians at once: "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in
you" (Philippians 2:12-13, ESV).

He then gives them three people who lived it. Timothy looks after their interests (Philippians
2:20-21). Epaphroditus nearly died serving them (Philippians 2:25-30). And Paul himself, who uses
*hēgeomai* three more times of his own life: "whatever gain I had, I counted as loss for the sake of
Christ" (Philippians 3:7, ESV).

## Gentle and lowly

### The King on a donkey

Jesus once described His own heart, and He chose these two words:

> ✝️ Matthew 11:28-30 (ESV)
>
> 28 Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. 29 Take my yoke upon
> you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls.
> 30 For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light."

"Gentle" is *praus*, Moses' word in the Greek of Numbers 12:3. "Lowly" is *tapeinos*. His lowliness
is the reason He is safe to come to. A proud master would load the burden on. This one carries it.

When He came to Jerusalem to die, He came the way Zechariah said Israel's King would come:
"righteous and having salvation is he, humble and mounted on a donkey" (Zechariah 9:9, ESV). Matthew
quotes it, "Behold, your king is coming to you, humble" (Matthew 21:5, ESV), using *praus* again.

### The towel

John sets the scene of the last supper carefully. Jesus knew "that the Father had given all things
into his hands, and that he had come from God and was going back to God" (John 13:3, ESV). Knowing
all of that, He got up.

> ✝️ John 13:4-5 (ESV)
>
> 4 rose from supper. He laid aside his outer garments, and taking a towel, tied it around his waist.
> 5 Then he poured water into a basin and began to wash the disciples' feet and to wipe them with the
> towel that was wrapped around him.

Washing feet was a servant's job. The *ESV Study Bible* notes it was reserved for non-Jewish slaves
(note on John 13:1-17). Feet walked dirt roads in sandals. Jesus washed all twelve pairs, including
the feet of the man who would betray Him that night (John 13:11).

Then He explained it:

> ✝️ John 13:14-15 (ESV)
>
> 14 If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's
> feet. 15 For I have given you an example, that you also should do just as I have done to you.

His humility came from knowing who He was. He knew He was Lord and
Teacher, and He used the knowledge to kneel. He had told the Twelve the same thing on the road: "the
Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark
10:45, ESV). The towel was a picture of the cross.

## Before God

### Where God lives

Humility begins with God's greatness, because only a great God can be humbled before. Isaiah says it
in one verse:

> ✝️ Isaiah 57:15 (ESV)
>
> 15 For thus says the One who is high and lifted up, who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy: "I
> dwell in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit, to revive
> the spirit of the lowly, and to revive the heart of the contrite.

God has two addresses. One is "the high and holy place." The other is the person who is
<span dir="rtl">דַּכָּא</span> (*dakka*, H1793), "crushed," and <span dir="rtl">שְׁפַל־רוּחַ</span>
(*shephal ruach*, H8217), "low of spirit." So this shows that God, the Holy One who inhabits
eternity, chooses to live with people who know they are low, and He comes to them to give them life.
Isaiah says it again at the end of the book: "this is the one to whom I will look: he who is humble
and contrite in spirit and trembles at my word" (Isaiah 66:2, ESV).

### Walk humbly with your God

Micah answers the question every religious person asks: what does God want from me? The worshipper
offers calves, rams, rivers of oil, even his firstborn (Micah 6:6-7). Micah answers:

> ✝️ Micah 6:8 (ESV)
>
> 8 He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice,
> and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?

"Walk humbly" is <span dir="rtl">הַצְנֵעַ לֶכֶת</span> (*hatsneaʿ lekhet*, H6800). This verb occurs
nowhere else in the Old Testament. Its root appears once more, as an adjective, in Proverbs 11:2:
"with the humble is wisdom" (ESV).

**The meaning of this word is contested.** Every English version compared for this study reads
"humbly." The *NIV
Biblical Theology Study Bible* notes that newer study of the word argues for "prudently" (note on
Micah 6:8), and the LSB's footnote offers "circumspectly." On that reading, Micah asks for a careful,
attentive walk with God. The two readings share a lot: a person who walks carefully with God is
paying attention to Him. This study keeps the traditional "humbly," and does
not rest any argument on this word alone. The rest of Scripture says the same thing without it.

### Humble yourself, and He will lift you up

James and Peter both quote the same proverb, and both quote it from the Greek Old Testament:

> ✝️ James 4:6-7, 10 (ESV)
>
> 6 But he gives more grace. Therefore it says, "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the
> humble." 7 Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. ...
> 10 Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will exalt you.

> ✝️ 1 Peter 5:5-7 (ESV)
>
> 5 Likewise, you who are younger, be subject to the elders. Clothe yourselves, all of you, with
> humility toward one another, for "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble." 6 Humble
> yourselves, therefore, under the mighty hand of God so that at the proper time he may exalt you,
> 7 casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you.

The proverb is Proverbs 3:34. The Septuagint reads κύριος ὑπερηφάνοις ἀντιτάσσεται, "the Lord opposes
the proud," and James and Peter both write ὁ θεός, "God." "Opposes" is ἀντιτάσσω
(*antitassō*, G498), which Abbott-Smith glosses "to range in battle against." God sets Himself against the proud person, and He gives grace to the humble.

Peter joins humility to anxiety. The humble person hands their worries to God "because he cares for
you" (1 Peter 5:7). Pride carries its own load because it will not admit it needs help. Humility is
trust.

Both apostles end with the same promise: humble yourself, and God will lift you up. Jesus said it
three times, to three audiences (Matthew 23:12; Luke 14:11; 18:14): "everyone who exalts himself will
be humbled, but the one who humbles himself will be exalted" (Luke 18:14, ESV). It is exactly what
the Father did for Jesus in Philippians 2:9.

### When God humbles

Sometimes God does the humbling. Moses reminded Israel of forty years in the wilderness: "he humbled
you and let you hunger and fed you with manna ... that he might make you know that man does not live
by bread alone" (Deuteronomy 8:3, ESV). God humbled them to teach them to depend on Him. Hard seasons
can be that same kindness. If you are low today, you are where God has promised to dwell.

## In yourself

### A way of thinking

*Tapeinophrosynē* is built on the word for "mind," and Paul's commands about humility keep coming
back to thinking. "Have this mind" (Philippians 2:5) is φρονεῖτε (*phroneite*, G5426). Romans 12:3
uses words built on that root four times in one sentence:

> ✝️ Romans 12:3 (ESV)
>
> 3 For by the grace given to me I say to everyone among you not to think of himself more highly than
> he ought to think, but to think with sober judgment, each according to the measure of faith that God
> has assigned.

Humility here is accurate thinking. Paul does not ask you to think of yourself as worthless. He asks
you to think "with sober judgment," to measure yourself by what God has given. He asks one question
that settles it: "What do you have that you did not receive? If then you received it, why do you
boast as if you did not receive it?" (1 Corinthians 4:7, ESV). Your gifts are real, and they are
gifts. You can name them without pride, and you can use them without shame.

### Like a child, and grown up

When the disciples asked who was greatest, Jesus stood a child in front of them. "Whoever humbles
himself like this child is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 18:4, ESV). A child in
that world had no status to protect.

Paul adds the other half to a church that was proud of its spiritual gifts:

> ✝️ 1 Corinthians 14:20 (ESV)
>
> 20 Brothers, do not be children in your thinking. Be infants in evil, but in your thinking be mature.

The KJV has "in understanding be men." Corinth prized speaking in tongues in church because it looked
spiritual, whether anyone understood or not. The *ESV Study Bible* calls that childish because it
does not care for the needs of others (note on 1 Corinthians 14:20). So a humble person is childlike
in the kingdom's sense: no status to guard, and ready to receive. And a humble person is grown up in
the mind: they think hard, and they think about what builds others up.

## Toward others

Every time the letters name humility as a virtue, it is aimed at other people.

- **Count others more significant.** "In humility count others more significant than yourselves"
  (Philippians 2:3, ESV). Verse 4 says how: look "to the interests of others." Treat their interests
  as weighing more than yours, the way Jesus treated ours.
- **Bear with one another.** "With all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another
  in love" (Ephesians 4:2, ESV). In Ephesians and Colossians, humility comes with patience, because the
  people you are humble toward will try it.
- **Put it on.** "Put on then, as God's chosen ones, holy and beloved, compassionate hearts, kindness,
  humility, meekness, and patience" (Colossians 3:12, ESV). It is a garment, put on every day. Peter's
  "clothe yourselves" says the same (1 Peter 5:5).
- **Go where the lowly are.** "Do not be haughty, but associate with the lowly. Never be wise in your
  own sight" (Romans 12:16, ESV).
- **Wash feet.** "You also ought to wash one another's feet" (John 13:14, ESV). The foot washing is a
  custom of one time and place. The act it pictures, taking the lowest task for someone else's good,
  belongs to every time and place.

## Humility for show

### The same word at Colossae

Two of the seven uses of *tapeinophrosynē* describe something God rejects. Teachers at Colossae were
telling believers they fell short: they had not fasted enough, kept the right days, or seen the right
visions.

> ✝️ Colossians 2:18 (ESV)
>
> 18 Let no one disqualify you, insisting on asceticism and worship of angels, going on in detail
> about visions, puffed up without reason by his sensuous mind,

"Asceticism" is *tapeinophrosynē*. The *ESV Study Bible* says so, and suggests Paul had fasting in
mind (note on Colossians 2:18). The *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* adds that fasting, a long
way of humbling oneself, was also used by some to seek revelations (note on Colossians 2:18).

In the same sentence the man practising "humility" is "puffed up,"
φυσιούμενος (*physioō*, G5448). The verb means to inflate, like a bellows. Six of its seven New
Testament uses are in 1 Corinthians, the letter to the church proud of its gifts (1 Corinthians 4:6,
18, 19; 5:2; 8:1; 13:4). "Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up" (1 Corinthians 8:1, ESV).

Four verses later, Paul gives his verdict on the whole system:

> ✝️ Colossians 2:23 (ESV)
>
> 23 These have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and
> severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh.

"Self-made religion" is ἐθελοθρησκία (*ethelothrēskia*, G1479), found only here: worship a person
chooses for themselves. The humility costs real effort and real hardship, and it has "an appearance of
wisdom." But it is self-chosen, it ranks other believers, and it leaves the person inflated. And it
cannot do the one thing it claims: it has no power against the flesh.

### How to tell them apart

Scripture commends fasting (see [Fasting](fasting.md)). The difference at Colossae is where the
practice points. The false kind points to the person doing it: it keeps count, it compares, and it
judges others as "disqualified." The true kind points away from the self, to God and to other people.
Paul's answer to Colossae was Christ. "Not holding fast to the Head" (Colossians 2:19, ESV) is the
root of the problem, and a few verses later the same word
*tapeinophrosynē* is a garment the chosen people of God put on (Colossians 3:12).

Jesus gave the same test: "Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be
seen by them" (Matthew 6:1, ESV). Humility that needs to be noticed has become pride.

### What your English Bible shows

Most English versions hide the word at Colossians 2:18 and 2:23.

| Version | Colossians 2:18 |
|---|---|
| ESV | "asceticism" |
| CSB | "ascetic practices" |
| NASB 1995, LSB | "self-abasement" (LSB footnote: "Or humility") |
| NIV, NKJV | "false humility" |
| NASB 2020 | "humility" |

Every one of these is a fair translation of what the teachers were doing. But only the NASB 2020 lets
an English reader see that Paul used the same word here as in Philippians 2:3, and that is the
warning: the counterfeit uses the real thing's name.

## The opposite: pride

Pride is the sin humility answers, and Scripture treats it as deep. Jesus lists it among the evils
that come "from within, out of the heart of man" (Mark 7:21-22, ESV). The word there is ὑπερηφανία
(*hyperēphania*, G5243), which occurs only in that verse in the New Testament. God "opposes" it
(James 4:6). "Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall" (Proverbs 16:18,
ESV).

Where pride comes from, how to recognise it, and the question of whether Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28
describe the fall of Satan belong to a study of their own: [Pride](../sin/pride.md).

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** *Tapeinos* was usually an insult in Greek before the New Testament. Why would
   the apostles choose a word for low status to name a Christian virtue?
2. **The text in its context.** Paul answers a quarrel in Philippi (Philippians 4:2) with the story of
   the incarnation. What does Philippians 2:6-8 say to two believers who cannot agree?
3. **Christ.** Jesus washed feet *because* He knew the Father had given all things into His hands
   (John 13:3). How does knowing who you are in Christ make humility easier?
4. **Before God.** Isaiah 57:15 gives God two dwelling places. What would change if you believed your
   lowest days are where He has promised to live?
5. **The hard part.** The man at Colossae practised "humility" and was "puffed up" (Colossians 2:18).
   Where might your own disciplines have become a way of measuring yourself against others?
6. **Be transformed.** Whose interests could you count as more significant than your own this week,
   and what would it cost?
