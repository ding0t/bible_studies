# First Take the Log Out

Jesus told a joke you can draw. A man with a roof beam sticking out of his eye leans in close to
his brother and offers to remove a splinter. The disciples on the hillside could picture it. They
could laugh at it. Then they could feel where it landed, because Jesus had just said, "Judge not,
that you be not judged" (Matthew 7:1, ESV), and the joke is His reason.

> ✝️ Matthew 7:3-5 (ESV)
>
> 3 Why do you see the speck that is in your brother's eye, but do not notice the log that is in your
> own eye? 4 Or how can you say to your brother, 'Let me take the speck out of your eye,' when there
> is the log in your own eye? 5 You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you
> will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye.

**In one sentence:** God alone is the Judge, and He measures you with your own measure; so Jesus
tells a carpenter's joke to say it: let God take the log out of your own eye first, by confession
and His mercy, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's, gently, as one
forgiven.

## Key Takeaways

### Lessons about Jesus

- **Jesus used humour, and aimed it at His friends.** The beam and the speck (Matthew 7:3-5) and the
  gnat and the camel (Matthew 23:24) both get their laugh from size. In Matthew 7 the punchline lands
  on His own disciples: "You hypocrite" (Matthew 7:5, ESV).
- **Jesus judges by authority the Father gave Him, and seeks the Father's will as He does it.** "As I
  hear, I judge, and my judgment is just, because I seek not my own will but the will of him who sent
  me" (John 5:30, ESV). The Judge of all the earth shows you how to judge.
- **Jesus gives clear sight.** The verb behind "see clearly" in Matthew 7:5 describes the blind man
  at Bethsaida after Jesus touched his eyes a second time (Mark 8:25).
- **Jesus bore the judgment you deserved.** "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who
  are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1, ESV).

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** God alone is the Judge, and in His Son Jesus He has already judged your sin. "For our
  sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of
  God" (2 Corinthians 5:21, ESV). You speak to a brother's fault as one who has been acquitted.
- **Attitude.** Come to every fault you notice as a fellow patient. "Keep watch on yourself, lest you
  too be tempted" (Galatians 6:1, ESV). Grieve over sin before you speak about it (1 Corinthians
  5:2).
- **Do.** This week, before you raise anyone's fault, pray Psalm 139:23-24 over yourself: "Search
  me, O God, and know my heart! ... And see if there be any grievous way in me" (ESV). Confess to Him
  what He shows you (1 John 1:9). Then go to your brother alone, and gently (Matthew 18:15).

## Judge not, in its sermon

Matthew sets the scene. "Seeing the crowds, he went up on the mountain, and when he sat down, his
disciples came to him" (Matthew 5:1, ESV). Jesus sat to teach, as He did in the synagogue at
Nazareth (Luke 4:20). His disciples are the first audience, and the crowds listen in (Matthew 7:28).

Matthew 7:1 opens with no connecting word: Μὴ κρίνετε, "Judge not". Three things Jesus has just
said meet in the next five verses:

- **The eye.** "The eye is the lamp of the body" (Matthew 6:22, ESV).
- **The hypocrite.** Three times in chapter 6 Jesus warned against acting "as the hypocrites do"
  (Matthew 6:2, ESV; 6:5, 16).
- **Forgiveness.** "If you do not forgive others their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive
  your trespasses" (Matthew 6:15, ESV).

The man in Matthew 7:3-5 has something wrong with his eye, is called a hypocrite, and is about to be
measured by his own measure. The joke gathers up the chapter before it.

κρίνω (*krinō*, G2919) is a broad word: it can mean to decide, to judge or to condemn, and the New
Testament uses it 115 times. Luke's parallel fixes its sense here by pairing it with a narrower verb:

> ✝️ Luke 6:37-38 (ESV)
>
> 37 "Judge not, and you will not be judged; condemn not, and you will not be condemned; forgive, and
> you will be forgiven; 38 give, and it will be given to you. Good measure, pressed down, shaken
> together, running over, will be put into your lap. For with the measure you use it will be
> measured back to you."

καταδικάζω (*katadikazō*, G2613) is the court's verdict against a person: "condemn". The judging
Jesus forbids is the verdict one disciple passes on another from a bench he has set up for himself.

Many readers arrive at Matthew 7:1 holding a version of it that forbids every moral evaluation.
Jesus' next sentences rule that version out. Matthew 7:6 needs the disciple to tell "dogs" and
"pigs" from those who will receive what is holy. Matthew 7:16 says of false prophets, "You will
recognize them by their fruits" (ESV). And John 7:24 opens with the same two Greek words as Matthew
7:1, μὴ κρίνετε, then finishes: "Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment"
(John 7:24, ESV). How to test a teacher without turning suspicious of every brother is worked
through in [Test the Spirits](test-the-spirits.md).

Luke sets the saying in a different frame. After "give, and it will be given to you", "He also told
them a parable: 'Can a blind man lead a blind man? Will they not both fall into a pit? A disciple is
not above his teacher, but everyone when he is fully trained will be like his teacher'" (Luke
6:39-40, ESV). Then comes the speck. In Luke the man with the log is a blind guide offering to lead,
and the remedy is to be trained by the Teacher until you see as He sees.

## The measure you use

"For with the judgment you pronounce you will be judged, and with the measure you use it will be
measured to you" (Matthew 7:2, ESV). Both verbs are passive, and the sermon names who acts. Your
Father "who sees in secret will reward you" (Matthew 6:4, ESV; 6:6, 18), and "your heavenly Father
will forgive you" (Matthew 6:14, ESV). The Judge of Matthew 7:2 is God. James says it outright:
"There is only one lawgiver and judge, he who is able to save and to destroy. But who are you to
judge your neighbor?" (James 4:12, ESV).

μέτρον (*metron*, G3358) is a measure, the container a merchant filled with grain. Jesus says God
uses your own container on you. Luke shows the same rule running toward generosity: the measure is
"pressed down, shaken together, running over" (Luke 6:38, ESV). So the verse is a warning to the
harsh and a promise to the merciful. "Mercy triumphs over judgment" (James 2:13, ESV).

The saying was at home in Jewish speech. The Mishnah, written down about AD 200, gives it as a rule:
"With the measure that a person measures, he is measured with it"
(<span dir="rtl">בַּמִדָּה שֶׁאָדָם מוֹדֵד, בָּהּ מוֹדְדִין לוֹ</span>, *Mishnah Sotah* 1:7). That
shows the proverb was common. It does not tell you where Jesus took it from, and His use of it
makes God the one holding the measure.

### What "you will be judged" means for a believer

This is where readers disagree, and the texts give reasons on both sides.

- **For a believer: discipline now, and assessment at the judgment seat.** "There is therefore now
  no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1, ESV). Paul still expects God to
  deal with His children: "But if we judged ourselves truly, we would not be judged. But when we are
  judged by the Lord, we are disciplined so that we may not be condemned along with the world"
  (1 Corinthians 11:31-32, ESV). And every believer will "appear before the judgment seat of Christ"
  (2 Corinthians 5:10, ESV). On this reading, Matthew 7:2 tells a disciple that God will hold him to
  the standard he holds others to, as discipline now and at the judgment seat for reward. This study
  holds that reading; [The Judgment Seat of Christ](../last-things/judgment-seat-of-christ.md) works
  through what is assessed there.
- **For a merciless heart: something worse.** Jesus' parable of the unforgiving servant ends, "So
  also my heavenly Father will do to every one of you, if you do not forgive your brother from your
  heart" (Matthew 18:35, ESV), and James says "judgment is without mercy to one who has shown no
  mercy" (James 2:13, ESV). A life of merciless verdicts can show a heart that never received mercy
  at all. Matthew 7:21-23 describes people who said "Lord, Lord" and were never known by Him.

The two readings agree that God is the Judge and that harshness toward a brother is serious. The
second does not threaten anyone who is truly in Christ; it warns the person whose harshness shows
they have never come to Him. [Forgive Us Our Debts](forgiveness.md) works through the debt God
cancels and why the forgiven forgive.

## A carpenter's joke

The two objects in Jesus' picture are as unequal as He could make them.

- **The speck.** κάρφος (*karphos*, G2595) is a dry stalk, a chip or a twig. The Greek Old
  Testament uses it once, when Noah's dove comes back "and behold, in her mouth was a freshly plucked
  olive leaf" (Genesis 8:11, ESV), where the Septuagint has φύλλον ἐλαίας κάρφος, "an olive leaf, a
  twig".
- **The beam.** δοκός (*dokos*, G1385) is a timber that holds up a roof. In the Septuagint it
  translates the Hebrew <span dir="rtl">קוֹרָה</span> (*qorah*, H6982): "the beams of our house are
  cedar" (Song of Songs 1:17, ESV), and the timber the sons of the prophets went to the Jordan to
  cut for a house, where "as one was felling a log, his axe head fell into the water" (2 Kings 6:5,
  ESV).

In the New Testament both words appear only in this saying: Matthew 7:3-5 and Luke 6:41-42. A beam is
something two men carry. Jesus puts one in an eye, and makes the man wearing it fuss over a twig.

The laugh comes from size, and Jesus used the same shape again: "You blind guides, straining out a
gnat and swallowing a camel!" (Matthew 23:24, ESV). Both pictures show someone careful about the
smallest thing while missing the largest. The *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* says of the
speck and the plank that the image "would likely draw laughter—and provoke thought" (note on Matthew
7:3-5). The Gospels do not record the crowd's reaction, so take the laughter as likely.

The people of Nazareth called Jesus "the carpenter" (Mark 6:3, ESV), τέκτων (*tektōn*, G5045), a
builder in wood or stone, and "the carpenter's son" (Matthew 13:55, ESV). A builder knows what a
roof beam weighs and what a splinter costs. That Jesus took the picture from the workshop is likely;
the *ESV Study Bible* says He "may have" (note on Matthew 7:3-5). The joke works on size whether He
did or not.

### The rabbis' splinter and beam

Within a century of Jesus' ministry, Rabbi Tarfon used the same picture: "I would be surprised if
there is anyone in this generation who can receive rebuke. Why? Because if the one rebuking says to
him: Remove the splinter from between your eyes, … the other says to him: Remove the beam from
between your eyes" (Babylonian Talmud, *Arakhin* 16b, William Davidson edition). The two commands
are Hebrew, <span dir="rtl">טוֹל קֵיסָם מִבֵּין עֵינֶיךָ</span> and
<span dir="rtl">טוֹל קוֹרָה מִבֵּין עֵינֶיךָ</span>, and the beam is the same
<span dir="rtl">קוֹרָה</span> as 2 Kings 6. In Tarfon's saying the man being corrected throws the
beam back to escape the rebuke. Jesus hands the beam to the one doing the correcting, so that the
correction can happen.

### What English versions do with it

The ESV, NASB and LSB print "speck" and "log". The BSB and KJV
print "beam" ("mote" for the speck, in the KJV). The CSB prints "splinter" and "beam of wood". The NIV
prints "the speck of sawdust" and "the plank", and its "of sawdust" adds a workshop reading that
κάρφος does not carry: its one Septuagint use is an olive twig. "Beam" is closest to δοκός. "Log"
has one gain in the ESV: it calls the timber of 2 Kings 6:5 "a log" too, so an ESV reader meets the
same English word where the Greek has the same δοκός.

## Why you cannot see your own log

Matthew 7:3 uses two different verbs. You "see" the speck: βλέπεις, from βλέπω, ordinary looking. You
do not "notice" the log: οὐ κατανοεῖς, from κατανοέω (*katanoeō*, G2657), to look closely and
consider, as in "Consider the ravens" (Luke 12:24, ESV). The KJV keeps that sense with "considerest
not the beam". The man glances at his brother and never studies himself.

James uses the same verb for a man in front of a mirror:

> ✝️ James 1:23-25 (ESV)
>
> 23 For if anyone is a hearer of the word and not a doer, he is like a man who looks intently at his
> natural face in a mirror. 24 For he looks at himself and goes away and at once forgets what he was
> like. 25 But the one who looks into the perfect law, the law of liberty, and perseveres, being no
> hearer who forgets but a doer who acts, he will be blessed in his doing.

"Looks intently" in James 1:23 and "looks" in 1:24 are κατανοέω. The ESV's three English verbs
(notice, looks intently, looks) hide that Matthew and James use one word. James gives the cure for the
unnoticed log: keep looking into the word, and do what it shows you.

A log cannot be found by the eye it is in. David knew it: "Who can discern his errors? Declare me
innocent from hidden faults" (Psalm 19:12, ESV). So he asked God to look for him: "Search me, O God,
and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead
me in the way everlasting!" (Psalm 139:23-24, ESV).

Then Jesus names the man. ὑποκριτής (*hypokritēs*, G5273) was the Greek word for a stage actor.
Matthew uses it thirteen times, and in chapter 6 the hypocrites were other people, praying on street
corners and disfiguring their faces to be seen (Matthew 6:2, 5, 16). In twelve of the thirteen the
word is plural. At Matthew 7:5 it is singular, ὑποκριτά, and it is spoken to the listener: "You
hypocrite". (Luke 6:42 has the singular too.) The actor in this scene is playing the eye doctor.

## You are the man

The Old Testament tells this story about its best king.

David had taken Uriah's wife and arranged Uriah's death (2 Samuel 11). "And the LORD sent Nathan to
David" (2 Samuel 12:1, ESV), with a story about a rich man who had "very many flocks and herds" and a
poor man who "had nothing but one little ewe lamb" (2 Samuel 12:2-3, ESV). The rich man took the
lamb to feed a guest.

David's verdict was instant:

> ✝️ 2 Samuel 12:5-7 (ESV)
>
> 5 Then David's anger was greatly kindled against the man, and he said to Nathan, "As the LORD
> lives, the man who has done this deserves to die, 6 and he shall restore the lamb fourfold, because
> he did this thing, and because he had no pity." 7 Nathan said to David, "You are the man!..."

In Hebrew Nathan's sentence is two words: <span dir="rtl">אַתָּה הָאִישׁ</span> (*attah ha'ish*).
David judged the lamb-thief correctly and severely. His eye for wrong was sharp, and it pointed
outward. The speck he condemned was a small copy of his own beam: a rich man taking what belonged to a
poor man.

Judah did the same with Tamar. Told that she was pregnant, he said, "Bring her out, and let her be
burned" (Genesis 38:24, ESV). Shown his own signet, cord and staff, he said, "She is more righteous
than I" (Genesis 38:26, ESV).

Paul states the pattern plainly: "in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself, because you,
the judge, practice the very same things" (Romans 2:1, ESV), and "you then who teach others, do you
not teach yourself?" (Romans 2:21, ESV).

From these stories and Romans 2:1 it seems likely that the fault you spot fastest in someone else is
often a chip off your own beam. That is an inference from Scripture's examples. Jesus' saying does
not state it, but both stories show it.

Then the beam came out. "David said to Nathan, 'I have sinned against the LORD.' And Nathan said to
David, 'The LORD also has put away your sin; you shall not die'" (2 Samuel 12:13, ESV).

## First, then

"First take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your
brother's eye" (Matthew 7:5, ESV). The Greek is πρῶτον… καὶ τότε, "first… and then". Jesus used the
same shape earlier in this sermon: "First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your
gift" (Matthew 5:24, ESV). Both times the word "brother" is there, and both times the order is the
command. Jesus keeps the speck-removal. He puts it second.

David lived the order. Psalm 51 is titled "A Psalm of David, when Nathan the prophet went to him,
after he had gone in to Bathsheba" (Psalm 51:1, ESV, title). The psalm asks, "Create in me a clean
heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me" (Psalm 51:10, ESV). Only after that does David
promise anything to others: "Then I will teach transgressors your ways, and sinners will return to
you" (Psalm 51:13, ESV). The man whose beam God removed became a teacher of sinners.

The verb for "see clearly" is διαβλέπω (*diablepō*, G1227). It appears three times in the New
Testament: Matthew 7:5, Luke 6:42 and Mark 8:25. In Mark it describes a blind man at Bethsaida.
After Jesus' first touch he said, "I see people, but they look like trees, walking" (Mark 8:24,
ESV). After the second, "he opened his eyes, his sight was restored, and he saw everything clearly"
(Mark 8:25, ESV). Mark and Matthew are telling different stories, and neither points to the other.
Still, the word says something true: clear sight is what Jesus' hands give.

An eye is the tenderest place on the body to work, and the *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible*
notes that ancient eye surgery was delicate work that could blind a patient (note on Matthew 7:3-5).
Paul gives the surgeon's rules:

> ✝️ Galatians 6:1 (ESV)
>
> 1 Brothers, if anyone is caught in any transgression, you who are spiritual should restore him in a
> spirit of gentleness. Keep watch on yourself, lest you too be tempted.

"Restore" is the goal, and Jesus names the same goal: "If he listens to you, you have gained your
brother" (Matthew 18:15, ESV). [Hurt by the Church](hurt-by-the-church.md#the-road-back) works
through the road back between believers who have wronged each other.

## When Scripture tells you to judge

The Jesus who said "Judge not" also said "judge with right judgment" (John 7:24, ESV). Paul told
Corinth "do not pronounce judgment before the time" (1 Corinthians 4:5, ESV), and a chapter later
asked, "Is it not those inside the church whom you are to judge?" (1 Corinthians 5:12, ESV). Scripture
hands believers some judgments and keeps others for God.

| Scripture tells you to judge | Scripture keeps for God |
|---|---|
| Sin inside the church, step by step: alone, with witnesses, then the church (Matthew 18:15-17; 1 Corinthians 5:12) | Those outside: "God judges those outside" (1 Corinthians 5:13, ESV) |
| Disputes between believers, settled by someone "wise enough" (1 Corinthians 6:5, ESV) | The heart's motives, until the Lord "will bring to light the things now hidden in darkness" (1 Corinthians 4:5, ESV) |
| Teachers and spirits: "test the spirits to see whether they are from God" (1 John 4:1, ESV); "test everything" (1 Thessalonians 5:21, ESV) | Another master's servant: "It is before his own master that he stands or falls" (Romans 14:4, ESV) |
| Fruit: "You will recognize them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:16, ESV) | The final verdict on any person: "There is only one lawgiver and judge" (James 4:12, ESV) |

### Church discipline

Jesus gave the church a sequence: go to your brother alone; if he does not listen, take one or two
others; then "tell it to the church" (Matthew 18:15-17, ESV). [Where Two or Three Are
Gathered](../israel-and-church/where-two-or-three-are-gathered.md#three-steps) works through the
steps.

At Corinth a man had "his father's wife" (1 Corinthians 5:1, ESV), and the church had done nothing.
Paul's first charge was against the church's heart: "And you are arrogant! Ought you not rather to
mourn?" (1 Corinthians 5:2, ESV). The goal of the discipline he ordered was the man's rescue: "so
that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord" (1 Corinthians 5:5, ESV).

### Disputes between brothers

Corinthian believers were suing each other before pagan magistrates. Paul answered with the saints'
future: "Or do you not know that the saints will judge the world? And if the world is to be judged by
you, are you incompetent to try trivial cases? Do you not know that we are to judge angels? How much
more, then, matters pertaining to this life!" (1 Corinthians 6:2-3, ESV). He asked whether there was
"no one among you wise enough to settle a dispute between the brothers" (1 Corinthians 6:5, ESV). Then
he went further: "Why not rather suffer wrong? Why not rather be defrauded?" (1 Corinthians 6:7, ESV).

### The saints who will judge the world and angels

Paul's "Do you not know" assumes a teaching the church already had. Daniel saw it: "judgment was
given for the saints of the Most High, and the time came when the saints possessed the kingdom"
(Daniel 7:22, ESV). Jesus promised the Twelve, "in the new world, when the Son of Man will sit on his
glorious throne, you who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes
of Israel" (Matthew 19:28, ESV). John saw "thrones, and seated on them were those to whom the
authority to judge was committed" (Revelation 20:4, ESV), and they "reigned with Christ for a
thousand years". This site reads that as Christ's reign on the earth after His return; [Your Kingdom
Come](../last-things/your-kingdom-come.md#not-yet-complete) sets out the case.

Paul does not say which angels. The fallen angels whom God is keeping "until the judgment" (2 Peter
2:4, ESV; Jude 6) are the likeliest reading, and the text leaves it there.

Paul used this future to correct the present. The saints will judge angels, so a quarrel about money
is a small case. He was training them for the office. The training was humility: settle it among
yourselves, or suffer the wrong.

## Authority, a lowly heart, and wisdom from above

Three things mark the judging Scripture commands. The man with the beam has none of them.

### Authority given

Moses charged Israel's first judges: "You shall not be partial in judgment. You shall hear the small
and the great alike. You shall not be intimidated by anyone, for the judgment is God's" (Deuteronomy
1:17, ESV). King Jehoshaphat told his judges, "Consider what you do, for you judge not for man but
for the LORD. He is with you in giving judgment" (2 Chronicles 19:6, ESV). A judge in Israel held
God's authority on loan and answered to Him for it.

The Son holds judgment the same way. "The Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the
Son" (John 5:22, ESV), "and he has given him authority to execute judgment, because he is the Son of
Man" (John 5:27, ESV). Then Jesus tells you how He uses it:

> ✝️ John 5:30 (ESV)
>
> 30 "I can do nothing on my own. As I hear, I judge, and my judgment is just, because I seek not my
> own will but the will of him who sent me.

The saints' thrones are given too. In Revelation 20:4 the Greek is κρίμα ἐδόθη αὐτοῖς, "judgment was
given to them". The church judges sin among its members because Christ commissioned it to (Matthew
18:18), and it does so "when you are assembled in the name of the Lord Jesus" (1 Corinthians 5:4,
ESV). Authority to correct comes with a stricter account: "we who teach will be judged with greater
strictness" (James 3:1, ESV).

The man with the beam appointed himself. Nobody gave him the bench.

### A lowly heart

The Corinthians were πεφυσιωμένοι, "arrogant" in the ESV, from φυσιόω (*physioō*, G5448), "to puff
up", a word from the bellows ([Pride](../sin/pride.md#showing-off-smoke-and-swelling) traces it).
Paul says they should have mourned: πενθέω (*pentheō*, G3996), the verb of "Blessed are those who
mourn" (Matthew 5:4, ESV). The beatitude at the start of the Sermon on the Mount is the posture of a
church dealing with sin.

Pride judges from above a brother, and it enjoys the verdict. Humility kneels beside him, grieves,
and hopes. Paul tells Timothy to correct opponents "with gentleness. God may perhaps grant them
repentance leading to a knowledge of the truth" (2 Timothy 2:25, ESV). The humble corrector says what
is true and leaves the change to God, knowing he could be next: "Keep watch on yourself, lest
you too be tempted" (Galatians 6:1, ESV).

### Wisdom from above

Solomon began his reign by admitting he did not know how: "I am but a little child. I do not know how
to go out or come in" (1 Kings 3:7, ESV). He asked for one thing: "Give your servant therefore an
understanding mind to govern your people, that I may discern between good and evil" (1 Kings 3:9,
ESV). After he judged between the two mothers, Israel "perceived that the wisdom of God was in him to
do justice" (1 Kings 3:28, ESV).

Isaiah saw that wisdom resting on the Messiah, and it changed how He judges:

> ✝️ Isaiah 11:2-4 (ESV)
>
> 2 And the Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him, the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, the
> Spirit of counsel and might, the Spirit of knowledge and the fear of the LORD. 3 And his delight
> shall be in the fear of the LORD. He shall not judge by what his eyes see, or decide disputes by
> what his ears hear, 4 but with righteousness he shall judge the poor, and decide with equity for the
> meek of the earth...

The wise judge looks beneath appearances, which is what John 7:24 asks of you.

James describes that wisdom, and its last two words answer Matthew 7:

> ✝️ James 3:17 (ESV)
>
> 17 But the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, open to reason, full of mercy
> and good fruits, impartial and sincere.

"Impartial" is ἀδιάκριτος (*adiakritos*, G87), built from the κρίνω family: judging without
favouritism. James 3:17 is its only New Testament use. "Sincere" is ἀνυπόκριτος (*anypokritos*,
G505), literally "without hypocrisy". The wisdom God gives is judging with the log already out.

And God gives it to anyone who asks: "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives
generously to all without reproach, and it will be given him" (James 1:5, ESV).

## What this shows about God

**This shows that God is the Judge, and that He is also the Surgeon.** He sees the beam you cannot
see. He sends a Nathan, or His word, to show it to you. When you own it, He takes it out: "The LORD
also has put away your sin" (2 Samuel 12:13, ESV).

He did it at the cost of His Son. Jesus, who alone could judge without a beam, took the judgment Himself:
"For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness
of God" (2 Corinthians 5:21, ESV). That is the atonement, and its verdict over everyone in Christ is
justification: "no condemnation" (Romans 8:1, ESV). "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to
forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9, ESV).

So you never correct a brother from the bench. You come as a patient the Surgeon has already
treated, with clear sight and gentle hands, hoping to gain your brother. And the measure God uses
with His children is "pressed down, shaken together, running over" (Luke 6:38, ESV), so you can
measure mercy out freely. "Should not you have had mercy on your fellow servant, as I had mercy on
you?" (Matthew 18:33, ESV).

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** Matthew uses ὑποκριτής thirteen times, and only at Matthew 7:5 is it singular,
   spoken to the listener: "You hypocrite". In chapter 6 "the hypocrites" were other people (Matthew
   6:2, 5, 16). What changes for you when the word turns from "them" to "you"?
2. **The text in its context.** Luke puts the speck and the log straight after "Can a blind man lead
   a blind man?" and "A disciple is not above his teacher" (Luke 6:39-40). What does the blind guide
   add to the picture of the man with the log?
3. **Christ.** Jesus says, "As I hear, I judge, and my judgment is just, because I seek not my own
   will but the will of him who sent me" (John 5:30). How should the way the Son judges shape the way
   you correct a brother?
4. **The hard part.** Jesus says "Judge not" (Matthew 7:1), and Paul asks, "Is it not those inside
   the church whom you are to judge?" (1 Corinthians 5:12). How do you tell, in a real situation, which
   one you are being asked to do?
5. **Be transformed.** David condemned the rich man in a sentence and needed Nathan to show him his own
   sin (2 Samuel 12:5-7). Who has God given you to be your Nathan, and what would it take for you to
   listen when they speak?
