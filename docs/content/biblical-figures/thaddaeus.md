# Thaddaeus (Judas son of James)

He appears at position ten in the Synoptic lists under two different names, asks one question in the
whole of Scripture, and vanishes. The question is a good one, and the answer to it is among the most
remarkable promises Jesus ever made.

## Key Takeaways

### Lessons about Jesus

Jesus answered a question nobody else thought to ask, and answered it by describing where God
intends to live: "we will come to him and make our home with him" (John 14:23). The disclosure
Thaddaeus was asking about turns out not to be a public spectacle but a permanent residence.

### Memory verse

> ✝️ John 14:23 (ESV)
>
> 23 Jesus answered him, "If anyone loves me, he will keep my word, and my Father will love him, and we will come to him and make our home with him.

### Be transformed

His question assumed that a real Messiah would eventually go public — and it was a fair assumption.
The answer redirected it entirely. Check whether you are waiting for God to do something visible and
external while he is offering to move in.

### Prayer

Father, we ask why you are not more obvious, and you answer that you want to make your home with us.
Forgive us for wanting a spectacle more than a guest. Come and dwell with us, and let our keeping of
your word be the proof.

## The names

**Thaddaeus** — <span lang="grc">Θαδδαῖος</span> (*Thaddaios*, thad-DAH-yoss). Strong's lists this
as **of uncertain origin**, and that restraint is worth honouring. A commonly repeated derivation
connects it to an Aramaic root for "breast," yielding something like "beloved" or
"warm-hearted," but this is a proposal rather than an established etymology, and it is often quoted
with more confidence than it has earned. The honest answer is that we do not know what his name
meant.

Some manuscripts of Matthew 10:3 read **Lebbaeus** here, and some conflate the two ("Lebbaeus, who
was also called Thaddaeus" — the reading behind the KJV). The earliest text has simply *Thaddaeus*.

**Judas son of James** — <span lang="grc">Ἰούδας Ἰακώβου</span> — is what Luke calls him in both his
Gospel (6:16) and Acts (1:13). *Judas* is <span dir="rtl">יְהוּדָה</span> *Yehudah*, "praised."

Two names for one man is the straightforward reading: Matthew and Mark have Thaddaeus at position
ten, Luke and Acts have Judas son of James in the same group of four, and the lists are otherwise
identical in membership. Carrying both a Semitic and a Greek or Latin name was entirely ordinary.
And in this case the second name had an obvious practical use — John, when he introduces him,
immediately clarifies which Judas he means.

## What Scripture records

One verse.

> ✝️ John 14:22 (ESV)
>
> 22 Judas (not Iscariot) said to him, "Lord, how is it that you will manifest yourself to us, and not to the world?"

John's parenthesis — "not Iscariot" — is the only characterisation he receives in the New Testament,
and it is a disambiguation.

The question comes in the upper room, after Jesus has said that whoever loves him will be loved by
his Father and that he will "manifest myself to him" (John 14:21). Thaddaeus catches the word *to
him*. He has been expecting a Messiah who would be revealed publicly, to everyone, in a way that
settled the matter. Why the restriction?

Jesus does not correct the premise so much as reframe the whole expectation. The manifestation is
not a demonstration to be witnessed but an indwelling to be received — the Father and the Son coming
to make their home with anyone who loves and obeys.

## Is he the author of Jude?

No, on the most likely reading. The letter of Jude opens "Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and
brother of James" (Jude 1:1) — *brother* of James, whereas this apostle is *son* of James. The
letter is generally attributed to Jude the brother of Jesus, named alongside James in Mark 6:3. The
similarity of names is the whole basis of the confusion.

## What tradition says

Traditions attach to him unusually loosely. He is associated with mission in Mesopotamia, Persia, or
Armenia, and in Eastern tradition with **Edessa** — but the well-known Edessa story needs care.
Eusebius relates the correspondence between King Abgar of Edessa and Jesus, and the sending of
Thaddaeus to heal the king (*Church History* 1.13). The Thaddaeus in that account is expressly
described as one of **the seventy**, not one of the Twelve (1.12).

That distinction is not a modern quibble. The editor of the standard English edition says Eusebius
made the mistake himself: "Thaddeus is seen to be one of the Twelve, apparently identical with Jude
and Lebbæus... Eusebius here sunders him from the apostles and makes him one of the Seventy,
committing an error." Eusebius introduces it with <span lang="grc">φασί</span> (*phasi*, "they say")
— he is passing on oral report, and he is the first known source for it. So the Edessa tradition may
not concern this apostle at all.

In Western devotion he became Saint Jude, patron of lost causes — a role with no ancient basis
whatever. See [Patristic Sources](../resources/patristic-sources.md) for how these are graded.

## Takeaway

He asked why God was not being more obvious. It is the question underneath a great deal of modern
unbelief, and it was asked first by an apostle, at the table, on the last night. The answer he was
given was not an explanation but an invitation.
