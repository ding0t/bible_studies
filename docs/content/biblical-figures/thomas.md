# Thomas

"Doubting Thomas" is the least fair nickname in Christian usage. Everything John records about him
before the famous scene shows a man of blunt, dangerous loyalty — and the scene itself ends with the
strongest thing anyone says about Jesus in the entire Gospel.

## Key Takeaways

### Lessons about Jesus

Jesus came back a week later, walked through a locked door, and offered Thomas precisely the
evidence he had demanded — hands, side, the specific terms Thomas had set. Then he called for
belief. He did not shame the request; he answered it and then addressed it.

### Memory verse

> ✝️ John 20:28 (ESV)
>
> 28 Thomas answered him, "My Lord and my God!"

### Be transformed

Thomas said out loud what the others had already been given. When Jesus first appeared to them, "he
showed them his hands and his side" — and *then* "the disciples were glad when they saw the Lord"
(John 20:20). They saw the wounds too. Thomas's only distinction was being absent the first time and
honest about it afterwards. Stop treating articulated doubt as a worse condition than unexamined
agreement.

### Prayer

Lord, you did not despise a man who asked for evidence, and you did not leave him without it. Where
we doubt, keep us honest, and keep us in the room. And bring us to the place where we say with
Thomas, without reservation, "My Lord and my God."

## The name

<span lang="grc">Θωμᾶς</span> (*Thōmas*, tho-MAHSS), from Aramaic
<span dir="rtl">תְּאוֹמָא</span> *teʾoma* (teh-oh-MAH), "twin." John three times adds the Greek
equivalent, <span lang="grc">Δίδυμος</span> (*Didymos*), which also means "twin" — the ESV simply
translates it, "called the Twin" (John 11:16; 20:24; 21:2).

Whose twin he was is never stated. The name is all Scripture gives.

## What Scripture records

Thomas speaks three times in John, and the first two are usually left out of his reputation.

**He volunteers to die.** Jesus proposes returning to Judea, where the authorities have recently
tried to stone him. The disciples object. Thomas ends the debate:

> ✝️ John 11:16 (ESV)
>
> 16 So Thomas, called the Twin, said to his fellow disciples, "Let us also go, that we may die with him."

That is not the remark of a sceptic. It is fatalistic, and it is courageous, and no one else in the
room said it.

**He admits he is lost.** In the upper room, Jesus says the disciples know the way to where he is
going. Thomas contradicts him: "Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?"
(John 14:5). The answer to that admission is one of the most quoted sentences in Scripture — "I am
the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). Thomas's willingness to say he did not understand
is what draws it out.

**He demands evidence.** Absent when Jesus first appeared, Thomas refuses the others' report on
strict terms: "Unless I see in his hands the mark of the nails, and place my finger into the mark of
the nails, and place my hand into his side, I will never believe" (John 20:25).

Eight days later Jesus returns and meets the demand item by item:

> ✝️ John 20:27-28 (ESV)
>
> 27 Then he said to Thomas, "Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not disbelieve, but believe." 28 Thomas answered him, "My Lord and my God!"

John does not say Thomas ever touched him.

## The confession

"My Lord and my God" — <span lang="grc">ὁ κύριός μου καὶ ὁ θεός μου</span> — is the climactic
confession of John's Gospel. It is addressed directly to Jesus, and Jesus accepts it. John opened
with "the Word was God" (1:1) and closes the narrative with a man saying the same thing to Jesus'
face. The doubter supplies the Gospel's final and highest Christological statement.

Jesus' reply looks past Thomas to everyone reading: "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet
have believed" (John 20:29) — which is a blessing on the reader, not a rebuke of the man in the
room.

## What tradition says

Thomas is popularly associated with **India**, and that association is weaker than it is usually
presented — including in an earlier version of this page, which called it "unusually strong" before
the sources were checked.

The **oldest** form of the tradition sends Thomas not to India but to **Parthia**. Eusebius records
it that way, citing Origen: "Parthia, according to tradition, was allotted to Thomas as his field of
labor" (*Church History* 3.1). The same assignment appears in the Clementine *Recognitions* (9.29)
and in Socrates (*H.E.* 1.19), while Rufinus and Socrates elsewhere name **Edessa** as his burial
place. Philip Schaff's editorial note in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* is blunt about what
came next: "Later traditions extended his labors eastward as far as India" — and of the Kerala
community's own name for itself, that "the name cannot be traced beyond the eighth century, and is
derived, probably, from a Nestorian missionary."

So the honest ordering is: Parthia early and comparatively well attested, Edessa next, India later.

The *Acts of Thomas* is where the India story is told at length. It opens with the apostles dividing
up the world by lot: "By lot, then, India fell to Judas Thomas, also called Didymus. And he did not
wish to go... how can I, being an Hebrew man, go among the Indians to proclaim the truth?" It is a
good story, and it is not history. The ANF editors describe it as a book "held in great estimation by
the heretics of the first and second centuries," whose distinctive teaching was that Thomas baptized
with oil rather than water — condemned in the Decree of Gelasius and grouped with the disputed books
in the Synopsis ascribed to Athanasius. It attests that a tradition existed early; it does not
establish where Thomas went.

Schaff's broader warning covers the whole field: "The efforts to derive from the varying traditions
any trustworthy particulars as to the apostles themselves is almost wholly vain."

A separate caution: several later works carry his name, including the *Gospel of Thomas*. These are
not by the apostle and are not part of Scripture.

## Takeaway

Thomas asked for exactly what the other ten had already been given, and was given it. The Gospel
that records his doubt is the same one that lets him say the last and largest word about who Jesus
is. Whatever "doubting Thomas" is supposed to mean, John clearly did not mean it as the summary.
