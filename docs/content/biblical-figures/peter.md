# Simon Peter

He is first in every list of the Twelve, he speaks more often than all the others combined, and he
is the only man Jesus ever called Satan. Peter is the most fully drawn of the apostles because he
was the most willing to be wrong out loud.

## Key Takeaways

### Lessons about Jesus

Jesus gave Peter his new name at their first meeting, before Peter had said or done anything
(John 1:42). The name was not a reward for becoming rocklike. It was a statement of what Jesus
intended to make of him, issued years before the evidence supported it — and it survived the
denial.

### Memory verse

> ✝️ Luke 22:31-32 (ESV)
>
> 31 "Simon, Simon, behold, Satan demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat, 32 but I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail. And when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers."

### Be transformed

Peter's failure was not the end of his usefulness, and Jesus said so *before* the failure happened —
"when you have turned again" assumes both the fall and the return. If you are treating a past
collapse as a permanent disqualification, you are holding a stricter standard than the one Jesus
applied to the man who denied him to his face.

### Prayer

Lord, you named Peter a rock while he was still sand. Thank you that you speak to us in terms of
what you intend to make of us, and not only what we currently are. When we fail, turn us back, and
make our turning useful to somebody else.

## The name

**Simon** — <span lang="grc">Σίμων</span> (*Simōn*), from Hebrew <span dir="rtl">שִׁמְעוֹן</span>
*Shimʿon* (shim-OWN), "he has heard," the name of one of Jacob's sons and his tribe.

**Peter** — <span lang="grc">Πέτρος</span> (*Petros*, PET-ross), "a rock, a stone." Jesus actually
gave him the Aramaic form: <span lang="grc">Κηφᾶς</span> (*Kēphas*, kay-FAHS), from Aramaic
<span dir="rtl">כֵּיפָא</span> *kepha*, "rock." John translates it for his Greek readers, and shows
Jesus doing the renaming on the spot:

> ✝️ John 1:42 (ESV)
>
> 42 He brought him to Jesus. Jesus looked at him and said, "You are Simon the son of John. You shall be called Cephas" (which means Peter ).

The New Testament keeps using both names, sometimes in the same breath — "Simon Peter." Paul's
usual name for him is Cephas (1 Corinthians 1:12; Galatians 2:9).

## Background

A fisherman on the Sea of Galilee, working in a partnership with his brother Andrew and with James
and John (Luke 5:10). Originally from Bethsaida (John 1:44), later based at Capernaum, where his
mother-in-law's house appears in the narrative (Mark 1:29-31) — Peter was married, and was still
travelling with his wife decades later (1 Corinthians 9:5).

He was brought to Jesus by Andrew — a fact easily lost whenever Peter is treated as the
natural leader: he arrived as somebody's younger-brother introduction.

## What Scripture records

Peter is the disciple who steps forward, and the pattern is consistent — a real insight followed by
a real collapse, usually within the same chapter.

- **He names Jesus rightly** and is told the insight was not his own: "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" (Matthew 16:16), to which Jesus responds that "flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father who is in heaven" (16:17). Verses later, Peter rebukes Jesus for predicting the cross and is answered "Get behind me, Satan" (16:23).
- **He walks on water and sinks** (Matthew 14:28-31) — the only disciple who got out of the boat, and the only one who needed pulling out of the lake.
- **He denies Jesus three times** (Mark 14:66-72), having sworn hours earlier that he would not.
- **He is restored three times** on the beach, question matched to denial: "Simon, son of John, do you love me?" (John 21:15-17). Jesus does not extract an apology; he issues a commission — *feed my sheep*.
- **He preaches at Pentecost** (Acts 2:14-41), and is the one who first takes the gospel to a Gentile household (Acts 10).
- **He is publicly corrected by Paul** at Antioch for withdrawing from Gentile believers (Galatians 2:11-14). The New Testament does not tidy this away.

Two letters bear his name (1 and 2 Peter), written to scattered believers under pressure.

## What tradition says

The earliest witness is **1 Clement**, written from Rome to Corinth around AD 96 — within living
memory, and to readers assumed to know the story already:

> Let us set before our eyes the good apostles: Peter, who because of unrighteous jealousy endured
> not one or two but many trials, and having thus borne witness went to the place of glory that was
> his due.
>
> — 1 Clement 5.3-4 (translated from the Greek; the verb is <span lang="grc">μαρτυρήσας</span>, *martyrēsas*, "having borne witness," from which "martyr" comes)

Note carefully what that does **not** say. It attests Peter's death as a witness; it does not name
Rome, and it says nothing about crucifixion. Those come later — from Origen, quoted by Eusebius:

> And at last, having come to Rome, he was crucified head-downwards; for he had requested that he
> might suffer in this way.
>
> — Eusebius, *Church History* 3.1, citing Origen

So the layers are: martyrdom (c. AD 96, near-contemporary), then Rome and the inverted cross (3rd
century). Both are early by the standards of apostolic tradition, but they are not equally early, and
collapsing them into one confident sentence overstates the case. See
[Patristic Sources](../resources/patristic-sources.md).

Scripture itself gestures at the manner of his death without describing it: after the restoration,
Jesus tells him he will one day stretch out his hands and be carried where he does not wish to go —
and John adds the note "this he said to show by what kind of death he was to glorify God"
(John 21:18-19).

## Takeaway

Peter is in the Bible in this much detail for a reason. A version of him with the failures edited
out would be useless to the people the New Testament was written for. What is offered is not a man
who never fell, but a man whose falling was anticipated, prayed for in advance, and made the raw
material of his commission to strengthen everyone else.
