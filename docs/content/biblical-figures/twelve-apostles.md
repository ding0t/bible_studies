# The Twelve: Disciples and Apostles

Scripture names the Twelve four separate times, and no two lists put them in the same order. That
looks at first like the ordinary untidiness of four men remembering the same group of friends. It
isn't. Lay the four lists side by side and count positions, and a structure appears that none of the
four authors mentions and all four obey.

## Key Takeaways

### Types and prophecy

**Twelve is a prophecy about Israel, not a coincidence about staffing.** Israel had twelve tribes.
When Jesus appoints exactly twelve men, he is making a claim in the shape of a number — and he says
so outright: the Twelve will "sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (Matthew
19:28). John's vision closes the loop. The city has twelve gates bearing the names of the tribes and
twelve foundations bearing the names of the apostles (Revelation 21:12-14). Both are named. Neither
is dissolved into the other.

### Lessons about Jesus

- **He chose before he sent.** Mark gives the purpose clause in two halves, and the order is not
  reversible: "so that they might be with him and he might send them out to preach" (Mark 3:14).
  Being with him is not preparation for the work; it is the first half of the appointment.
- **He prayed all night over the choice** (Luke 6:12) — and one of the twelve he chose betrayed him.
  Both facts stand in the same passage.
- **He renames people.** Simon becomes Peter, James and John become Sons of Thunder. Renaming is
  what God does to Abram and Jacob; Jesus does it on his own authority, at first meeting (John 1:42).
- **He chose men who could not have chosen each other.** A tax collector working for Rome and a
  Zealot sworn against it are four names apart in the same list.

### Memory verses

> ✝️ Mark 3:14 (ESV)
>
> 14 And he appointed twelve (whom he also named apostles) so that they might be with him and he might send them out to preach

> ✝️ John 15:16 (ESV)
>
> 16 You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit and that your fruit should abide, so that whatever you ask the Father in my name, he may give it to you.

### Be transformed

The Twelve are not a leadership seminar. Reading their lists honestly should dismantle three
assumptions rather than reinforce them.

- **That God recruits for competence.** Nothing in any of the four lists is a résumé. Six of the
  twelve get nothing but a name.
- **That the people you cannot work alongside disqualify the room.** Matthew and Simon the Zealot
  had every political reason to despise each other, and were given three years in the same small
  group. If your fellowship has been narrowed to people who already agree with you, it is narrower
  than the one Jesus assembled.
- **That obscurity means failure.** Nine of the Twelve never speak a recorded word. They are named
  on the foundations of the city anyway (Revelation 21:14).

### Prayer

Father, you chose twelve ordinary men and made them the foundation of the city that has no need of
sun or moon. You did not choose them for their strength, and you did not lose them for their
weakness. Teach us to want your presence before we want your commission — to be with your Son first,
and sent second. And where we have narrowed the company you have given us, widen it again.

## Four lists, not one

The Twelve are enumerated at four points in the New Testament, and each list sits at a different
hinge in the story.

| Passage | Where it sits | What it is doing |
|---|---|---|
| **Mark 3:16-19** | The appointment on the mountain | The founding moment itself |
| **Luke 6:14-16** | After a whole night of prayer (6:12) | The moment the name *apostles* is given |
| **Matthew 10:2-4** | Immediately before the sending discourse (10:5) | A commissioning roster |
| **Acts 1:13** | The upper room, before Pentecost | The surviving eleven, waiting |

Mark's is the constitutional act. Luke's is the naming. Matthew's is the deployment order. Acts' is
a roll call taken after one name has been struck off it.

**Who wrote these, and for whom.** Mark is the earliest of the three Gospels here, traditionally
associated with Peter's preaching in Rome and written for a largely Gentile audience — which is why
he stops to translate Semitic terms like *Boanerges* (3:17). Matthew writes for readers who know the
Hebrew Scriptures, and arranges his material into five great discourses, of which the sending of the
Twelve (chapter 10) is the second. Luke and Acts are two halves of one work addressed to Theophilus,
written with the stated aim of an orderly account (Luke 1:3) — which is why Luke alone dates the
naming of the apostles and alone tells us about the night of prayer that preceded it. Each list
therefore serves its own author's purpose before it serves our desire to harmonise them, and the
table below is a comparison, not a claim that any evangelist was trying to write one.

## The skeleton inside the lists

Compare the four lists by *position* rather than by name, and the untidiness resolves into
something highly ordered. There are three groups of four. The membership of each group never
changes. The order *within* a group changes constantly.

| Position | Matthew 10:2-4 | Mark 3:16-19 | Luke 6:14-16 | Acts 1:13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Simon Peter** | **Simon Peter** | **Simon Peter** | **Peter** |
| 2 | Andrew | James | Andrew | John |
| 3 | James | John | James | James |
| 4 | John | Andrew | John | Andrew |
| **5** | **Philip** | **Philip** | **Philip** | **Philip** |
| 6 | Bartholomew | Bartholomew | Bartholomew | Thomas |
| 7 | Thomas | Matthew | Matthew | Bartholomew |
| 8 | Matthew | Thomas | Thomas | Matthew |
| **9** | **James son of Alphaeus** | **James son of Alphaeus** | **James son of Alphaeus** | **James son of Alphaeus** |
| 10 | Thaddaeus | Thaddaeus | Simon the Zealot | Simon the Zealot |
| 11 | Simon the Zealot | Simon the Zealot | Judas son of James | Judas son of James |
| 12 | Judas Iscariot | Judas Iscariot | Judas Iscariot | *(absent)* |

Three things hold across all four lists without exception:

1. **Peter is first every time.** Matthew does not merely list him first — he numbers him, <span lang="grc">πρῶτος</span> (*prōtos*, PRO-toss), "first," though no one else in his list is given a number. It is an ordinal marker with nothing after it to be ordinal against.
2. **Philip is fifth every time, and James son of Alphaeus is ninth every time.** Each heads a group of four.
3. **Judas Iscariot is last every time he appears at all,** and he is simply gone in Acts.

Membership never crosses a group boundary. Andrew moves between positions 2 and 4, never to 5.
Thomas and Matthew swap places freely, never out of the second four.

Scripture never explains this. But four independent lists agreeing on group membership and group
leaders while disagreeing on internal order is not what carelessly-remembered lists look like. It
looks like a remembered organisational structure — three sub-groups, each with a fixed head.

**One dependency.** Group three's membership is only constant if Thaddaeus (Matthew, Mark) and Judas
son of James (Luke, Acts) are the same man. If they were two different people, the pattern in that
group breaks. The identification is the straightforward reading and is discussed below, but the
structural claim rests on it.

**An independent trace of three groups.** The early church also remembered the apostles in threes,
though along different lines. Summarising the oldest form of the mission traditions, Richard Lipsius
found the Twelve divided into three groups: Peter and Andrew, Matthew and Bartholomew around the
Black Sea; Thomas, Thaddaeus and Simon the Cananaean in Parthia; John and Philip in Asia Minor
(reported in A. C. McGiffert's notes to Eusebius, *Church History* 3.1). The groupings do not match
the list order — Matthew and Bartholomew travel with the first pair rather than staying in the
second four — so this is not confirmation of the pattern above. It is a separate witness that the
Twelve were remembered as organised in threes at all, which is at least suggestive that the list
structure is reflecting something real rather than accidental.

## Matthew's list is six pairs, and English hides it

Matthew builds his list differently from Mark, and the difference is in the Greek grammar rather
than in the words. Matthew joins names with <span lang="grc">καί</span> (*kai*, "and") *inside* each
pair, then drops the conjunction entirely *between* pairs:

> Simon who is called Peter **and** Andrew his brother, James the son of Zebedee **and** John his
> brother, Philip **and** Bartholomew, Thomas **and** Matthew the tax collector, James the son of
> Alphaeus **and** Thaddaeus, Simon the Cananaean **and** Judas Iscariot.

Six pairs. Mark, by contrast, strings every single name on the same conjunction — καί, καί, καί —
a flat chain with no internal grouping at all. This is a feature of the text itself, not of a modern
editor's commas.

Matthew places this paired list immediately before the sending discourse. And Mark, describing the
sending, writes:

> ✝️ Mark 6:7 (ESV)
>
> 7 And he called the twelve and began to send them out two by two, and gave them authority over the unclean spirits.

The Greek is <span lang="grc">δύο δύο</span> (*duo duo*), literally "two two" — a Semitic way of
saying "in twos," the same doubling Hebrew uses for distribution.

Be careful about what this does and does not establish. Scripture never names which two travelled
together, and this study will not guess. What can be said is narrower: Matthew, alone among the four, arranges the Twelve in pairs at precisely the point where they are
about to be sent out in pairs.

**An early reader saw the same thing.** The apocryphal *Acts of Thomas* — a third-century romance,
not history — opens by listing the apostles as they divide up the world, and it lists them in pairs:

> Simon called Peter, and Andrew his brother; James the son of Zebedee, and John his brother; Philip
> and Bartholomew; Thomas, and Matthew the tax-gatherer; James of Alphæus and Simon the Cananæan;
> and Judas of James.
>
> — *Acts of the Holy Apostle Thomas* 1 (*Ante-Nicene Fathers*, Vol. 8)

The first four pairs are Matthew's, in Matthew's order, down to "Matthew the tax-gatherer" — the
self-designation only Matthew's Gospel uses. The last four are re-paired because Judas Iscariot is
gone and Thaddaeus appears under his other name. So this is **not independent testimony**: it is a
text depending on Matthew. But that is precisely what makes it useful. It shows a reader in
antiquity, working from Matthew's Greek, taking the list to be paired — which is evidence that the
pairing is really in the text rather than an artefact of modern punctuation.

## From disciple to apostle

The two words are not synonyms, and Luke marks the exact moment one becomes the other.

**<span lang="grc">μαθητής</span>** (*mathētēs*, math-ay-TACE, G3101) — from
<span lang="grc">μανθάνω</span>, "to learn." A learner, a pupil. It is a wide word. Anyone following
Jesus is a *mathētēs*, and there were many.

**<span lang="grc">ἀπόστολος</span>** (*apostolos*, ah-POS-toh-los, G652) — from
<span lang="grc">ἀποστέλλω</span>, "to send off." A delegate, an envoy, one dispatched with the
authority of the sender. The word is about commission, not education.

> ✝️ Luke 6:12-13 (ESV)
>
> 12 In these days he went out to the mountain to pray, and all night he continued in prayer to God. 13 And when day came, he called his disciples and chose from them twelve, whom he named apostles:

The sequence in verse 13 is precise: he called the *disciples* — the wide group — chose twelve
**from among them**, and *named* them apostles. The Greek verb is
<span lang="grc">ὠνόμασεν</span> (*ōnomasen*), "he named." The title is conferred, not earned.

A textual note that strengthens rather than weakens the point: Mark 3:14 also contains the clause
"whom he also named apostles," but **some manuscripts omit it** — both the CSB and the NLT flag the
variant in their notes, and the ESV brackets it in parentheses. If the shorter reading of Mark is
original, then Luke 6:13 is our sole witness to the moment the name was given, and Luke's careful
dating of it matters all the more. Either way the naming is Luke's contribution to the account.

A detail worth having: Matthew uses the word *apostolos* exactly once in his entire Gospel — at
10:2, heading this list. Everywhere else his term is *disciples*. The word arrives when the sending
does.

## The calling was three stages, not one

Asking "in what order were they called?" assumes a single event. There were at least three, and
running them together flattens the story.

**Stage one — first encounter (John 1:35-51).** In Judea, near where John was baptising, two of
John's disciples follow Jesus. One is Andrew; he fetches his brother Simon. Jesus renames Simon on
the spot, before he has done anything:

> ✝️ John 1:42 (ESV)
>
> 42 He brought him to Jesus. Jesus looked at him and said, "You are Simon the son of John. You shall be called Cephas" (which means Peter ).

The next day Jesus finds Philip, and Philip finds Nathanael. Nobody has left an occupation yet.

**Stage two — the call to follow (Matthew 4:18-22; Luke 5:1-11).** Later, in Galilee, the fishermen
leave the nets. This is where "follow me" becomes a change of life rather than a conversation.

**Stage three — the appointment (Mark 3:13-19; Luke 6:12-16).** Later still, out of a large body of
disciples, twelve are formally constituted and named apostles.

So men who had already been following for months were *appointed* only at stage three. Encounter,
then discipleship, then commission. The order matters pastorally: nobody in this story was
commissioned on first meeting.

## Why the number had to be twelve

Israel had twelve tribes. That is the entire background, and Jesus does not leave it to inference:

> ✝️ Matthew 19:28 (ESV)
>
> 28 Jesus said to them, "Truly, I say to you, in the new world, when the Son of Man will sit on his glorious throne, you who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel.

"In the new world" translates <span lang="grc">παλιγγενεσία</span> (*palingenesia*,
pal-ing-gen-es-EE-ah, G3824) — from *palin*, "again," and *genesis*, "origin": a rebirth or renewal.
The word occurs only twice in the New Testament, here and at Titus 3:5, where it describes the
individual believer's regeneration. Here it is cosmic in scale rather than personal — the renewal of
the world itself, with the Son of Man enthroned and twelve thrones beside his.

This was legible to Jesus's contemporaries. The expectation that God would restore the twelve tribes
was widespread, and the community at Qumran — a renewal movement within Israel — organised itself
under a council of twelve for what appears to be exactly this reason (Community Rule, 1QS 8:1-2; see
the *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* on Mark 3:14). Appointing twelve men was a recognisable
public claim: *this is the restoration of Israel, and it is beginning here.*

Read dispensationally, this is the point at which the number refuses to be flattened. The Twelve do
not *replace* the twelve tribes — they are promised thrones from which to **judge** them, which
requires the tribes to still be there to be judged. John's final vision keeps both sets of names
permanently visible:

> ✝️ Revelation 21:12-14 (ESV)
>
> 12 It had a great, high wall, with twelve gates, and at the gates twelve angels, and on the gates the names of the twelve tribes of the sons of Israel were inscribed— 13 on the east three gates, on the north three gates, on the south three gates, and on the west three gates. 14 And the wall of the city had twelve foundations, and on them were the twelve names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb.

Twelve gates for Israel. Twelve foundations for the apostles. One city, two sets of names, neither
erased. Paul says the same thing structurally when he calls the Church "built on the foundation of
the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone" (Ephesians 2:20, ESV).

## The men themselves

Six of the Twelve never speak a recorded word in Scripture. What follows is what the text actually
supplies — where a meaning is uncertain, it is marked so.

| # | Name | Original | Meaning | Background |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Simon Peter | <span lang="grc">Σίμων Πέτρος</span> | *Simon* from Hebrew <span dir="rtl">שִׁמְעוֹן</span> *Shimʿon*, "he has heard"; *Peter* / Aramaic *Cephas*, "rock" | Fisherman, Bethsaida and Capernaum |
| 2 | Andrew | <span lang="grc">Ἀνδρέας</span> | A Greek name, from <span lang="grc">ἀνήρ</span> *anēr*, "man" — "manly" | Fisherman; Peter's brother; first a disciple of John the Baptist |
| 3 | James son of Zebedee | <span lang="grc">Ἰάκωβος</span> | Jacob, <span dir="rtl">יַעֲקֹב</span> *Yaʿaqob*, "heel-grabber, supplanter" | Fisherman; first apostle martyred (Acts 12:2) |
| 4 | John | <span lang="grc">Ἰωάννης</span> | <span dir="rtl">יוֹחָנָן</span> *Yochanan*, "Yahweh has been gracious" | Fisherman; James's brother; both named Sons of Thunder |
| 5 | Philip | <span lang="grc">Φίλιππος</span> | A Greek name — *philos* + *hippos*, "lover of horses" | Bethsaida, same town as Andrew and Peter |
| 6 | Bartholomew | <span lang="grc">Βαρθολομαῖος</span> | Aramaic *bar-Talmai*, "son of Talmai" — a patronymic, not a personal name | Traditionally identified with Nathanael of Cana (see below) |
| 7 | Thomas | <span lang="grc">Θωμᾶς</span> | Aramaic <span dir="rtl">תְּאוֹמָא</span> *teʾoma*, "twin"; Greek *Didymus* means the same (John 11:16) | Unrecorded; whose twin is never said |
| 8 | Matthew | <span lang="grc">Μαθθαῖος</span> | <span dir="rtl">מַתִּתְיָה</span> *Mattityah*, "gift of Yahweh"; also called Levi, "attached" | Tax collector (<span lang="grc">τελώνης</span>, *telōnēs*) under Rome |
| 9 | James son of Alphaeus | <span lang="grc">Ἰάκωβος</span> | As above; *Alphaeus* is of uncertain derivation | Nothing recorded beyond the lists |
| 10 | Thaddaeus / Judas son of James | <span lang="grc">Θαδδαῖος</span> | **Uncertain.** Strong's declines to derive it | Speaks once (John 14:22) |
| 11 | Simon the Zealot | <span lang="grc">Καναναῖος</span> / <span lang="grc">Ζηλωτής</span> | Aramaic <span dir="rtl">קַנְאָן</span> *qanʾan*, "zealous" — **not** "Canaanite" | Zealot, or of zealot temperament |
| 12 | Judas Iscariot | <span lang="grc">Ἰούδας Ἰσκαριώτης</span> | *Judah*, "praised"; *Iscariot* probably *ish-Qeriyoth*, "man of Kerioth" | Kept the money box (John 12:6); betrayed Jesus |

Three notes on that table, because each is a place where confident-sounding tradition outruns the
text.

**Simon the Cananaean is not a Canaanite.** Matthew and Mark call him
<span lang="grc">Καναναῖος</span> (*Kananaios*), which the KJV rendered "Simon the Canaanite" —
and Strong's itself flags that as an error, noting the word was mistaken for a derivative of
<span lang="grc">Χαναάν</span>, "Canaan." It is Aramaic *qanʾan*, "zealous one." Luke simply
translates it into Greek for his readers: <span lang="grc">Ζηλωτής</span>, *Zēlōtēs*, "Zealot"
(Luke 6:15). Two names, one man, one meaning — Luke is doing his readers a service, not recording
a different person.

This is one place where checking the translations pays off in a concrete, usable way. The ESV
renders *Kananaios* as "Zealot" at both Matthew 10:4 and Mark 3:18, silently doing for the modern
reader exactly what Luke did for his — so an ESV reader sees one consistent man across all four
lists and never encounters the problem. A KJV reader meets "Simon the Canaanite" in Matthew and
Mark and "Simon Zelotes" in Luke, and has no way to tell from the English that these are the same
word. If you are teaching from the KJV, this is worth correcting out loud.

**Bartholomew is probably Nathanael, but Scripture never says so.** The argument runs: *Bar-Talmai*
is a patronymic ("son of Talmai"), so the man needs a personal name; Bartholomew appears in all
four lists and Nathanael in none; John never mentions Bartholomew and the Synoptics never mention
Nathanael; Philip brings Nathanael to Jesus (John 1:45) and Philip stands immediately before
Bartholomew in three of the four lists; and John 21:2 places Nathanael among named apostles at a
resurrection appearance. That is a strong cumulative case. It remains an inference.

**Thaddaeus and Judas son of James.** Matthew and Mark have Thaddaeus at position 10; Luke and Acts
have Judas son of James in the same group. The straightforward reading is one man with two names —
unremarkable in a period when many Jews carried both a Semitic and a Greek or Latin name — and the
second name would have been useful precisely because *Judas* had become unusable.

### A tax collector and a Zealot

The most striking fact about the roster is political. **Matthew** collected taxes for the Roman
occupation; a <span lang="grc">τελώνης</span> was regarded as a collaborator and a thief, which is
why the phrase "tax collectors and sinners" needs no explanation in the Gospels. **Simon** was a
Zealot — the party of armed Jewish nationalism, whose whole programme was throwing Rome out, and
whose extreme wing later took to assassinating collaborators.

Under any normal circumstance these two men are enemies. Jesus put them in the same group of twelve
and kept them there for three years.

Matthew's own Gospel adds a quiet detail here. In Mark and Luke, Matthew is listed *before* Thomas.
In Matthew's list, he places himself *after* Thomas — and he is the only one of the four writers to
attach "the tax collector" to his own name.

## Sent to Israel first

The commission that follows Matthew's list is explicitly restricted:

> ✝️ Matthew 10:5-6 (ESV)
>
> 5 These twelve Jesus sent out, instructing them, "Go nowhere among the Gentiles and enter no town of the Samaritans, 6 but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.

This is a real limitation with a date on it, not a permanent policy — the same Gospel ends with
"make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19). But the order of operations is deliberate and fits
the number twelve exactly: the restoration of Israel is offered to Israel first, by twelve men who
are themselves the sign of that restoration.

There is a quiet counterpoint. When Greeks later come looking for Jesus, they approach **Philip**,
who fetches **Andrew** (John 12:20-22) — the only two of the Twelve carrying purely Greek names,
both from Bethsaida in Galilee. Nothing in the text says this is why they were approached.

## They all fled

Any honest account has to include this, because the Gospels insist on it.

> ✝️ Mark 14:50 (ESV)
>
> 50 And they all left him and fled.

They argued about which of them was the greatest — at the Last Supper (Luke 22:24). They failed to
understand teaching they had heard repeatedly. Peter denied him three times. One of them sold him.
Mark's five-word sentence does not soften any of it.

This matters for how the Twelve are taught. They are not presented as a standard of spiritual
achievement. Their qualification was that Jesus chose them and they were with him — "You did not
choose me, but I chose you" (John 15:16, ESV).

## The twelfth chair

After the ascension, the eleven's first recorded act is not preaching. It is arithmetic. They set
about becoming twelve again — which only makes sense if the number carries the weight argued above.

Peter states the qualification:

> ✝️ Acts 1:21-22 (ESV)
>
> 21 So one of the men who have accompanied us during all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us, 22 beginning from the baptism of John until the day when he was taken up from us—one of these men must become with us a witness to his resurrection."

Two requirements: presence for the whole public ministry, from John's baptism to the ascension, and
eyewitness of the resurrection. Both are conditions no later generation can satisfy. Whatever else
is said about apostleship, the office as defined here closes with the people who met those terms.
Matthias was chosen by lot (Acts 1:26) — the last recorded use of the lot in Scripture, and notably
before Pentecost.

## Theological principle

The Twelve are a claim before they are a team. By appointing exactly twelve men and promising them
twelve thrones over twelve tribes, Jesus announced the restoration of Israel and staffed it with
people who had nothing to recommend them but his choosing. Their calling ran presence-before-mission
— *with him*, then *sent out* — and their commission was grounded not in their competence, which
repeatedly failed, but in their having been chosen and having seen the risen Lord.

This does not rest on the list structure or on any single word study. It is stated outright in
Mark 3:14, Matthew 19:28, John 15:16 and Acts 1:21-22.

## Questions for discussion

1. Mark puts "be with him" before "send them out." What changes in how you evaluate a ministry — your own or someone else's — if the first half of the appointment is the presence rather than the productivity?
2. Jesus prayed all night and still chose Judas (Luke 6:12-16). What does that do to the assumption that a well-prayed decision is one that cannot go wrong?
3. Matthew and Simon the Zealot were political enemies kept in the same group for three years. Who is missing from your fellowship, and is their absence a conviction or a preference?
4. Nine of the Twelve never speak a recorded word, and all twelve names are on the city's foundations. What does that suggest about how visible faithfulness has to be to count?
5. The apostolic qualification in Acts 1:21-22 cannot be met by anyone alive. What follows for how we should treat claims to apostolic authority today?

**Method.** The exegesis-before-application order followed here is that of Gordon D. Fee and
Douglas Stuart, *How to Read the Bible for All Its Worth*, 4th ed. (Zondervan).

**A note on what is not here.** Traditions about how each apostle died come from sources of widely
varying reliability, most of them centuries later than the events. They are treated in the
individual profiles, labelled as tradition, and are deliberately absent from the argument above.
