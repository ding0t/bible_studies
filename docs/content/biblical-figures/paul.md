# Paul: Apostle to the Gentiles

Saul was a zealous persecutor of the Way, a man who had believers dragged from their houses to be
jailed for the sake of the Jewish faith he thought he was protecting. Scripture is explicit about
what he was called to instead: carry the gospel to the Gentiles. He is not one of the Twelve, and
Luke doesn't even switch to calling him "Paul" until the moment his mission turns to Gentile
territory (Acts 13:9).

## Key Takeaways

### Types & Prophecy

Scripture never calls Paul a **type** (τύπος, *typos*, the word it uses of Adam patterning Christ
in Romans 5:14) of the Gentiles being grafted in. It calls him something narrower: a
**ὑποτύπωσις** (*hypotyposis*), a "pattern" or working sketch, of how grace reaches "the
foremost of sinners" (1 Timothy 1:15-16) -- a statement about the gospel's power to save anyone,
not a statement about Gentile inclusion specifically. What *is* stated outright, repeatedly, is
his personal commission: he is named apostle to the Gentiles by the risen Christ himself (Acts
9:15; 26:17), and that commission is later ratified by the Jerusalem apostles as a distinct
assignment alongside Peter's to the circumcised (Galatians 2:7-9). The two facts sit side by side
in Paul's own biography and share a common grammar -- grace to someone with no natural claim -- but
the text keeps them as two claims, not one.

### Lessons about Jesus

- **He named the persecution personal before anything else.** "Saul, Saul, why are you
  persecuting *me*?" (Acts 9:4) -- not "my church" or "my people." Jesus identifies himself with
  the body he had not yet named the church.
- **He chose the instrument before the instrument had done anything to deserve it.** The word to
  Ananias is not a description of Saul's character but a decision already made: "he is a chosen
  instrument of mine" (Acts 9:15, ESV).
- **He gave Paul a commission with a cost attached, in the same breath.** "I will show him how
  much he must suffer for the sake of my name" (Acts 9:16, ESV) -- the sending and the suffering
  are announced together, not the second as a surprise after the first.

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** If the man who approved of Stephen's death (Acts 8:1) is not disqualified, no
  résumé of past failure disqualifies anyone else. Paul's own logic is explicit: "I received
  mercy for this reason... as an example" (1 Timothy 1:16) -- his case is deliberately made
  public so that no one afterward can claim to be a harder case.
- **Attitude.** Hold the same two things together Paul held about himself: genuinely "the
  foremost of sinners" (1 Timothy 1:15, present tense, not "was") and genuinely appointed to the
  most consequential missionary work in the New Testament. Neither cancels the other.
- **Do.** Where the Jerusalem church could have gatekept Paul's ministry over his past, they
  instead gave him "the right hand of fellowship" once they perceived the grace given to him
  (Galatians 2:9). Extend that same test to someone whose past makes their present calling hard
  to credit.

### Prayer

Lord Jesus, you stopped a man mid-persecution and made him your envoy to the very people he
would never have chosen to reach. Thank you that you call by grace and not by résumé. Where I have
written someone off -- or written myself off -- because of what came before, interrupt that the way
you interrupted Saul on the road, and finish what you start. Amen.

## Saul of Tarsus, before the road

Paul supplies his own credentials, and they are substantial. Roman citizen by birth (Acts 22:28),
from the tribe of Benjamin, "a Hebrew of Hebrews" (Philippians 3:5), trained as a Pharisee under
Gamaliel (Acts 22:3), a rabbi whose reputation was respected even by the Jerusalem council (Acts
5:34-39). He measures his own former standing without false modesty:

> ✝️ Philippians 3:5-6 (ESV)
>
> 5 circumcised on the eighth day, of the people of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews; as to the law, a Pharisee; 6 as to zeal, a persecutor of the church; as to righteousness under the law, blameless.

That last item is the hinge. His zeal was not passive disapproval; it was active persecution --
"I persecuted this Way to the death, binding and delivering to prison both men and women" (Acts
22:4, ESV), present at Stephen's stoning while holding the coats of the men who threw the stones
(Acts 7:58; 8:1). Galatians is blunter still: "I persecuted the church of God violently and tried
to destroy it" (1:13, ESV). The word Scripture supplies for what Saul was, before the road, is not
softened anywhere in the record -- his own writing keeps it as sharp as any outside witness could.

## The road to Damascus

Luke tells the conversion once as narrative (Acts 9) and reports Paul retelling it twice more,
each time shaped to a different audience -- before a Jerusalem mob (Acts 22) and before King
Agrippa (Acts 26). All three keep the same core: a light, a voice, and a question.

> ✝️ Acts 9:3-6 (ESV)
>
> 3 Now as he went on his way, he approached Damascus, and suddenly a light from heaven shone around him. 4 And falling to the ground, he heard a voice saying to him, "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" 5 And he said, "Who are you, Lord?" And he said, "I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting. 6 But rise and enter the city, and you will be told what you are to do."

Jesus does not say "why are you persecuting my church" or "my people." The verb is direct:
**με διώκεις** (*me diōkeis*), "you are persecuting *me*." Saul had never laid a hand on Jesus of
Nazareth, who had already died, risen, and ascended years earlier. The identification of the
risen Christ with the body he had not yet called the church is total enough that an attack on one
is an attack on the other. Paul makes the point once, here, and does not return to it elsewhere.

The commission comes not to Saul directly but through Ananias, a disciple in Damascus who has
every reason to be afraid of the man he is sent to heal:

> ✝️ Acts 9:15-16 (ESV)
>
> 15 But the Lord said to him, "Go, for he is a chosen instrument of mine to carry my name before the Gentiles and kings and the children of Israel. 16 For I will show him how much he must suffer for the sake of my name."

**σκεῦος ἐκλογῆς** (*skeuos eklogēs*, SKEV-os ek-loh-GACE) -- literally "a vessel of choosing." The
noun **σκεῦος** (G4632) is an ordinary household word for a container -- a jar, a tool, anything
made to hold or carry something else. Paul later uses the same word of himself and every minister
of the gospel: "we have this treasure in jars of clay [ἐν ὀστρακίνοις σκεύεσιν], to show that the
surpassing power belongs to God and not to us" (2 Corinthians 4:7, ESV). The vessel is ordinary;
what it carries is not.

The qualifying word is **ἐκλογή** (*eklogē*, G1589), "a choosing, a selection" -- the same root
Paul uses of God's sovereign choice throughout Romans 9-11. That is not incidental. A few chapters
earlier in the same letter Paul had written of "vessels of mercy, which he has prepared beforehand
for glory... including us whom he has called, not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles"
(Romans 9:23-24, ESV) -- the same noun, **σκεύη** (*skeuē*), this time qualified by *mercy*
(ἐλέους) rather than *choosing* (ἐκλογῆς) -- two different qualifiers, not one image doubled. But
they are the same word for the same kind of thing: a container with nothing to recommend it except
that God decided to fill it, applied once to Paul by name and once to the mixed company of
believing Jews and Gentiles he numbers himself among.

## Two claims, kept separate

This is the point where it matters to be precise about what the text says and does not say,
because two true things about Paul are easy to blur into one overstated thing.

**Claim one: Paul is a named pattern of how grace saves an unlikely candidate.**

> ✝️ 1 Timothy 1:15-16 (ESV)
>
> 15 The saying is trustworthy and deserving of full acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost. 16 But I received mercy for this reason, that in me, as the foremost, Jesus Christ might display his perfect patience as an example to those who were to believe in him for eternal life.

The word translated "example" is **ὑποτύπωσις** (*hypotyposis*, hoo-po-TIP-oh-sis, G5296) -- built
from *hypo* ("under") and *typos* ("a mark, an impression, a pattern"), literally an "under-sketch"
or preliminary outline, the kind an artist lays down before finishing a work. It occurs exactly
twice in the New Testament, both times from Paul about the same subject: here, and at 2 Timothy
1:13, where Paul tells Timothy to hold "the pattern of sound words" he had heard from him. It is
not the word Scripture uses for a formal Old Testament type -- that word is **τύπος** (*typos*)
itself, which Paul applies to Adam as "a type of the one who was to come" (Romans 5:14, ESV). A
*typos* points forward across covenants to Christ. A *hypotyposis* is a worked example held up for
imitation. Paul is claiming the second kind of pattern for himself, and the ESV Study Bible's own
section heading over 1:12-17 names it precisely: "Paul: An Example of the Effect of the True
Gospel." The claim is about the gospel's power to transform *anyone*, stated in the most extreme
case available -- not a claim about Gentiles as a category.

**Claim two: Paul is named, specifically and repeatedly, apostle to the Gentiles.**

> ✝️ Romans 11:13 (ESV)
>
> 13 Now I am speaking to you Gentiles. Inasmuch then as I am an apostle to the Gentiles, I magnify my ministry

The same claim recurs at his own retelling of the Damascus commission -- "I am sending you to open
their eyes... a servant and a witness... delivering you from your people and from the Gentiles, to
whom I am sending you" (Acts 26:16-17, ESV) -- and again in his defense of that calling to the
Galatians: "he who worked through Peter for his apostolic ministry to the circumcised worked also
through me for mine to the Gentiles" (Galatians 2:8, ESV). The Jerusalem apostles did not simply
tolerate this; they ratified it as a division of labor equal in standing to Peter's own commission:

> ✝️ Galatians 2:9 (ESV)
>
> 9 and when James and Cephas and John, who seemed to be pillars, perceived the grace that was given to me, they gave the right hand of fellowship to Barnabas and me, that we should go to the Gentiles and they to the circumcised.

Both claims are stated in the plainest possible language, and neither one is the other. Scripture
never writes a sentence that says "Paul's conversion is a type of Gentile inclusion." What it does
give is a man in whom both truths run together biographically -- the same grace that reached the
least likely convert is the grace he then spent thirty years carrying to the people furthest,
covenantally, from where he had started. Drawing the connection is a legitimate reading of the
whole life; stating it as a formal type the text itself declares is more than the text claims.

## The grammar the two claims share

The real connection is not a stated type but a shared *shape* of grace -- the same argument, made
twice, about how each inclusion happens.

Romans 11 supplies the technical word for how Gentiles are joined to the people of God: **ἐγκεντρίζω**
(*enkentrizō*, en-ken-TRID-zo, G1461), "to graft in," a precise horticultural term that occurs six
times in the New Testament and nowhere outside this single passage (11:17, 19, 23 twice, 24
twice) -- Paul's own coinage-in-context for this argument, not a term he is drawing from elsewhere
in his letters.

> ✝️ Romans 11:24 (ESV)
>
> 24 For if you were cut from what is by nature a wild olive tree, and grafted, contrary to nature, into a cultivated olive tree, how much more will these, the natural branches, be grafted back into their own olive tree.

**παρὰ φύσιν**, "contrary to nature" -- the *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* notes that
ancient grafting normally ran the opposite direction: a *cultivated* shoot grafted onto a *wild*
rootstock to strengthen it, since a wild branch grafted into good stock (Paul's actual image) was
recognized in antiquity as the unnatural, backward version of the procedure. Paul chooses the
harder, less horticulturally sensible image on purpose. The grafting is not a natural improvement;
it runs against how the process is supposed to work, which is exactly his point to Gentile readers
tempted toward pride: *nothing in your position earned this.*

That is the same logic 1 Timothy 1:16 applies to Paul individually. Nothing in a Pharisee actively
persecuting the church "earned" an apostleship -- if anything, his record ran as hard against the
outcome as a wild branch runs against ending up rooted in a cultivated tree. The *NIV Biblical
Theology Study Bible* draws exactly this parallel at 1:16, on independent grounds: "Just as God
chose Israel for no merit or cause on their part (Deut 9:4-5), he showed mercy to Paul despite his
heinous error. If Paul could be saved, anyone could." Two arguments, same shape, run by the same
author, applied once to a nation-category and once to a name -- but two arguments, not one
declared type. Paul even applies a third version of the same shape to his own place among the
apostles:

> ✝️ 1 Corinthians 15:8-10 (ESV)
>
> 8 Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me. 9 For I am the least of the apostles, unworthy to be called an apostle, because I persecuted the church of God. 10 But by the grace of God I am what I am, and his grace toward me was not in vain.

**ἔκτρωμα** (*ektroma*, ek-TRO-mah, G1626) is a hard word -- "a miscarriage, a stillbirth," used
in the Septuagint of a child born dead (Numbers 12:12, Job 3:16, Ecclesiastes 6:3). It is the only
time the word appears in the New Testament, and Paul aims it at himself: he did not arrive at
apostleship on the normal schedule, by walking with Jesus through his earthly ministry as the
Eleven had. He arrived out of sequence, out of season, by an appearance of the risen Christ years
after the others had already received theirs. Grafted contrary to nature; born at the wrong time;
mercy shown to the foremost of sinners -- three images, three passages, one recurring shape: God
including someone, or a whole people, on terms the ordinary order of things would not have
produced.

## Called, but not one of the Twelve

Paul's apostleship is real and is asserted as strongly as any of the Twelve's, but it is a
different kind of appointment, and the New Testament is careful about the difference. The Twelve
were qualified by a specific, closed set of conditions -- presence through the whole of Jesus's
public ministry and firsthand witness of the resurrection (Acts 1:21-22; see
[The Twelve: Disciples and Apostles](twelve-apostles.md)). Paul meets neither in the ordinary
sense. His own word for it, again, is *ektroma* -- last of all, and out of season. What he shares
with the Twelve is not the manner of the calling but its source: none of them chose the role, and
neither did he. "I did not receive it from any man, nor was I taught it, but I received it through
a revelation of Jesus Christ" (Galatians 1:12, ESV) is Paul's own claim to the same kind of
unmediated, top-down commissioning Peter, Andrew, James, and John received on a Galilean hillside.

## Theological principle

Scripture makes two distinct claims about Paul, and both are load-bearing on their own: he is a
named pattern of how far grace reaches into an unlikely life (1 Timothy 1:16), and he is the
specific apostle Christ sent to the Gentiles (Acts 9:15; Romans 11:13; Galatians 2:7-9). The two
are not the same claim, and the text never fuses them into a formal type the way it does Adam and
Christ (Romans 5:14). What does connect them, on the text's own terms rather than by import, is a
single recurring grammar of grace -- unearned, out of the natural order, "contrary to nature" --
that Paul applies with the same logic to his own conversion, to his place among the apostles, and
to the Gentiles he was sent to reach. Holding the distinction and the connection together, rather
than collapsing one into the other, is exactly the discipline this site's companion study on
[Israel and the Church](../israel-and-church/israel-and-the-church.md) already applies to the
olive tree itself -- an illustration Paul builds to make one pastoral point, not a technical type
to be pressed further than he presses it.

## Discussion questions

1. Jesus asks "why are you persecuting *me*," identifying himself directly with the church Saul
   was attacking. What follows for how seriously to take mistreatment of other believers?
2. 1 Timothy 1:16 says Paul's conversion was made public *on purpose*, "as an example." Whose
   past is God still asking you to see as evidence, not as disqualification?
3. The Jerusalem pillars ratified Paul's distinct calling to the Gentiles rather than requiring
   him to conform to Peter's pattern (Galatians 2:7-9). Where might a church today be pressuring
   someone's calling into a shape it was never meant to take?
4. This study distinguishes "Paul as a pattern of saved sinners" from "Paul as a type of grafted
   Gentiles" even though both are true about him. Why does that distinction matter for how a
   reader uses Paul's story to argue a point?
5. "Contrary to nature," "untimely born," "the foremost of sinners" -- three ways Paul describes
   his own inclusion as fundamentally unearned. What would change in your own testimony if you
   described it with the same bluntness?
