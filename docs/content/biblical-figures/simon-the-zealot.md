# Simon the Zealot

Everything Scripture tells us about this man is contained in one word attached to his name — and
that word is the most politically explosive description given to any of the Twelve. He never speaks.
The label is the story.

## Key Takeaways

### Lessons about Jesus

Jesus put a Zealot and a tax collector in the same group of twelve and kept them there. He did not
ask either to renounce his history first, and he did not separate them to keep the peace. The
composition of the Twelve was itself a claim about what the kingdom does to enmity.

### Memory verse

> ✝️ Luke 6:15 (ESV)
>
> 15 and Matthew, and Thomas, and James the son of Alphaeus, and Simon who was called the Zealot,

### Be transformed

Simon's politics were not a peripheral hobby; they were a life-and-death commitment about who should
rule. He was called anyway, and so was the man on the other side. Whatever political division you
consider genuinely disqualifying in a fellow believer, Jesus assembled a group that was worse.

### Prayer

Lord, you gathered enemies into one company and made them brothers. We confess how quickly we sort
people by their politics and how slowly we sit down with them. Make your table wider than our
instincts.

## The name, and a mistranslation worth correcting

Luke calls him <span lang="grc">Ζηλωτής</span> (*Zēlōtēs*, zay-lo-TACE), "Zealot" (Luke 6:15;
Acts 1:13).

Matthew and Mark use a different word: <span lang="grc">Καναναῖος</span> (*Kananaios*,
kah-nah-NAH-yoss). This is **not** Greek and has nothing to do with Canaan or with Cana. It is
Aramaic — from <span dir="rtl">קַנְאָן</span> *qanʾan*, from the root
<span dir="rtl">קָנָא</span> *qanaʾ*, "to be jealous, to be zealous."

The KJV rendered it "Simon the Canaanite," and Strong's own entry flags this directly as an error,
noting the word was mistaken for a derivative of <span lang="grc">Χαναάν</span>, "Canaan." The two
Greek words are unrelated.

So Matthew and Mark **transliterate** the Aramaic epithet; Luke **translates** it for readers who
would not know Aramaic. One man, one meaning, two ways of getting it into Greek — and Luke's habit
of explaining Semitic terms for a Gentile audience is exactly what we would expect of him.

**Which translations preserve this?** The ESV renders *Kananaios* as "Zealot" at both Matthew 10:4
and Mark 3:18, doing for the English reader what Luke did for his Greek one — so all four lists
read consistently and the difficulty never surfaces. The KJV reader, by contrast, meets "Simon the
Canaanite" in Matthew and Mark and "Simon Zelotes" in Luke, with no way to tell from the English
that these render the same underlying word. Here the smoother modern translation is straightforwardly
more accurate, and a teacher working from an older version should correct it out loud.

## Background: what a Zealot was

There is a real historical question about how precisely to read the label, and it is worth stating
rather than papering over.

The **Zealots** as an organised revolutionary party are firmly attested at the time of the Jewish
revolt against Rome (AD 66-70) — decades *after* Simon was called. In Jesus' own ministry the term
may describe a formal affiliation with an earlier nationalist movement, or it may describe a
temperament: a man zealous for the law and for Israel's independence, in the tradition of Phinehas
(Numbers 25:11) and the Maccabees.

Either way the direction is unmistakable. The zealot outlook held that Israel had no king but God,
that paying tribute to Caesar was a form of apostasy, and that the occupation should be resisted by
force. Its extreme wing later took to assassinating Jewish collaborators in public places.

**Matthew collected taxes for Rome.** The two men are named four places apart in the same list.

Notice, too, what this makes of the question put to Jesus about whether it is lawful to pay taxes to
Caesar (Matthew 22:15-22). Both men were standing there. Whatever answer Jesus gave was going to
land hard on one of them.

## What Scripture records

Four verses — the four lists (Matthew 10:4; Mark 3:18; Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13). He is in the upper
room after the ascension. He says nothing anywhere.

## What tradition says

Very little that can be relied on. Later traditions send him to Persia, Egypt, North Africa, and —
in a persistent British legend with no ancient support at all — to Britain. He is often paired with
Thaddaeus in martyrdom accounts, largely because they are adjacent in the lists. The sources are
late and mutually contradictory.

## Takeaway

Simon's inclusion is an argument made by arrangement rather than by words. A man committed to
driving Rome out, and a man who worked for Rome, spent three years in the same group of twelve
under the same teacher. Neither is recorded as having demanded the other's removal.
