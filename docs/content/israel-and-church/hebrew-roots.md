# Hebrew roots of Christianity

Jesus's Bible, and the apostles' Bible, was the Hebrew Scriptures. Paul tells Timothy that "all
Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for
training in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16). The "Scripture" he has first in view there is the Old
Testament. The New Testament was still being written, and no collection of it yet existed.

When the risen Christ explained Himself to two confused disciples on the Emmaus road, He opened the
Scriptures they already had (Luke 24:27, below).

**The Old Testament, read the way Jesus and His apostles read it, is the record God used to explain
who Christ is, and it still does that job for the church today.** A church that skips past it, or
mines it only for isolated proof-texts, is skipping the book its own Lord treated as authoritative and
the tool His first followers reached for to explain Him.

### Types & Prophecy

**Type.** The Old Testament's institutions function as **σκιά** (*skia*, skee-AH, G4639), shadow:
its sacrifices, its priesthood, its appointed feasts (Hebrews 10:1; Colossians 2:17).

A shadow is cast by something real. It becomes recognisable once you see the object casting it,
which is why the New Testament keeps reaching back into the Old to say what Christ fulfils.

**Prophecy.** Direct prediction is a different thing from the type-shadow relationship. Jesus told
His disciples that everything written about Him in the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms must be
fulfilled (Luke 24:27, 44).

### Lessons about Jesus

- He treated the Old Testament as the authoritative text He had come to fulfil (Matthew 5:17).
- He read Himself out of it, in His own words, on the day of His resurrection (Luke 24:27).
- He kept its appointed feasts (John 2:13; 7:10), and the apostles read His death and resurrection
  through the one they were keeping when He died (1 Corinthians 5:7).

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** The Old Testament is the Scripture Jesus called authoritative and the apostles preached
  from.
- **Attitude.** Read like the Bereans, who "received the word with all eagerness, examining the
  Scriptures daily to see if these things were so" (Acts 17:11). They searched the Old Testament to
  test a message about Jesus.
- **Do.** When a New Testament writer quotes or alludes to an Old Testament text, stop and read the
  original passage in its own setting. The point is usually sharper there than the brief citation
  shows.

## Cautions

Two different things share the phrase "Hebrew roots," and this study is about the first.

1. **Studying the Old Testament's shadows and types, its Torah, its feasts, and its prophecy**, the
   subject of the rest of this page, is what Jesus and the apostles did, and it is what this section
   of the site is for.
2. **"Hebrew Roots" as a theological movement** teaches things this site does not hold, chiefly that
   the Church *is* Israel, or that Gentile believers are a scattered tribe of Israel rediscovering its
   identity. Scripture sends the gospel to the nations *as* nations. [Israel and the
   Church](israel-and-the-church.md) sets out why Scripture keeps the two distinct, and nothing below
   blurs that line.

A related error runs the other direction: treating study of the Torah as grounds for requiring
Gentile believers to keep it. Paul's warning against it is some of his sharpest language: "if you
accept circumcision, Christ will be of no advantage to you… you are severed from Christ, you who
would be justified by the law" (Galatians 5:2-4). The Jerusalem council reached the same conclusion
by a gentler route, refusing to place on Gentile disciples "a yoke… that neither our fathers nor we
have been able to bear" (Acts 15:10). Studying what the Torah reveals about God's character, and what
it foreshadows in Christ, is this page's subject; binding Gentile believers to keep it is the error
both texts are written against.

## Key features

Six things repay a church's attention, each a different angle on the same Old Testament.

### Shadows and types

An Old Testament person, object, or ritual can pattern Christ or the gospel without saying so in
words. The New Testament's word for this is **τύπος** (*typos*, TOO-pos, G5179; Romans 5:14). The
tabernacle's sacrifices are the clearest case: they covered sin repeatedly and never finally, "since
the law has but a shadow of the good things to come instead of the true form of these realities"
(Hebrews 10:1). Recognising the shadow is what lets a reader recognise the substance when it arrives.
This site's [Melchizedek priesthood](../jesus/melchizedek-priesthood.md) study works one such type
through in full.

### Torah

**Torah**, <span dir="rtl">תּוֹרָה</span> (*torah*, toh-RAH, H8451), is usually rendered "law" in
English. TWOT glosses it *direction*, a noun from <span dir="rtl">יָרָה</span> (*yarah*, yah-RAH,
H3384), a verb that can mean to point out or to teach. Jesus held every letter of it in place: "Not
an iota, not a dot, will pass from the Law until all is accomplished" (Matthew 5:18). The psalmist
had already called God's word a lamp to walk by (Psalm 119:105).

### Festivals

**Moed**, <span dir="rtl">מוֹעֵד</span> (*moed*, moh-ED, H4150, "appointed time"), is the word behind
"the appointed feasts of the LORD" (Leviticus 23:4). Each one is a holy convocation, kept on its own
calendar date every year, and the New Testament reads at least one of them as already fulfilled:
"Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed. Let us therefore celebrate the festival… with the
unleavened bread of sincerity and truth" (1 Corinthians 5:7-8). The [Feasts](../feasts/feasts.md)
section works through all seven in full. This page makes a narrower point: a church unfamiliar with
what a feast was foreshadowing cannot recognise it being fulfilled.

### Creation

The seven-day pattern is set at creation, before Israel receives a single command. "On the seventh
day God finished his work… and he rested on the seventh day" (Genesis 2:2).

[A Day Is a Thousand Years](../last-things/day-is-a-thousand-years.md#six-days-of-history) follows
that week through the whole of history, and [The Zadok Calendar](../feasts/zadok-calendar.md) traces
the same seven-based structure under Israel's appointed times, sabbatical years and jubilees included.

### Prophecy

The Old Testament also predicts, and Jesus read its predictions as being about Himself: "Beginning
with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning
himself" (Luke 24:27), on His resurrection day. [Bible Prophecy
Essentials](../last-things/prophecy-essentials.md) checks specific fulfilled prophecies against
source. This page's point is the starting posture: prophecy is where a reader should expect to find
Christ.

### Extra-biblical Jewish texts

Not everything the New Testament's first readers knew was inside the canon, and the New Testament
authors sometimes name their sources. Jude names his. "Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied,
saying, 'Behold, the Lord comes with ten thousands of his holy ones'" (Jude 14) quotes 1 Enoch 1:9, a
Jewish apocalyptic work the *ESV Study Bible* dates to the second or first century BC. That date is
debated for the part Jude quotes: 1:9 opens the Book of the Watchers (chapters 1–36), which many
scholars place earlier, and copies of 1 Enoch survive among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Quoting it does not make 1 Enoch Scripture (*ESV Study Bible*, note on Jude 14-16). Jude writes to
readers who knew the book, and he draws on it as a true example on this one point.

Five verses earlier he alludes to another non-biblical work without naming it. The archangel Michael
"disputing about the body of Moses" (Jude 9) has no source in the Old Testament's account of Moses's
death (Deuteronomy 34:5-7). Which work Jude drew on is contested. Two study Bibles name the
*Assumption of Moses*; the *ESV Study Bible* suggests "perhaps a lost section of a work called
Testament of Moses." The Jewish work that survives under those titles (R. H. Charles published it as
the *Assumption of Moses*) breaks off before Moses dies (12:8), so the dispute is not in what
remains. Origen names the book: "The Ascension of Moses (a little treatise, of which the Apostle Jude
makes mention in his Epistle)" (*De Principiis* 3.2.1). Either way, Jude expects his readers to
recognise a story his letter does not stop to explain.

Paul does the same with names. "Jannes and Jambres opposed Moses" (2 Timothy 3:8) names Pharaoh's
magicians from Exodus 7, who go unnamed in Exodus. The names come from early extrabiblical Jewish
writings (*ESV Study Bible*, note on 2 Timothy 3:8), and Paul expects Timothy to recognise them
without explanation.

The Mishnah is a different case. It was redacted around AD 200, after the New Testament, so no New
Testament author quotes it the way Jude quotes 1 Enoch.

Some of what it documents is older than its redaction date: it records the liturgical practice of the
feasts, and how much of that a first-century Jew would already have known is debated. This site
therefore cites it as background: the four cups of the Passover Seder in [The Last Supper and the
Cups of Passover](../feasts/last-supper-four-cups.md), and the shofar procedure behind [the Feast of
Trumpets](../feasts/trumpets.md).

## Why this still matters for the church today

Paul says why. "Whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction" (Romans 15:4).
He writes that of the Old Testament, to a church centuries after it was first written, and he calls
it instruction.

A church that treats the Old Testament as solved background reading loses what Paul says you still
need for endurance and hope.

## Discussion questions

1. Colossians 2:17 calls the festivals, new moons, and Sabbaths a shadow whose substance is Christ.
   What changes about how you read an Old Testament ritual once you look for what it is a shadow
   *of*?
2. Jesus opened the Hebrew Scriptures to explain Himself on the Emmaus road (Luke 24:27) before a
   single Gospel had been written. What does that say about how much of the church's Christology was
   already available before the New Testament existed?
3. The caution section names two opposite errors: collapsing the Church into Israel, and requiring
   Gentile believers to keep the Torah. What keeps a church from drifting into either one while still
   taking the Old Testament seriously?
4. Which of the six features (shadows and types, Torah, festivals, creation, prophecy, or
   extra-biblical texts) is most unfamiliar to you, and what would studying it change about how you
   read the rest of Scripture?
5. Jude quotes 1 Enoch as true on one point without treating it as Scripture (Jude 14-15). What is
   the difference between citing a non-biblical source the way Jude does here and treating it as
   authoritative the way Scripture is?
