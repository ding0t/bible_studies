# Idolatry

A carpenter cuts down a tree. Half of it he burns to bake his bread and roast his meat. "And the rest
of it he makes into a god, his idol, and falls down to it and worships it. He prays to it and says,
'Deliver me, for you are my god!'" (Isaiah 44:17, ESV).

The question this study began with was "Is idolatry the worship of demons?" Scripture's answer has
two halves. The block of wood is nothing. The worship offered to it goes to demons. And most of the
idols a Christian meets are never carved at all, because Paul names one of them in the middle of a
list of the heart's desires: "covetousness, which is idolatry" (Colossians 3:5, ESV).

**In one sentence:** Idolatry is giving a created thing the trust and service that belong to God; in
its plainest form it is a made god, whose worship goes to demons, and in the form Paul names in the
church it is greed, money trusted in God's place, while pride is the self-sufficiency that opens the
door to both, and God answers every one of them with a jealous love and the call back to His Son
Jesus Christ, the true God.

### Lessons about Jesus

- **Jesus is the true God the idols counterfeit.** John closes his letter by naming Him: "his Son
  Jesus Christ. He is the true God and eternal life. Little children, keep yourselves from idols"
  (1 John 5:20-21, ESV).
- **Jesus is the reason you can put the idol to death.** Paul's command in Colossians 3:5 rests on
  what has already happened: "you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians
  3:3, ESV).
- **Jesus delivers from the wrath idolatry earns.** The Thessalonians "turned to God from idols … to
  wait for his Son from heaven, … Jesus who delivers us from the wrath to come" (1 Thessalonians
  1:9-10, ESV).

- **Think.** An idol is whatever you trust to deliver you and whatever you serve (Isaiah 44:17;
  Matthew 6:24). Money is the one Paul names (Colossians 3:5).
- **Attitude.** Hold everything you have as given. "It is he who gives you power to get wealth"
  (Deuteronomy 8:18, ESV).
- **Do.** Ask the three questions in [Three questions for your heart](#three-questions-for-your-heart),
  name the answer to God, and give away something the answer has a grip on.

## What idolatry is

The first commandment forbids two things, and they describe idolatry exactly: "You shall not bow
down to them or serve them, for I the LORD your God am a jealous God" (Exodus 20:5, ESV). Bowing is
worship. Serving is the life that follows it.

Underneath both is trust. The carpenter's prayer is "Deliver me, for you are my god!" (Isaiah 44:17,
ESV). Jeremiah sets the two trusts side by side:

> ✝️ Jeremiah 17:5, 7 (ESV)
>
> 5 Thus says the LORD: "Cursed is the man who trusts in man and makes flesh his strength, whose
> heart turns away from the LORD. … 7 "Blessed is the man who trusts in the LORD, whose trust is the
> LORD."

Paul sums up the whole human story the same way: people "exchanged the truth about God for a lie and
worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator" (Romans 1:25, ESV). His verb for "served"
is λατρεύω (*latreuō*, G3000), the word for religious service. So idolatry is giving a created thing
the trust, worship and service that belong to the God who made it. The created thing may be an image,
a power, money or a man. In every case the heart has turned toward it and away from the LORD.

## Made to worship

God made you for Himself. He speaks of His people as "everyone who is called by my name, whom I
created for my glory" (Isaiah 43:7, ESV). Of His Son, Paul writes, "all things were created through
him and for him" (Colossians 1:16, ESV). Heaven's worship gives the same reason: "Worthy are you,
our Lord and God, to receive glory and honor and power, for you created all things" (Revelation
4:11, ESV).

Refusing God does not switch that design off. Paul traces what happens instead. "Although they knew
God, they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him" (Romans 1:21, ESV). Four verses later the
same people "worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator" (Romans 1:25, ESV). The
worship they withheld from God went to something He made. Jeremiah draws the same picture. A people
who forsake "the fountain of living waters" go on drinking: they hew out "broken cisterns that can
hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13, ESV). The thirst stays, so they dig.

Paul saw it in Athens. "His spirit was provoked within him as he saw that the city was full of idols"
(Acts 17:16, ESV). He told the Athenians they were "very religious" and pointed to their altar "To
the unknown god" (Acts 17:22-23, ESV). Then he told them why: God made every nation "that they should
seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him and find him. Yet he is actually not far from each
one of us" (Acts 17:27, ESV). A city full of idols was a city full of people looking for God in the
wrong places.

Scripture states the two ends plainly: God created you for His glory, and those who refuse Him
worship the creature. The step between them, that the need to worship is built into every person
and is always filled with something, is how the church has long read these texts. Augustine opened
his *Confessions* by telling God that He made us for Himself and that our heart is restless until it
rests in Him (*Confessions* 1.1). Ecclesiastes says God "has put eternity into man's heart"
(Ecclesiastes 3:11, ESV).

This shows that God made you to find your joy in Him. Every idol is that design misdirected, a thirst
for the fountain carried to a cistern. So the answer to idolatry is more than giving the idol up. It
is coming back to the worship you were made for.

## The made god is nothing

### A god you have to carry

Scripture mocks idols. Isaiah's carpenter warms himself at one half of the log and kneels to the
other, and never stops to ask, "Shall I fall down before a block of wood?" (Isaiah 44:19, ESV).
Jeremiah says idols "are like scarecrows in a cucumber field, and they cannot speak; they have to be
carried, for they cannot walk" (Jeremiah 10:5, ESV). A god you have to carry is a god you control,
and that is its appeal.

The psalmist adds the cost:

> ✝️ Psalm 115:4-5, 8 (ESV)
>
> 4 Their idols are silver and gold, the work of human hands. 5 They have mouths, but do not speak;
> eyes, but do not see. … 8 Those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them.

You become like what you trust. The worshipper of a deaf god grows deaf to God.

The Hebrew has a name for this kind of god. "All the gods of the peoples are worthless idols, but
the LORD made the heavens" (Psalm 96:5, ESV), where "worthless idols" is
<span dir="rtl">אֱלִילִים</span> (*ʾelilim*, eh-lee-LEEM, H457), a pun on *ʾelohim*, "gods".
[Those Called Gods](../spiritual-beings/those-called-gods.md#what-the-nations-worshipped) sets that
pun beside the other names Scripture gives the gods of the nations.

### The exchange

Israel's first idol shows what an idol costs the worshipper. At Sinai "they made a calf in Horeb and
worshiped a metal image. They exchanged the glory of God for the image of an ox that eats grass"
(Psalm 106:19-20, ESV). The verb is <span dir="rtl">מוּר</span> (*mur*, H4171), "exchange", and
Jeremiah uses it twice in one verse: "Has a nation changed its gods, even though they are no gods? But
my people have changed their glory for that which does not profit" (Jeremiah 2:11, ESV). Then he
names the two evils: "they have forsaken me, the fountain of living waters, and hewed out cisterns for
themselves, broken cisterns that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13, ESV).

Paul takes up the psalm's wording in Greek. The Septuagint of Psalm 106:20 reads ἠλλάξαντο τὴν δόξαν
αὐτῶν ἐν ὁμοιώματι μόσχου, "they exchanged their glory for the likeness of a calf". Romans 1:23
reads ἤλλαξαν τὴν δόξαν τοῦ ἀφθάρτου θεοῦ ἐν ὁμοιώματι, "exchanged the glory of the immortal God for
images". The verb, the object and the phrase "for the likeness" are the same. Paul reads the sin of
the nations through the sin of Israel at Sinai.

This shows that God is the living God and the fountain, and that He is glorious. Every idol is a
trade of that glory for something smaller, something you can hold.

## The worship goes to demons

### Paul's two statements

In 1 Corinthians Paul writes to Christians in a city where meat was sacrificed in temples, sold in
the market and eaten in temple dining rooms (1 Corinthians 8:10). He makes two statements about
idols, two chapters apart, in one argument.

The first agrees with the Corinthians: "we know that 'an idol has no real existence,' and that 'there
is no God but one'" (1 Corinthians 8:4, ESV). The Greek is plainer: οὐδὲν εἴδωλον ἐν κόσμῳ, "an
idol is nothing in the world" (*eidōlon*, G1497).

The second comes when he turns to the temple meal itself:

> ✝️ 1 Corinthians 10:19-22 (ESV)
>
> 19 What do I imply then? That food offered to idols is anything, or that an idol is anything?
> 20 No, I imply that what pagans sacrifice they offer to demons and not to God. I do not want you
> to be participants with demons. 21 You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons. You
> cannot partake of the table of the Lord and the table of demons. 22 Shall we provoke the Lord to
> jealousy? Are we stronger than he?

Paul holds both. The image is nothing. The sacrifice is real, and it reaches someone:
δαιμονίοις καὶ οὐ θεῷ, "to demons and not to God" (*daimonion*, dai-MON-ee-on, G1140). A worshipper
who eats at that table becomes a κοινωνός (*koinōnos*, G2844), a "participant" or partner, with
demons, the same word Paul has just used of Israel's priests and the altar (1 Corinthians 10:18).
[The Lord's Supper](../feasts/lords-supper.md) sets out what the other table, the Lord's, shares.

### The Song of Moses

Paul is quoting. "To demons and not to God" is the Septuagint of Deuteronomy 32:17 word for word:
ἔθυσαν δαιμονίοις καὶ οὐ θεῷ, "they sacrificed to demons and not to God". His next question,
"Shall we provoke the Lord to jealousy?", takes the verb παραζηλόω (*parazēloō*, G3863) from four
verses later, where God says, "They have made me jealous with what is no god" (Deuteronomy 32:21,
ESV). Paul reads the Corinthian temple through the Song of Moses.

> ✝️ Deuteronomy 32:16-17 (ESV)
>
> 16 They stirred him to jealousy with strange gods; with abominations they provoked him to anger.
> 17 They sacrificed to demons that were no gods, to gods they had never known, to new gods that
> had come recently, whom your fathers had never dreaded.

The Hebrew word is <span dir="rtl">שֵׁדִים</span> (*shedim*, shay-DEEM, H7700), and it occurs
twice in the Old Testament, here and in Psalm 106:37: "They sacrificed their sons and their
daughters to the demons." The next verse calls the same victims sacrificed "to the idols of Canaan"
(Psalm 106:38, ESV), so the psalm names the idol and the demon in one act. The law had already
forbidden Israel to "sacrifice their sacrifices to goat demons, after whom they whore" (Leviticus
17:7, ESV), where "goat demons" is <span dir="rtl">שְׂעִירִם</span> (*seʿirim*, H8163), "hairy
ones".

One modern study Bible reads the *shedim* of Deuteronomy 32:17 as minor gods of the region, beings
whose moral nature cannot be determined, and concludes they "would not have been considered demons"
(*NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible*, note on Deuteronomy 32:17). Paul read the verse in Greek as
δαιμόνια and built his warning to the church on it, and the Septuagint translators had read it that
way before him. The apostle's reading of Moses carries the argument here.

### Where Scripture puts the demons

Every text that names demons in connection with idols describes **sacrifice or worship offered to a
god**:

| Text | What is offered | To whom |
|---|---|---|
| Leviticus 17:7 | "their sacrifices" | "goat demons" |
| Deuteronomy 32:17 | sacrifice | "demons that were no gods" |
| Psalm 106:37-38 | "their sons and their daughters" | "the demons", "the idols of Canaan" |
| 1 Corinthians 10:20 | "what pagans sacrifice" | "demons and not … God" |
| Revelation 9:20 | worship | "demons and idols of gold and silver" |

None of them speaks of covetousness or pride. So the statement this study can defend is precise:
**where a god is worshipped in the LORD's place, the worship goes to demons.** Scripture does not say
that a demon stands behind every desire that takes God's place in the heart.

The devil is in the background of all sin in a different way. Before Christ every believer walked
"following the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that is now at work in the sons of
disobedience" (Ephesians 2:2, ESV). James calls the wisdom of "bitter jealousy and selfish ambition"
"earthly, unspiritual, demonic" (James 3:14-15, ESV), and answers the heart's warring desires with
"Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (James 4:7, ESV). That is the enemy's work against
every believer. It is a different claim from 1 Corinthians 10:20, and the two should be kept
distinct. A Christian who loves money is an idolater by Paul's own word. Scripture calls that sin
greed and calls him to put it to death. It does not call his bank account an altar to demons.

## Greed, the idolatry Paul names

### "Which is idolatry"

Paul's list in Colossians 3:5 runs from the body to the heart: sexual immorality, impurity, passion,
evil desire, "and covetousness, which is idolatry" (Colossians 3:5, ESV). The Greek singles out the
last item. It has the article, τὴν πλεονεξίαν, and the relative ἥτις ἐστὶν εἰδωλολατρία, "the very
thing that is idolatry". The word is πλεονεξία (*pleonexia*, pleh-oh-nex-EE-ah, G4124), "wanting
more". Ephesians 5:5 says it again: "everyone who … is covetous (that is, an idolater), has no
inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and God" (ESV). εἰδωλολατρία (*eidōlolatria*, G1495) occurs four
times in the New Testament (1 Corinthians 10:14; Galatians 5:20; Colossians 3:5; 1 Peter 4:3), and
greed is the only sin it is used to define.

### Job's gold and the Babylonian's net

Paul did not invent the idea. Job, defending his integrity, puts gold in the same list as the sun and
the moon:

> ✝️ Job 31:24-28 (ESV)
>
> 24 "If I have made gold my trust or called fine gold my confidence, 25 if I have rejoiced because
> my wealth was abundant or because my hand had found much, 26 if I have looked at the sun when it
> shone, or the moon moving in splendor, 27 and my heart has been secretly enticed, and my mouth has
> kissed my hand, 28 this also would be an iniquity to be punished by the judges, for I would have
> been false to God above.

Trusting gold and kissing the hand to the moon are one sin to Job. Both are "false to God above".

Habakkuk sees it in Babylon. The Chaldeans are "guilty men, whose own might is their god!" (Habakkuk
1:11, ESV), and the prophet pictures their king as a fisherman who "sacrifices to his net and makes
offerings to his dragnet; for by them he lives in luxury" (Habakkuk 1:16, ESV). The net is the means
of his wealth, and he worships it.

### Two masters

Jesus puts money in God's place by name: "No one can serve two masters … You cannot serve God and
money" (Matthew 6:24, ESV). His verb is δουλεύω (*douleuō*, G1398), to serve as a slave serves. It is
the word Paul uses when the Thessalonians "turned to God from idols to serve the living and true God"
(1 Thessalonians 1:9, ESV). Money is a master that takes a slave's service, and the only alternative
Jesus offers is the living God.

He told a parable to show where the service ends. A rich man with a full harvest builds bigger barns
and says to his soul, "Soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be
merry." God answers, "Fool! This night your soul is required of you" (Luke 12:19-20, ESV). Jesus
introduced it with the warning, "Be on your guard against all covetousness, for one's life does not
consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15, ESV).

Greed is idolatry because money is trusted to do what only God does. Paul tells Timothy to charge
the rich "not to be haughty, nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but on God, who
richly provides us with everything to enjoy" (1 Timothy 6:17, ESV). Hope, security and provision are
the things money promises, and they belong to God. This shows that God is the giver. He "richly
provides", and the covetous heart takes the gift and forgets the hand.

## Pride, the door to the idol

### Self-sufficiency comes first

Is pride an idolatry of the self? Scripture's own words place it one step earlier. Moses warns Israel
what wealth will do:

> ✝️ Deuteronomy 8:12-14, 17, 19 (ESV)
>
> 12 lest, when you have eaten and are full and have built good houses and live in them, 13 and when
> your herds and flocks multiply and your silver and gold is multiplied and all that you have is
> multiplied, 14 then your heart be lifted up, and you forget the LORD your God … 17 Beware lest you
> say in your heart, 'My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth.' … 19 And if you
> forget the LORD your God and go after other gods and serve them and worship them, I solemnly warn
> you today that you shall surely perish.

The order is wealth, a lifted heart, forgetting God, and then other gods. The Song of Moses tells the
same story after it happened: "Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked … then he forsook God who made him …
They sacrificed to demons that were no gods" (Deuteronomy 32:15, 17, ESV). Self-sufficiency is the
door. The idol comes through it.

### "Idolatry of self"

Scripture never calls pride idolatry. The nearest verse is Samuel's rebuke of Saul: "presumption is
as iniquity and idolatry" (1 Samuel 15:23, ESV). "Idolatry" there is <span dir="rtl">תְרָפִים</span>
(*teraphim*, H8655), household idols, and the sentence is a comparison: stubborn self-will before
God's word is *as* serious as the idols Israel knew it must not keep.

What Scripture does describe is the proud heart claiming God's place. To the prince of Tyre God says,
"Because your heart is proud, and you have said, 'I am a god' … yet you are but a man, and no god"
(Ezekiel 28:2, ESV), and the man of lawlessness ends the story "proclaiming himself to be God" (2
Thessalonians 2:4, ESV). Paul's list of the last days opens with "lovers of self, lovers of money"
and closes with "lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God" (2 Timothy 3:2, 4, ESV).

So "idolatry of self" is a teaching phrase. It describes something true, the self put where God
belongs, but it is not a term the Bible uses. The Bible's name for that sin is pride, and
[Pride](pride.md) is the study of it on this site. Used carefully, the two studies fit: pride is the
self-sufficient heart, greed is that heart's trust in what it owns, and the carved god is where both
end when they are left alone.

## The idol in the heart

### Ezekiel's elders

The elders of Israel in exile came and sat before Ezekiel to ask for a word from the LORD (Ezekiel
14:1; also 8:1; 20:1). Nothing in their hands or on their faces gave them away. God told the prophet
what He saw:

> ✝️ Ezekiel 14:3, 5-6 (ESV)
>
> 3 "Son of man, these men have taken their idols into their hearts, and set the stumbling block of
> their iniquity before their faces. Should I indeed let myself be consulted by them? … 5 that I may
> lay hold of the hearts of the house of Israel, who are all estranged from me through their idols.
> 6 "Therefore say to the house of Israel, Thus says the Lord GOD: Repent and turn away from your
> idols, and turn away your faces from all your abominations.

The Hebrew is <span dir="rtl">הֶעֱלוּ גִלּוּלֵיהֶם עַל־לִבָּם</span> (*heʿelu gilluleyhem ʿal
libbam*), "they have brought up their idols onto their heart". *Gillulim* (*gillulim*, gil-loo-LEEM,
H1544) is Ezekiel's word: 39 of its 48 occurrences are in his book. It is most likely formed from a
word for dung, so the prophet's name for the gods of Babylon is close to "dung pellets". That
derivation is widely held but not certain.

One study Bible suggests the elders wore amulets of Babylonian gods on their chests (*NIV Cultural
Backgrounds Study Bible*, note on Ezekiel 14:3). That may be part of it. God's own answer points
inward: He will "lay hold of the hearts", and the one He rejects is the man "who separates himself
from me, taking his idols into his heart" (Ezekiel 14:5, 7, ESV).

### What God does with it

God's first word to an idolater is "Repent and turn away from your idols" (Ezekiel 14:6, ESV). He
answers the man who comes to Him with an idol in his heart "myself" (Ezekiel 14:7), and He does it
"that I may lay hold of the hearts of the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 14:5, ESV). This shows that God
pursues the heart. He will not be consulted as one advisor among many, and He will not leave His
people estranged.

## Why God is jealous

The first commandment gives its reason: "for I the LORD your God am a jealous God" (Exodus 20:5,
ESV). The Song of Moses tells it as grief: "They stirred him to jealousy with strange gods"
(Deuteronomy 32:16, ESV). The law calls the sacrifice to goat demons whoring after them (Leviticus
17:7), and James calls a believer's friendship with the world adultery: "You adulterous people!"
(James 4:4, ESV).

The jealousy is a husband's. God bound Israel to Himself by covenant (Jeremiah 2:2), and the church
is betrothed to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:2). An idol is another lover, and God will not share His people with it. This shows that God
loves exclusively. His jealousy is the measure of His love: He wants all of you because He has given
all of Himself, and the same verse that warns of His jealousy promises "steadfast love to thousands
of those who love me and keep my commandments" (Exodus 20:6, ESV).

## Three questions for your heart

The texts above give three questions that find an idol without needing a carved one.

1. **What do I trust to deliver me?** The carpenter prayed to his log, "Deliver me" (Isaiah 44:17).
   Job's test was gold made "my trust" (Job 31:24). Ask what you reach for first when trouble comes,
   and what you would feel helpless without.
2. **What do I serve?** "You cannot serve God and money" (Matthew 6:24). Ask what gets your best
   hours, your first thoughts in the morning and your hardest work, and whether you would give the
   same to God.
3. **What could I not give up?** The rich fool's soul was "required" of him and his barns stayed
   behind (Luke 12:20). Ask what God could take from you that would leave you angry with Him.

Money will be one answer for most readers, because Paul names it. Other answers are a career, a
reputation, a relationship, a child, comfort, or your own strength. Each is a good gift. Each becomes
an idol when it is trusted and served in God's place.

## Turning back

God's command and His promise come together. "Repent and turn away from your idols" (Ezekiel 14:6,
ESV). The Thessalonians did exactly that: they "turned to God from idols to serve the living and true
God, and to wait for his Son from heaven, whom he raised from the dead, Jesus who delivers us from the
wrath to come" (1 Thessalonians 1:9-10, ESV). The turning has a direction. It is toward a living
God, a Son who rose, and a deliverance He has already won.

The worship the idol took comes home. Jesus told a woman at a well, beside a cistern, "the true
worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth, for the Father is seeking such people to
worship him" (John 4:23, ESV). [The Woman at the Well](../jesus/woman-at-well.md) follows that
conversation from Jeremiah's broken cisterns to the spring Jesus gives.

Paul grounds the command to put greed to death in what Christ has done:

> ✝️ Colossians 3:1, 3-4 (ESV)
>
> 1 If then you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated
> at the right hand of God. … 3 For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God. 4 When
> Christ who is your life appears, then you also will appear with him in glory.

The "therefore" of Colossians 3:5 points back to these verses. You put the idol to death because you
have already died with Christ, and your life is hidden with Him. Christ is your life, so the idol has
nothing left to offer you. This is the gospel at work: God forgives the idolater through the death of
His Son, unites him to Christ in His resurrection, and gives him a new heart to seek the things above.

John's letter ends on the same ground. "He is the true God and eternal life. Little children, keep
yourselves from idols" (1 John 5:20-21, ESV). The true God has come in His Son Jesus Christ, and you
are in Him. So you may keep yourself from every idol, the carved and the uncarved, because you have
been given the one they all imitate.

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** Paul calls πλεονεξία, "wanting more", "the very thing that is idolatry"
   (Colossians 3:5), and Jesus uses the slave's verb δουλεύω for serving money (Matthew 6:24). What
   does each word show you about how greed works?
2. **The text in its context.** Paul says "an idol has no real existence" (1 Corinthians 8:4) and
   then says sacrifices to idols go "to demons" (1 Corinthians 10:20). Why did the Corinthians need
   to hear both?
3. **Christ.** John ends "He is the true God and eternal life. Little children, keep yourselves from
   idols" (1 John 5:20-21). Why does he put those two sentences side by side?
4. **The hard part.** Every text that names demons behind idols describes sacrifice to a god, and
   none of them speaks of greed. How would you explain to a friend what Scripture does and does not
   say about a demon behind the love of money?
5. **Be transformed.** Moses warned that a full house and a lifted heart come before other gods
   (Deuteronomy 8:12-19). Which of the three questions above was hardest for you to answer, and what
   is one thing you will give away or give up this week because of it?
