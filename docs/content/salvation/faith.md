# Faith

Israel stood at Kadesh, on the edge of the land God had promised them. The spies came back, and the
people wept all night and wanted a new leader to take them back to Egypt. God said to Moses, "how
long will they not believe in me, in spite of all the signs that I have done among them?" (Numbers
14:11, ESV).

The next morning they changed their minds. "Here we are. We will go up to the place that the LORD
has promised, for we have sinned" (Numbers 14:40, ESV). Moses told them the LORD was not with them.
"But they presumed to go up" (Numbers 14:44, ESV), and they were beaten back to Hormah.

One day they would not go with God. The next morning they went without Him. Both days they trusted
themselves.

**In one sentence:** Faith is leaning your whole weight on God and on what He has finished in Jesus;
pride is the same trust pointed at yourself, and it shows two faces, refusing God's promise and then
trying to earn it, so the humble answer, every day, is to open the door to Jesus and say, "I
believe; help my unbelief."

## Key Takeaways

### Lessons about Jesus

- **Jesus finished the work.** "It is finished" (John 19:30, ESV). "By a single offering he has
  perfected for all time those who are being sanctified" (Hebrews 10:14, ESV). Faith rests on a work
  that needs nothing added.
- **Jesus is the Amen.** He names Himself "the Amen, the faithful and true witness" (Revelation 3:14,
  ESV). The Hebrew word for believing comes from the same root as *amen*. When you believe, you say amen to Him.
- **Jesus reaches for the sinking.** Peter cried "Lord, save me," and "Jesus immediately reached out
  his hand and took hold of him" (Matthew 14:30-31, ESV).
- **Jesus stands at the door and knocks.** He speaks to a church that said it needed nothing, and He
  offers to come in and eat with anyone who opens (Revelation 3:20).

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** Your salvation is finished. Jesus did the work, and God counts you righteous as a gift
  (Romans 4:4-5). Nothing you do today will make it more finished.
- **Attitude.** Rest. "Whoever has entered God's rest has also rested from his works as God did from
  his" (Hebrews 4:10, ESV). When you catch yourself trying to make it up to God, or to earn a better
  standing with Him, stop and thank Him instead.
- **Do.** Each morning this week, pray the father's prayer before anything else: "I believe; help my
  unbelief." Name one thing you cannot do on your own that day, and ask Jesus to come in and help.

## What the words mean

### Firm enough to lean on

The Hebrew verb is <span dir="rtl">אָמַן</span> (*ʾaman*, ah-MAHN, H539). Its root sense is to be firm,
steady and reliable. In the form translated "believe" it means to treat something as firm, and so to
lean on it. The word *amen* comes from the same root. To say "amen" is to say, "This is firm. I put
my weight on it."

The first time the Bible says anyone believed, it is Abraham, on a night when he had no son and God
told him to count the stars:

> ✝️ Genesis 15:6 (ESV)
>
> 6 And he believed the LORD, and he counted it to him as righteousness.

The Hebrew says he believed *in* the LORD (<span dir="rtl">בַּיהוָה</span>, *ba-YHWH*, "in the LORD"). Abraham's faith had a
person as its object. He leaned on God, and God counted him righteous.

### Hands held steady

The noun is <span dir="rtl">אֱמוּנָה</span> (*ʾemunah*, eh-moo-NAH, H530), firmness or faithfulness.
It appears 49 times in the Hebrew Bible, and its first use is a picture. Israel was fighting Amalek,
and as long as Moses held up his hands, Israel prevailed. His arms grew tired.

> ✝️ Exodus 17:12 (ESV)
>
> 12 But Moses' hands grew weary, so they took a stone and put it under him, and he sat on it, while
> Aaron and Hur held up his hands, one on one side, and the other on the other side. So his hands
> were steady until the going down of the sun.

"Steady" is *ʾemunah*. Moses' hands were firm because others held them up. This is a story, and it
defines nothing, but it is where the word begins, and it pictures faith well: firmness that comes
from being held.

God's own character is the same word. "The Rock, his work is perfect... A God of faithfulness"
(Deuteronomy 32:4, ESV). Isaiah made a pun on the root to a frightened king: "If you are not firm in
faith, you will not be firm at all" (Isaiah 7:9, ESV). Both halves are *ʾaman*. If you will not lean
on God, you will have nothing firm to stand on.

Faith in Scripture is putting your weight on God, because He is the one thing that will hold it. The Greek New Testament carries this into πίστις (*pistis*, G4102, "faith") and
πιστεύω (*pisteuō*, G4100, "believe"), which appear 242 and 241 times.

## The proud and the righteous

### Habakkuk's question

Habakkuk prophesied as Babylon was rising. He complained that God was doing nothing about injustice
in Judah, and God answered that He was raising up the Babylonians to judge it (Habakkuk 1:6). That
made it worse: how could a holy God use a nation more wicked than the one it punished? God told the
prophet to write the vision down and wait for it. "If it seems slow, wait for it; it will surely
come" (Habakkuk 2:3, ESV). Then He gave the heart of His answer:

> ✝️ Habakkuk 2:4 (ESV)
>
> 4 "Behold, his soul is puffed up; it is not upright within him, but the righteous shall live by his
> faith.

The New Testament quotes this verse three times as its text for faith (Romans 1:17; Galatians 3:11;
Hebrews 10:38). Paul quotes only the second half. God set the two halves side by side. There are
two kinds of people in this verse: the one who is swollen with himself, and the one who lives by
leaning on God.

In context, the swollen soul is Babylon and its king, whose pride the rest of the chapter judges in
five woes (Habakkuk 2:6-20). The ESV Study Bible sums up the contrast: a proud person relies on
himself; a righteous person relies on God.

### What is contested here

The first half of the verse is hard Hebrew. The CSB's translators footnote it "Hb obscure," and
translations differ over how to read it. The Greek Old Testament reads it differently again, and
Hebrews quotes that form: "if he shrinks back, my soul has no pleasure in him" (Hebrews 10:38, ESV).

The second half is also read two ways. *ʾEmunah* can mean "faith" or "faithfulness," and the CSB and
LSB footnote "Or faithfulness." The Greek Old Testament has "by *my* faithfulness," which would make
it God's. Both readings are true to the root. The righteous live by leaning on God, and what they
lean on is His faithfulness. Paul quotes it as "The righteous shall live by faith" (Romans 1:17, ESV)
and builds on the believer's trust, the same trust Abraham showed in Genesis 15:6.

## Kadesh: pride's two faces

### The same word

The Hebrew word behind "puffed up" is <span dir="rtl">עָפַל</span> (*ʿaphal*, ah-FAHL, H6075). It means
to swell. It appears twice in the Hebrew Bible. The first is Habakkuk 2:4. The second is Numbers
14:44: "they presumed to go up."

The NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible points to Numbers 14:44 to explain "puffed up" in its note on
Habakkuk 2:4. Read together, the two uses show what the word looks like in action: the swollen soul
of Habakkuk is the people who presumed at Kadesh, the story this study opened with.

### Two days, one sin

Read Numbers 14 again with that word in mind. On the first day Israel would not believe. God's own
diagnosis was "how long will they not believe in me" (Numbers 14:11, ESV), with *ʾaman*. Moses
retold it forty years later: "in spite of this word you did not believe the LORD your God"
(Deuteronomy 1:32, ESV).

On the second day they confessed, "we have sinned" (Numbers 14:40), and then tried to fix it by
effort. Moses remembered what they said: "We ourselves will go up and fight" (Deuteronomy 1:41,
ESV). They went without the ark and without Moses (Numbers 14:44). It was the same people, the same
hill country and the same promise. The first day they trusted their fear. The second day they trusted
their swords. Neither day did they lean on God.

That is what pride does with God's promises. It has two faces:

- **It will not believe.** "It's too good to be true. God could not save someone like me."
- **It tries to earn it.** "I have sinned, so now I'll make up for it. I will go up and fight."

The second face looks like repentance. It confesses sin and gets busy. But it still has the self at
the centre, and it is still unbelief.

### Rest from your works

The letter to the Hebrews reads the wilderness generation as a warning to Christians, and its text is
Psalm 95: "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts" (Psalm 95:7-8, ESV, quoted at
Hebrews 3:7-8). The psalm ends with God's oath, "They shall not enter my rest" (Psalm 95:11, ESV).
Hebrews gives the reason: "So we see that they were unable to enter because of unbelief" (Hebrews
3:19, ESV). Then it tells you what believing looks like:

> ✝️ Hebrews 4:9-10 (ESV)
>
> 9 So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God, 10 for whoever has entered God's rest
> has also rested from his works as God did from his.

God rested on the seventh day because His work was finished (Genesis 2:2). You enter His rest the
same way: by trusting that the work is finished and stopping your own. Jesus said "It is finished"
(John 19:30). Faith takes Him at His word.

## Trusting in yourself

### The same Greek phrase

Pride is faith aimed at yourself. Jesus told the parable
of the Pharisee and the tax collector "to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous"
(Luke 18:9, ESV). The Greek is πεποιθότας ἐφ᾽ ἑαυτοῖς (*pepoithotas eph' heautois*): having put
their confidence on themselves. The verb is πείθω (*peithō*, G3982), "to trust."

Paul uses the same phrase about the worst season of his ministry. In Asia he "despaired of life
itself" (2 Corinthians 1:8, ESV). "But that was to make us rely not on ourselves but on God who
raises the dead" (2 Corinthians 1:9, ESV). The Greek is again πεποιθότες ὦμεν ἐφ᾽ ἑαυτοῖς. God let Paul come to the end of himself so
that he would put his weight on God.

The Pharisee had faith. It rested on himself. The tax collector stood far off, beat his breast and
said, "God, be merciful to me, a sinner!" (Luke 18:13, ESV). He had nothing to lean on but God, and
he "went down to his house justified" (Luke 18:14, ESV). The [Pride](../sin/pride.md) study traces
this parable as pride's habit of comparison; here it is also a picture of faith. The man who went
home justified brought nothing.

### Wages and gifts

Paul explains why faith and works cannot share the credit:

> ✝️ Romans 4:4-5 (ESV)
>
> 4 Now to the one who works, his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due. 5 And to the one who
> does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is counted as righteousness,

Work earns wages, and wages are owed. Faith receives a gift. God "justifies the ungodly," so the
people He declares righteous had nothing to bring. "That is why it depends on faith, in order that
the promise may rest on grace" (Romans 4:16, ESV). And the two do not mix: "if it is by grace, it is
no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace" (Romans 11:6, ESV).

This is where [humility](../christian-life/humility.md) and faith meet. Humility is living before God
as someone who depends on Him for everything. Faith is that dependence at the point of salvation.
Both say, "I have nothing in my hand."

### Adding to grace

The Galatians had believed the gospel. Then teachers came and told them they needed circumcision too,
to be complete. Paul answered:

> ✝️ Galatians 3:3 (ESV)
>
> 3 Are you so foolish? Having begun by the Spirit, are you now being perfected by the flesh?

They were trying to earn a better salvation than the one they had been given. Paul's verdict is
severe: "You are severed from Christ, you who would be justified by the law; you have fallen away
from grace" (Galatians 5:4, ESV). Whatever you add to Christ as the ground of your standing with God,
you are leaning on it instead of Him. Readers differ over what "fallen away from grace" means for a
true believer. Paul's point in the verse is that law and grace cannot both be the ground you stand
on. This site's [statement of faith](../about/statement-of-faith.md) holds that those God justifies,
He also glorifies (Romans 8:30), and that "he who began a good work in you will bring it to
completion" (Philippians 1:6, ESV).

Paul had a longer list to add than anyone. He was "circumcised on the eighth day... a Hebrew of
Hebrews; as to the law, a Pharisee... as to righteousness under the law, blameless" (Philippians
3:5-6, ESV). He counted it all loss to "be found in him, not having a righteousness of my own that
comes from the law, but that which comes through faith in Christ" (Philippians 3:9, ESV).

### The one work

A crowd once asked Jesus, "What must we do, to be doing the works of God?" (John 6:28, ESV). They
asked about works, plural. Jesus answered with one: "This is the work of God, that you believe in him
whom he has sent" (John 6:29, ESV). The one work God asks for is to stop working for your standing
and trust His Son.

Luke records something similar. The apostles said, "Increase our faith!" (Luke 17:5, ESV). Jesus told
them a mustard seed of faith was enough, and then, without a break, told them about a servant who
comes in from the field and serves his master's supper first. "So you also, when you have done all
that you were commanded, say, 'We are unworthy servants; we have only done what was our duty'" (Luke
17:10, ESV). Luke put the two sayings together. Faith that grows is faith that stops keeping
accounts with God.

## Small faith in a great God

### The mustard seed

"If you have faith like a grain of mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, 'Move from here to
there,' and it will move" (Matthew 17:20, ESV). A mustard seed is tiny. What moves the mountain is
God, and a tiny faith in a great God is enough, because the weight is on Him.

If you worry that your faith is too weak, look at what Jesus measured. A faith you had to build up
to a certain size before God would act would be one more work to earn with. He asks you to lean on
Him, and He does the lifting.

### "Why did you doubt?"

Jesus called His disciples ὀλιγόπιστοι (*oligopistoi*, G3640), "you of little faith," five times
(Matthew 6:30; 8:26; 14:31; 16:8; Luke 12:28). The best-known is Peter on the water. Peter asked to
come, Jesus said "Come," and Peter walked on the water toward Jesus.

> ✝️ Matthew 14:30-31 (ESV)
>
> 30 But when he saw the wind, he was afraid, and beginning to sink he cried out, "Lord, save me."
> 31 Jesus immediately reached out his hand and took hold of him, saying to him, "O you of little
> faith, why did you doubt?"

Peter's faith got him out of the boat. What went wrong was where he looked: "when he saw the wind."
The word for "doubt," διστάζω (*distazō*, G1365), is used twice in the New Testament, here and at
Matthew 28:17. It means to waver or hesitate. Peter's weight shifted from Jesus to the
storm. And even then, Jesus caught him "immediately." His faithfulness held when Peter's faith
failed. "If we are faithless, he remains faithful— for he cannot deny himself" (2 Timothy 2:13, ESV).

### "Help my unbelief"

A father brought his son, tormented by a spirit since childhood, and said to Jesus, "if you can do
anything, have compassion on us and help us" (Mark 9:22, ESV). Jesus threw the "if" back at him:
"'If you can'! All things are possible for one who believes" (Mark 9:23, ESV).

> ✝️ Mark 9:24 (ESV)
>
> 24 Immediately the father of the child cried out and said, "I believe; help my unbelief!"

This is faith at its most honest. The father believes, and he knows his belief is not enough, so he
asks Jesus for that too. Jesus healed the boy. He did not wait for the father's faith to be complete.

Your faith is held up by Christ, too. In Ephesians 2:8 "this is not your own doing" uses a neuter
"this" (*touto*), and "faith" is feminine, so "this" points back to the whole of "by grace you have
been saved through faith": grace, faith and all, as "the gift of God." The [Assurance of
Salvation](assurance-of-salvation.md#the-grammar-of-not-your-own-doing) study sets out the grammar.
The verse ends "so that no one may boast" (Ephesians 2:9, ESV). Hebrews tells you where to look while
you run: "looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith" (Hebrews 12:2, ESV). The Greek
has "the faith," and the ESV supplies "our"; the CSB has "the source and perfecter of our faith."
Either way, Jesus is the one who begins faith and brings it to its end, and He ran that race Himself
to the cross.

## Faith that works

Paul and James both quote Genesis 15:6. Paul uses it to show that Abraham was counted righteous
before he had done anything (Romans 4:3-5). James uses it to show that Abraham's faith was alive:

> ✝️ James 2:22-23 (ESV)
>
> 22 You see that faith was active along with his works, and faith was completed by his works; 23 and
> the Scripture was fulfilled that says, "Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as
> righteousness"—and he was called a friend of God.

The two are answering different questions. Paul asks how a sinner is declared righteous before God,
and answers from Genesis 15, where Abraham only believed. James asks what living faith looks like,
and answers from Genesis 22, decades later, where Abraham offered Isaac because he trusted God to keep
His promise. James's opponent says he has faith and does nothing (James 2:14-17). Paul's opponent
does things and calls them righteousness. Each writer answers the one in front of him.

Faith that leans on God goes where He sends it. Paul, straight after "not a result of works, so that
no one may boast," writes: "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works"
(Ephesians 2:10, ESV). The works follow the salvation. They are what faith does, and they add
nothing to what Jesus finished.

## The door at Laodicea

### A church that needed nothing

The last of the seven letters in Revelation goes to Laodicea. It opens with Jesus' name for Himself:
"The words of the Amen, the faithful and true witness" (Revelation 3:14, ESV). The Greek is ὁ Ἀμήν
(*ho Amēn*, G281), the Hebrew word carried straight over. The one who is firm and faithful speaks to a
church that has stopped leaning on Him.

> ✝️ Revelation 3:17-18 (ESV)
>
> 17 For you say, I am rich, I have prospered, and I need nothing, not realizing that you are wretched,
> pitiable, poor, blind, and naked. 18 I counsel you to buy from me gold refined by fire, so that you
> may be rich, and white garments so that you may clothe yourself and the shame of your nakedness may
> not be seen, and salve to anoint your eyes, so that you may see.

Laodicea was a banking city with a textile trade, a medical establishment, and eye salve made in
the region (NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible). An earthquake damaged it in AD 60, and the city
rebuilt without help from Rome. Tacitus records that it recovered by its own resources, with no help
from Rome (*Annals* 14.27), and the ESV Study Bible says it declined imperial relief. The church had caught the city's spirit. "I need nothing"
echoes proud Ephraim: "Ah, but I am rich; I have found wealth for myself" (Hosea 12:8, ESV). It is
Habakkuk's puffed-up soul in a church pew.

Jesus tells them to buy from Him, and they have nothing to buy with. The ESV Study Bible hears Isaiah
behind it: "he who has no money, come, buy and eat! Come, buy wine and milk without money and without
price" (Isaiah 55:1, ESV). It is grace offered to people who have to admit they are poor to receive
it.

### He is knocking

> ✝️ Revelation 3:20 (ESV)
>
> 20 Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come
> in to him and eat with him, and he with me.

"I stand" is ἕστηκα (*hestēka*, G2476), a perfect form: Jesus has taken His stand at the door and is still
there. "Knock" is present tense, and He is knocking as He speaks. He is patient. He does not break
the door down. And what He wants is fellowship: to "eat with him, and he with me." In that world a
shared meal meant a restored relationship.

This verse is addressed first to a church, and Jesus is calling complacent believers back to Himself
(Revelation 3:19, "be zealous and repent"). It is also widely used as an invitation to someone who has
never believed, and "if anyone" allows it. The same Jesus does both. Opening the door looks the same
either way: you hear His voice, stop saying "I need nothing," and let Him in. A fuller study of this
verse is on the [backlog](../about/backlog.md#104-i-stand-at-the-door-and-knock).

### Every morning

You open the door more than once. Whether everyone in the Laodicean church was truly saved is
debated: "I will spit you out of my mouth" (Revelation 3:16, ESV) is severe, and "Those whom I love,
I reprove and discipline" (Revelation 3:19, ESV) is family language. Either way, a church can drift
into self-sufficiency, and so can any believer. Faith is a daily leaning. Manna came every morning
and would not keep (Exodus 16:19-20), and Jesus taught you to ask for "this day" (Matthew 6:11). The
writer of Lamentations, traditionally Jeremiah, sat in the ruins of Jerusalem and wrote:

> ✝️ Lamentations 3:22-23 (ESV)
>
> 22 The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; 23 they are new
> every morning; great is your faithfulness.

"Faithfulness" there is *ʾemunah*. God's firmness is new every morning, so your leaning can be too.
Each day Jesus is at the door. Each day you can say what the father said, and what Kadesh never
said: "I cannot do this on my own. I trust you. Help me." Faith like that is humble because it
brings nothing, and it is enough because it rests on Him.

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** The first use of *ʾemunah* is Moses' hands held steady by Aaron and Hur (Exodus
   17:12). What does it say about faith that the word begins with someone being held up?
2. **The text in its context.** Habakkuk 2:4 puts the puffed-up soul beside the one who lives by
   faith, and the same word for "puffed up" describes Israel's presumption at Kadesh (Numbers 14:44).
   Why might God have paired pride and faith in one verse?
3. **Christ.** Jesus calls Himself "the Amen" to a church that said it needed nothing (Revelation
   3:14, 17). What does it mean to say amen to Jesus?
4. **The two faces.** At Kadesh Israel first refused God's promise and then tried to earn it. Which
   face of pride do you slip into more easily: "God could not save me" or "I must make it up to Him"?
5. **The hard part.** James says "faith was completed by his works" (James 2:22), and Paul says God
   "justifies the ungodly" apart from works (Romans 4:5). How do the two fit together in your own
   life?
6. **Be transformed.** What would it look like for you to open the door to Jesus tomorrow morning,
   before anything else, and say, "I believe; help my unbelief"?

- **NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible** (Zondervan) — notes on Revelation 3:17-20: Laodicea's
  banking, textiles and eye salve, and the meal as covenant fellowship.
- **Christian Standard Bible** and **Legacy Standard Bible** — translator footnotes at Habakkuk 2:4
  ("Hb obscure"; "Or faithfulness").
- **Tacitus, *Annals* 14.27** — Laodicea's recovery from the AD 60 earthquake without Roman aid.
- **Macula Hebrew (WLC)** and **Macula Greek (SBLGNT)** — occurrence data for אָמַן, אֱמוּנָה, עָפַל,
  πίστις, πιστεύω, ὀλιγόπιστος and διστάζω, and the forms at Luke 18:9, 2 Corinthians 1:9 and
  Revelation 3:20.
- **Septuagint lemmas** (Open Scriptures Septuagint Project) — the Greek of Habakkuk 2:4.
