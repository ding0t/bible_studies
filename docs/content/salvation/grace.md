# Grace

Saul of Tarsus was "ravaging the church, and entering house after house, he dragged off men and
women and committed them to prison" (Acts 8:3, ESV). Years later he wrote to Timothy about it:

> ✝️ 1 Timothy 1:13-15 (ESV)
>
> 13 though formerly I was a blasphemer, persecutor, and insolent opponent. But I received mercy
> because I had acted ignorantly in unbelief, 14 and the grace of our Lord overflowed for me with
> the faith and love that are in Christ Jesus. 15 The saying is trustworthy and deserving of full
> acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost.

Saul had earned God's judgment. What he received was God's favour, and more of it than he could
hold. That is grace.

**In one sentence:** Grace is God's favour given freely, in His Son Jesus, to people who had earned
the opposite: it saves you apart from anything you bring, it overflows wherever sin has spread, and
the same grace trains you to live for God and works in you as you serve Him.

## Key Takeaways

*(This section follows the [Key Takeaways](../about/key-takeaways.md) format — see that page for what each part is for.)*

### Types & Prophecy

- **Israel at Sinai, a picture of the people grace makes.** God told Israel, "you shall be my
  treasured possession among all peoples" (Exodus 19:5, ESV), which the Greek Old Testament renders
  λαὸς περιούσιος (*laos periousios*). Titus 2:14 gives the phrase to the people Jesus redeems, "a
  people for his own possession" (ESV), the only New Testament use of περιούσιος. What Sinai
  promised a nation, grace gives you.

### Lessons about Jesus

- **Jesus is God's grace in person.** "The law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through
  Jesus Christ" (John 1:17, ESV). The God who proclaimed Himself "gracious" at Sinai (Exodus 34:6)
  came "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14, ESV).
- **Jesus became poor to make you rich.** "Though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so
  that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV).
- **Jesus has appeared in grace and will appear in glory.** "The grace of God has appeared"
  (Titus 2:11, ESV) is ἐπεφάνη, and the "blessed hope" you wait for is "the appearing of the glory of
  our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (Titus 2:13, ESV), ἐπιφάνεια. Titus 2 sets your life between
  the two.

### Memory verses

> ✝️ Ephesians 2:8-9 (ESV)
>
> 8 For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift
> of God, 9 not a result of works, so that no one may boast.

> ✝️ 1 Corinthians 15:10 (ESV)
>
> 10 But by the grace of God I am what I am, and his grace toward me was not in vain. On the
> contrary, I worked harder than any of them, though it was not I, but the grace of God that is with
> me.

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** God's favour rests on you because He chose to give it in His Son Jesus, when you had
  earned His wrath. You stand in grace (Romans 5:2), and your salvation is "the gift of God"
  (Ephesians 2:8).
- **Attitude.** Serve as someone who has received. Whatever you do for God, say with Paul, "not I,
  but the grace of God that is with me" (1 Corinthians 15:10, ESV).
- **Do.** Jesus said God "is kind to the ungrateful and the evil" (Luke 6:35, ESV). This week, do
  good to one person who cannot repay you, or who has wronged you.

## Favour in the eyes of the LORD

### The first time

The first time the Bible uses the Hebrew word for grace, the world is about to be judged. "The LORD
saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every intention of the thoughts of
his heart was only evil continually" (Genesis 6:5, ESV). Then comes one sentence:

> ✝️ Genesis 6:8 (ESV)
>
> 8 But Noah found favor in the eyes of the LORD.

"Favor" is <span dir="rtl">חֵן</span> (*chen*, KHEN, H2580, "favour, grace"). It occurs 69 times in
the Hebrew Bible, most often in the phrase "find favour in the eyes of" someone. Favour is found in
another person's eyes. It lives in the one who gives it.

### Moses on the mountain

The word's fullest scene comes after the golden calf. Israel had broken the covenant while Moses was
still on the mountain receiving it (Exodus 24:18; 32:1), and Moses went back up to plead for them
(Exodus 32:30-32). When he pleaded again, he built the plea on *chen*. "You have said, 'I know you
by name, and you have also found favor in my sight'" (Exodus 33:12, ESV). "If I have found favor in
your sight, please show me now your ways, that I may know you in order to find favor in your sight"
(Exodus 33:13, ESV). The word comes six times between Moses and the LORD (Exodus 33:12, 13, 16, 17;
34:9), and the last is a request for people who had earned the opposite: "it is a stiff-necked
people, and pardon our iniquity and our sin" (Exodus 34:9, ESV).

God answered with the verb, <span dir="rtl">חָנַן</span> (*chanan*, khah-NAHN, H2603, "show
favour"):

> ✝️ Exodus 33:19 (ESV)
>
> 19 And he said, "I will make all my goodness pass before you and will proclaim before you my name
> 'The LORD.' And I will be gracious to whom I will be gracious, and will show mercy on whom I will
> show mercy.

The Septuagint renders *chanan* here with ἐλεέω (*eleeō*, G1653, "have mercy"), and Paul quotes
that Greek in Romans 9:15, so the BSB reads "I will have mercy on whom I have mercy" (Exodus 33:19,
BSB).

Grace is God's to give. He gives it because He chooses to, and nothing in the one who receives it
obliges Him. Then He passed by and proclaimed His name: "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and
gracious" (Exodus 34:6, ESV). "Gracious" is <span dir="rtl">חַנּוּן</span> (*chanun*, khah-NOON,
H2587). Of its thirteen uses, twelve describe God; the thirteenth, Psalm 112:4, most likely
describes the man who fears Him. The first is in the law about a poor man's cloak taken in pledge:
"if he cries to me, I will hear, for I am compassionate" (Exodus 22:27, ESV), where "compassionate"
is *chanun*.

This shows that God is gracious by His own nature. He named Himself that way to a people who had
just sinned, and He hears the cry of the man with nothing left. [The Names of
God](../god/names-of-god.md) works through the whole proclamation of Exodus 34:6-7, and a study of
God's attributes will start from it ([backlog
11.2](../about/backlog.md#112-the-nature-of-god-his-attributes)).

## The word the apostles used

### From *chen* to *charis*

When Jewish scholars translated the Old Testament into Greek, they rendered every *chen* in Exodus
with χάρις (*charis*, KHAH-rees, G5485, "grace, favour"). Noah "found *charis*" in the Greek
(Genesis 6:8), and the angel used the same words to Mary: "you have found favor with God" (Luke
1:30, ESV). *Charis* occurs 155 times in the New Testament, and 101 of them are in Paul's letters (one fewer
in each for editions that omit Romans 16:24).

Luke's Gospel shows how wide the word was:

- **Favour.** Mary "found favor" (Luke 1:30), and Jesus grew "in favor with God and man" (Luke 2:52,
  ESV).
- **Graciousness.** In Nazareth they "marveled at the gracious words that were coming from his
  mouth" (Luke 4:22, ESV), literally "words of grace".
- **Thanks.** Of a master and his servant Jesus asks, "Does he thank the servant because he did what
  was commanded?" (Luke 17:9, ESV), where "thank" is "have *charis*". Paul's "thanks be to God" (1
  Corinthians 15:57, ESV) is *charis* too.

In the Greek world a benefactor's gift was *charis*, and the one who received it answered with
*charis*, thanks and honour. The *NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* notes that "recipients of
benefaction were to pay back the benefactors, though with honor, not money" (note on 2 Corinthians
8:14). The New Testament keeps the thanks and removes the repayment.

### Kind to the ungrateful

Jesus used the word to set God's kindness apart from ours:

> ✝️ Luke 6:32, 35 (ESV)
>
> 32 "If you love those who love you, what benefit is that to you? For even sinners love those who
> love them. ... 35 But love your enemies, and do good, and lend, expecting nothing in return, and
> your reward will be great, and you will be sons of the Most High, for he is kind to the ungrateful
> and the evil.

"Benefit" in Luke 6:32 is *charis*. Matthew's account of the same teaching asks "what reward do you
have?" (Matthew 5:46, ESV); Luke records the question with *charis*. Love that repays love is a fair
exchange. "The ungrateful" is ἀχαρίστους (*acharistous*, G884), the people with no *charis* to give
back. God is kind to them.

That is the shape of grace: favour given to people who have not earned it and cannot repay it.

### Grace and mercy

Mercy withholds the punishment you deserve; grace gives the favour you do not deserve, as the *NLT
Christian Basics Bible* puts the standard distinction. Ephesians 2 names both: "But God, being rich
in mercy... by grace you have been saved" (Ephesians 2:4-5, ESV).

## Grace and truth came through Jesus Christ

The Gospel of John uses *charis* only four times, all in its opening chapter:

> ✝️ John 1:14, 16-17 (ESV)
>
> 14 And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only
> Son from the Father, full of grace and truth. ... 16 For from his fullness we have all received,
> grace upon grace. 17 For the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus
> Christ.

John is very likely writing with Exodus 33-34 open. Moses asked, "Please show me your glory" (Exodus
33:18, ESV); John says, "we have seen his glory". God said, "man shall not see me and live" (Exodus
33:20, ESV); John says, "No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has
made him known" (John 1:18, ESV). "Dwelt" is ἐσκήνωσεν (*eskēnōsen*, G4637), "pitched His tent", the
word of the tabernacle. And "grace and truth" most likely translates the Hebrew of Exodus 34:6,
"abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness", <span dir="rtl">חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת</span> (*chesed
ve'emet*, H2617, H571). The ESV Study Bible and the NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible both read it
this way. The Septuagint of Exodus 34:6 uses other Greek words, so the link runs through the Hebrew
and the scene; it is probable, and it rests on those four shared features.

This shows that God's grace came in a person. The LORD who proclaimed Himself gracious on the
mountain came and lived among us as Jesus, and everything you receive of grace comes "from his
fullness". Paul says what it cost Him: "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so
that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV).

## By grace you have been saved

### Made alive together with Christ

In Ephesians 2:4-9 God does all the saving, and grace is Paul's word for it. Week one of this
series, [Why We Need Saving](../sin/why-we-need-saving.md), showed what we were: dead in trespasses,
"by nature children of wrath" (Ephesians 2:3, ESV), and still sinners when "Christ died for us"
(Romans 5:8, ESV). Paul goes on:

> ✝️ Ephesians 2:4-9 (ESV)
>
> 4 But God, being rich in mercy, because of the great love with which he loved us, 5 even when we
> were dead in our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ—by grace you have been saved—
> 6 and raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus, 7 so
> that in the coming ages he might show the immeasurable riches of his grace in kindness toward us in
> Christ Jesus. 8 For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it
> is the gift of God, 9 not a result of works, so that no one may boast.

*Charis* comes three times in these six verses (Ephesians 2:5, 7, 8). God is the subject of every
main verb: He made alive, raised and seated. The dead contribute nothing to being made alive. Paul
breaks into his own sentence to say it ("by grace you have been saved"), and then says it again in
Ephesians 2:8.

Ephesians 2:7 gives the reason God saves this way. He means to "show the immeasurable riches of his
grace" in the ages to come, and the people He saved are the display. Your salvation will be on show
for ever as evidence of what God is like.

The grace is received "through faith", which is next week's study, [Faith](faith.md). The grammar of
"this is not your own doing" is worked through in [Assurance of
Salvation](assurance-of-salvation.md#the-grammar-of-not-your-own-doing): the gift is the whole of
it, grace and faith and all.

### Freely, for no reason in you

Romans says the same in a courtroom word. All "are justified by his grace as a gift, through the
redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Romans 3:24, ESV). "As a gift" is δωρεάν (*dōrean*,
doh-reh-AHN, G1432), "freely, for nothing". Jesus used the same word of His enemies: "They hated me
without a cause" (John 15:25, ESV). They found no reason in Him for their hatred, and God finds no
reason in you for His favour. He justifies you *dōrean*.

Paul uses the word a third way: "if righteousness were through the law, then Christ died for no
purpose" (Galatians 2:21, ESV), *dōrean* again. If you could earn it, the cross was for nothing.
What it means to be justified is a week of its own in this series. For the "redemption" in Romans
3:24, [The Kinsman Redeemer](../jesus/kinsman-redeemer.md) follows the redeemer's price through
Ruth.

## Otherwise grace would no longer be grace

In Elijah's day God kept seven thousand who had not bowed to Baal (Romans 11:4), and Paul says the
same is true of Israel in his own day:

> ✝️ Romans 11:5-6 (ESV)
>
> 5 So too at the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace. 6 But if it is by grace, it is
> no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace.

Paul defines grace by what it excludes. Favour that is earned is a wage. Romans 4:4 makes the same
distinction in Greek that the ESV puts into English: "to the one who
works, his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due" (Romans 4:4, ESV). "As a gift" is κατὰ
χάριν (*kata charin*, G2596, G5485), "according to grace", and "as his due" is κατὰ ὀφείλημα (*kata
opheilēma*, G3783), "according to debt". A wage is owed. Grace is never owed.
[Faith](faith.md#wages-and-gifts) follows the next verse, where God "justifies the ungodly" (Romans
4:5, ESV).

Some Greek manuscripts, followed by the KJV, add the converse to Romans 11:6: "But if it be of
works, then is it no more grace: otherwise work is no more work" (Romans 11:6, KJV). [When Love
Grows Cold](../christian-life/when-love-grows-cold.md) treats Elijah's seven thousand and the remnant
in Romans 11:1-6.

## Where sin increased

### Much more

Where sin spread, grace spread further. Week one followed Romans 5:12-19 from Adam's side: one
man's trespass, and death reigning over all. Paul's "much more" falls on the other side, twice:

> ✝️ Romans 5:15-17 (ESV)
>
> 15 But the free gift is not like the trespass. For if many died through one man's trespass, much
> more have the grace of God and the free gift by the grace of that one man Jesus Christ abounded
> for many. 16 And the free gift is not like the result of that one man's sin. For the judgment
> following one trespass brought condemnation, but the free gift following many trespasses brought
> justification. 17 For if, because of one man's trespass, death reigned through that one man, much
> more will those who receive the abundance of grace and the free gift of righteousness reign in
> life through the one man Jesus Christ.

In Romans 5:15-17 Paul uses four Greek words of giving, eight times between them: *charis*, χάρισμα
(*charisma*, G5486), δωρεά (*dōrea*, G1431) and δώρημα (*dōrēma*, G1434). The ESV renders the last
three "free gift", five times in three verses. Paul piles them up because the gift is greater than
the trespass. Adam's one act brought death to many. Jesus' one act brings "the abundance of grace"
to everyone who receives it.

### Grace reigns

> ✝️ Romans 5:20-21 (ESV)
>
> 20 Now the law came in to increase the trespass, but where sin increased, grace abounded all the
> more, 21 so that, as sin reigned in death, grace also might reign through righteousness leading to
> eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.

"Abounded all the more" is ὑπερεπερίσσευσεν, from ὑπερπερισσεύω (*hyperperisseuō*,
hoo-per-per-is-SYOO-oh, G5248), to overflow past overflowing. Paul uses it only here and in
2 Corinthians 7:4. However far sin has run in a life, grace has run further. And grace is a king:
it "reigns", where sin used to reign.

Paul's next sentence raises the objection to grace that large: "Are we to continue in sin that grace
may abound? By no means! How can we who died to sin still live in it?" (Romans 6:1-2, ESV). "Sin
will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace" (Romans 6:14, ESV).
The grace that forgives you also takes the throne.

## The grace that trains

### Grace appeared

Paul gave Titus instructions for older men, older women, young women, young men and slaves on Crete
(Titus 2:1-10). Then he gave the reason, and it is grace:

> ✝️ Titus 2:11-14 (ESV)
>
> 11 For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people, 12 training us to
> renounce ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives
> in the present age, 13 waiting for our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God
> and Savior Jesus Christ, 14 who gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify
> for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works.

"Has appeared" is ἐπεφάνη, from ἐπιφαίνω (*epiphainō*, ep-ee-FIE-noh, G2014). Paul uses it again in
Titus 3:4, "when the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior appeared" (Titus 3:4, ESV), and
its noun ἐπιφάνεια (*epiphaneia*, G2015) for "the appearing" in Titus 2:13. Grace appeared when
Jesus came. Glory will appear when He comes again. You live between the two appearings.

### Grace raises children

"Training" is παιδεύουσα, from παιδεύω (*paideuō*, pie-DYOO-oh, G3811), the verb of bringing up a
child. Stephen uses it of Moses, who "was instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians" (Acts 7:22,
ESV), and Hebrews of the Father: "the Lord disciplines the one he loves, and chastises every son
whom he receives" (Hebrews 12:6, ESV). The LSB footnotes Titus 2:12 "Or disciplining". The grace
that saved you sets about raising you. It teaches you to say no ("renounce") and to live. A
traditional reading takes the three words as self-controlled toward yourself, upright toward others,
godly toward God.

### A people for God's own possession

Titus 2:14 takes Israel's words. "Redeem us from all lawlessness" echoes the Septuagint of Psalm
130:8, "he will redeem Israel from all his iniquities" (Psalm 130:8, ESV), and "a people for his own
possession" is the phrase of Exodus 19:5 and Deuteronomy 14:2, λαὸς περιούσιος (*laos periousios*,
G2992, G4041). The God who took Israel as His treasured possession at Sinai takes for Himself, in
Jesus, a people "zealous for good works". Those good works are what Ephesians 2:10 says He "prepared
beforehand, that we should walk in them" (Ephesians 2:10, ESV). [Israel and the
Church](../israel-and-church/israel-and-the-church.md) shows why the borrowed words leave Israel's
own promises standing.

### "For all people": a contested phrase

How far "bringing salvation for all people" reaches is read two ways, and each has reasons in the
text.

- **Every person.** The words are πᾶσιν ἀνθρώποις (*pasin anthrōpois*, G3956, G444), "all people",
  with no limit stated. The ESV Study Bible reads it as salvation "offered to all people (including
  all ethnic groups)". Paul says the same of God elsewhere: He "desires all people to be saved" (1
  Timothy 2:4, ESV).
- **Every kind of person.** The verse follows a list of every kind of person in a Cretan household,
  slaves included (Titus 2:1-10), and its "For" gives the reason that list applies to all of them.
  On this reading grace has reached old and young, men and women, free and slave.

The two readings agree on what the paragraph needs: no class, age, people or past puts anyone
outside the reach of the grace that has appeared. The [statement of
faith](../about/statement-of-faith.md#salvation) does not take a side on the question.

## The grace that works

### Not in vain

Paul ends his list of the people who saw the risen Jesus with himself:

> ✝️ 1 Corinthians 15:8-10 (ESV)
>
> 8 Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me. 9 For I am the least of the
> apostles, unworthy to be called an apostle, because I persecuted the church of God. 10 But by the
> grace of God I am what I am, and his grace toward me was not in vain. On the contrary, I worked
> harder than any of them, though it was not I, but the grace of God that is with me.

*Charis* comes three times in 1 Corinthians 15:10. Grace made Paul an apostle ("I am what I am"),
grace was given to him, and grace worked with him. "In vain" is κενή (*kenē*, from κενός, *kenos*,
G2756), empty. Paul uses the word three more times in the chapter: if Christ is not raised "our
preaching is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14, ESV), and because He is, "in the Lord your labor is not
in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:58, ESV). Grace is never empty. It produced the hardest worker among the
apostles, and Paul gives it the credit for the work.

### Given for others

Paul called his ministry "the stewardship of God's grace that was given to me for you" (Ephesians
3:2, ESV). "Stewardship" is οἰκονομία (*oikonomia*, G3622), which the KJV renders "the dispensation
of the grace of God". [Israel and the
Church](../israel-and-church/israel-and-the-church.md#what-scripture-means-by-a-mystery) works
through what Ephesians 3 says was entrusted to him. Paul said how he received it: "To me, though I
am the very least of all the saints, this grace was given, to preach to the Gentiles the
unsearchable riches of Christ" (Ephesians 3:8, ESV). Grace given to Paul was meant for others.

### Power made perfect in weakness

When Paul begged three times for his thorn to be taken away, the Lord answered with grace: "My grace
is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9, ESV). Grace is
God's favour, and it is also God's power at work in a weak servant.

This shows that God does not stop giving once He has saved you. The grace that stopped Saul on the
Damascus road (Acts 9:3-6) made him a servant and kept him through weakness. It will do the same in
you, so that whatever you do for Him, you can say with Paul, "not I, but the grace of God that is
with me" (1 Corinthians 15:10, ESV).

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** In Greek one word, χάρις (*charis*), means both God's favour and our thanks
   (Luke 17:9; 1 Corinthians 15:57). What does it say about the Christian life that the gift and
   the response share a name?
2. **The text in its context.** Paul grounds his instructions to old and young, men and women and
   slaves on "For the grace of God has appeared" (Titus 2:11). How does grace that "trains" (Titus
   2:12) change the way those instructions are heard?
3. **Christ.** John says "grace and truth came through Jesus Christ" (John 1:17), and the LORD
   proclaimed Himself "gracious" to Moses (Exodus 34:6). What do you learn about Jesus by reading
   John 1:14-18 beside Exodus 33:18-34:7?
4. **The hard part.** Paul says "where sin increased, grace abounded all the more" (Romans 5:20),
   and then "Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound? By no means!" (Romans 6:1-2). How do
   you hold out grace that large without making sin seem small?
5. **Be transformed.** Paul said of his work, "it was not I, but the grace of God that is with me"
   (1 Corinthians 15:10), and Jesus said God "is kind to the ungrateful and the evil" (Luke 6:35).
   Who is the ungrateful person grace could reach through you this week, and what would it cost you?
