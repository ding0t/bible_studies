# Assurance of Salvation

A believer who has just sinned badly, or who has simply had a bad week of doubt, tends to ask one
of two questions: "Am I still saved?" or "Was I ever?" Scripture treats that question as answerable.
John writes to a congregation that had just watched some of its own members walk out the door
(1 John 2:19), and tells the ones who stayed: "I write these things to you who believe in the name
of the Son of God, that you may know that you have eternal life" (1 John 5:13, ESV). Not "hope,"
not "feel confident about" — *know*. This study asks what that knowledge rests on, taking two other
texts as its frame: there is no other name by which anyone is saved (Acts 4:12), and salvation is
not earned by works (Ephesians 2:8-9).

**Assurance of salvation rests on what Christ already did, not on how the week has gone.** Scripture
grounds it in an objective fact — Jesus is the only name under heaven by which anyone is saved, and
that salvation was received by grace through faith, itself God's gift rather than human achievement
— then confirms it subjectively through the Spirit's own witness and a life of growing obedience.
Confirmation is not the foundation, and confusing the two is where assurance quietly turns into
either presumption or despair.

## Key Takeaways

### Lessons about Jesus

- Salvation exists in no one else and under no other name (Acts 4:12) — the claim Peter made to
  the same council that had condemned him to death weeks earlier, backed by a healing performed in
  that name the day before (Acts 3:1-10; 4:9-10).
- His own name is built on the claim: *Iēsous* renders the Hebrew *Yehoshua*/*Yeshua*, "YHWH is
  salvation," and Matthew makes the pun explicit — "you shall call his name Jesus, for he will save
  (*sōsei*) his people from their sins" (Matthew 1:21, ESV).
- He holds his own twice over — "I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one
  will snatch them out of my hand," followed at once by "no one is able to snatch them out of the
  Father's hand" (John 10:28-29, ESV) — the same forcible-seizure verb, denied to any hostile hand,
  stated of both Son and Father.
- He is not finished acting on a believer's behalf: he "always lives to make intercession" for those
  who come to God through him (Hebrews 7:25, ESV) and is "able to guard until that day what has been
  entrusted to" him (2 Timothy 1:12, ESV).
- He began the work and is named as the one who will finish it — "he who began a good work in you
  will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6, ESV).

### Be Transformed

- **Think:** locate where your own sense of standing with God comes from day to day — the
  finished work named in these verses, or your most recent performance. Those produce very different
  answers on a bad day.
- **Attitude:** let 2 Peter 1:5-11's ordering stand: growth in godliness is what *confirms* a calling
  already given (v. 10), not what *earns* one. Guilt over slow growth is not the same problem as
  doubt about whether you were ever saved, and this text answers only the second.
- **Do:** if 1 John 2:19 describes a departure your own church or family has felt, name the
  specific fear that departure raised in you, and bring it to the text this study covers — not to a
  general feeling of reassurance, but to the actual testimony God gave "concerning his Son"
  (1 John 5:9-10, ESV).

## "No other name," "not a result of works"

Acts 4:12 and Ephesians 2:8-9 are not two halves of one paragraph — they come from different
authors, different situations, years apart — but together they answer the two questions doubt
asks. *Am I saved by the right thing?* Only one name is under heaven by which anyone can be
saved (Acts 4:12). *Did I do enough of it?* No — grace, received through faith, is not your own
doing to begin with (Ephesians 2:8-9). Assurance built on anything narrower than both of these
collapses the moment either question is pressed.

**Acts 4:12.** Peter says this to the Sanhedrin the morning after healing a man lame from birth at
the temple gate (Acts 3:1-10), the same council that had condemned Jesus weeks earlier, at the
preceding Passover (Acts 4:1-6). Asked "by what power or by what name did you do this?" (4:7, ESV), Peter answers with
a pun the Greek makes plain: the lame man "has been healed" (*sesōstai*, a form of *sōzō*, 4:9) by
the name of Jesus, and "there is salvation (*sōtēria*) in no one else... by which we must be saved
(*sōthēnai*, again *sōzō*)" (4:12, ESV). One verb covers both the physical healing everyone in the
room had just seen and the spiritual salvation Peter is now claiming — the miracle functions as the
sermon's own exhibit. "Name" here carries the weight it does throughout Scripture: not a label but
the person and authority behind it — a way of speaking native to the ancient Near East, not to
modern English. What carries forward past the idiom is the claim underneath it, not the idiom
itself: whichever person the name actually points to is who salvation runs through, whatever word a
given language uses to say so. The same idiom sits behind "everyone who calls on the name of the
Lord will be saved" (Romans 10:13, ESV, quoting Joel 2:32) — a text originally naming YHWH, which
Paul applies to Jesus four verses earlier by way of "if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is
Lord" (Romans 10:9, ESV).

**Ephesians 2:8-9.** Unlike most of Paul's letters, Ephesians answers no specific crisis; it is
general instruction, probably meant to circulate among several churches in the Roman province of
Asia. Ephesus's own reputation for magic and the occult (Acts 19:19) sits behind the letter's
recurring emphasis on Christ's authority over every power, and 2:1-10 opens by describing the
readers' former state under exactly such powers — "dead in the trespasses and sins," walking
"following the prince of the power of the air" (2:1-2, ESV) — before "But God" (2:4, ESV) reverses
it. Verses 8-9 restate 2:5's claim ("by grace you have been saved") for emphasis, inside what is, in
the Greek, one long sentence running from 2:1 to 2:10. The verb *sesōsmenoi* ("have been saved," v.
8) is a perfect passive participle: grammatically, a completed action with a result that stands —
the tense itself states the doctrine before the argument for it is even made. "This is not your own
doing" (Greek *touto*, neuter) cannot grammatically be pointing at "faith" alone, since *pistis*
("faith") is feminine; the MACULA syntax annotation for this verse resolves *touto*'s referent back
to the whole preceding clause, "you have been saved." Paul's neuter pronoun sweeps up the entire
transaction — grace, faith, and all — as God's gift, not a partial one. And the very next verse
refuses to let that be misread as license: "we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good
works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them" (2:10, ESV). Not saved *by* works;
created *for* them.

## "That you may know" — 1 John 5:11-13 in its letter

First John is traditionally attributed to John the son of Zebedee, writing from Ephesus in the
decades after Jerusalem's fall in AD 70. It is often read as a response to an early Gnosticizing
threat, but that movement's documented heyday runs a century or more later than this letter, so the
one thing the text itself states plainly is safer ground than the label: "they went out from us, but
they were not of us" (1 John 2:19, ESV) — a schism, whatever its exact theology, that had shaken the
confidence of the members who stayed. The letter is not linear; the ESV Study Bible's introduction
advises the reader to "think paragraphs," since John circles back to the same handful of tests —
right belief about who Jesus is, obedience, and love for other believers — throughout, rather than
arguing them once in sequence.

Chapter 5 is where "believing" concentrates: six of the letter's nine uses of the verb fall here.
Verses 6-10 build a legal case for who Jesus is, using the two-or-three-witness standard of
Deuteronomy 17:6 and 19:15 — "the Spirit and the water and the blood; and these three agree"
(5:8, ESV), John's own gloss on Jesus' baptism, atoning death, and the Spirit's ongoing inward
testimony. Verses 11-12 state the content of that testimony as plainly as anything in the letter:
"God gave us eternal life, and this life is in his Son. Whoever has the Son has life; whoever does
not have the Son of God does not have life" (ESV). Verse 13 is John stepping back to say why he
wrote all of it. "That you may know" (Greek *hina eidēte*) uses the verb *oida* — one of two Greek
verbs 1 John renders "know," alongside *ginōskō*, which appears even more often across the letter
(25 occurrences against 15 for *oida*). Older commentary sometimes presses a hard distinction
between the two — *oida* for fixed, settled knowledge, *ginōskō* for the experiential kind — but the
letter's own usage does not support drawing that line sharply here: both verbs cover the same ground
throughout 1 John, and treating them as rigidly separate is a well-documented case of overreading a
lexical distinction past what the evidence supports. The real contrast sits with John's other book:
the Gospel states its purpose as being written "so that you may *believe*
that Jesus is the Christ" (John 20:31, ESV) — an evangelistic aim, addressed to those not yet
convinced. The letter's purpose in 5:13 is addressed to "you who believe" already, so that they may
*know* what they have. Same author, same vocabulary of life and belief, two different audiences and
two different goals.

*A translation note.* Some English versions (WEB among them) add a further clause to 1 John 5:13 —
"and that you may continue to believe in the name of the Son of God" — not present in the Greek text
behind the ESV. The SBLGNT critical text ends the verse at "the Son of God" with no second clause;
the addition reflects a longer, later manuscript tradition. It does not change the verse's basic
claim either way, but a reader comparing WEB against ESV here should not mistake the difference for
an error in either.

## Objective ground, subjective confirmation

Put together, these texts describe assurance running on two tracks that are not interchangeable.

**The objective ground is what Christ did**, stated as a finished fact rather than an ongoing
negotiation: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1,
ESV); "Whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life. He does not come into
judgment, but has passed from death to life" (John 5:24, ESV, present-tense possession, not a future
hope only); "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17, ESV). Nothing in this
track depends on how the week has gone.

**The subjective confirmation is what the Spirit and a changed life supply**, and Scripture is
careful to keep these in the right order. "The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we
are children of God" (Romans 8:16, ESV) — Paul's readers cry "Abba! Father!" (8:15, ESV), the same
Aramaic word, left untranslated in the Greek text, that Jesus himself used addressing God in
Gethsemane (Mark 14:36). The Spirit given at belief is described in commercial, legal language as a
down payment: "sealed with the promised Holy Spirit, who is the guarantee (Greek *arrabōn*, a term
for a first installment that legally secures the rest of a payment) of our inheritance" (Ephesians
1:13-14, ESV). And growth in godliness — self-control, steadfastness, brotherly affection, love,
the list Peter gives in 2 Peter 1:5-7 — is what he tells his readers to be "all the more diligent"
to pursue, "to confirm your calling and election" (2 Peter 1:10, ESV). The ESV Study Bible's note on
that verse states the ordering precisely: "Good works are evidence of and give assurance of
salvation, though they are never the basis for it." Reverse that order — treat the fruit as the ground rather than the confirmation — and
assurance becomes exactly the performance-based anxiety these texts were written to answer.

That leaves one honest pastoral question: what about someone who once seemed to believe and then
walked away, the way John's own readers had just watched happen (1 John 2:19)? The texts gathered
here answer with security for those who are Christ's, not a guarantee that every profession was
genuine. "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and
they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:27-28, ESV) — stated of
those who are actually his, and backed a second time by the Father (10:29). Paul's list in Romans
8:38-39 is exhaustive on purpose — "neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present
nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able
to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (ESV) — and does not include the
believer's own future failures, because Paul has already spent Romans 8:1-4 establishing that no
condemnation remains for them. 1 John 2:19's departure is not, on this text's own terms, evidence
that grace can run out; it is evidence that not every visible profession was the real thing to begin
with — a hard word, but a different hard word than "you can lose what you were actually given."

## Discussion questions

1. Where does your own confidence that you are saved actually come from — Christ's finished work, or
   how recently you have sinned? What would change if you answered honestly?
2. Acts 4:12 and Ephesians 2:8-9 answer two different doubts. Which one do you actually struggle
   with — whether Jesus alone is sufficient, or whether grace alone is sufficient?
3. 2 Peter 1:5-10 tells believers to pursue godliness (vv. 5-7) so as to "confirm" their calling
   (v. 10). How is that different from pursuing godliness to *earn* one? Where has that line gotten
   blurred for you?
4. 1 John was written to people who had just watched fellow believers walk away (2:19). What does
   this study's distinction — assurance for those who are Christ's, not a guarantee that every past
   profession was genuine — say to someone in exactly that situation now?
5. Read Romans 8:38-39's list slowly. What specific thing were you privately afraid might be the
   exception?
