# Firstfruits: The Sheaf Waved on the Third Day

Of the seven appointed times in Leviticus 23, Firstfruits is the one with no story attached. Passover
has the exodus; Tabernacles has the wilderness. Firstfruits has a farmer, a handful of barley, and a
prohibition: until this sheaf is waved, nobody eats.

That prohibition is the point. The sheaf is not a sample sent ahead of the harvest — it is the act
that releases it. Which is why, when Paul reaches for one word to say what the resurrection did, he
reaches for this feast's word.

## Key Takeaways

### Types & Prophecy

**Type.** The sheaf is the pattern, and the New Testament names it rather than leaving it to be
spotted. The Septuagint calls the offering of Leviticus 23:10 an **ἀπαρχή** (*aparchē*,
"firstfruits"), and that is the exact noun Paul applies to Christ twice in four verses:
"Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep"
(1 Corinthians 15:20). The type carries three things the statute makes explicit — the offering is
*first*, it is *the same substance as the rest of the crop*, and until it is presented **the harvest
may not be touched** (Leviticus 23:14).

**A second thing the statute fixes: the day.** Leviticus 23:11 dates the waving "on the day after
the Sabbath," which is the one appointed time in the chapter located by a day of the week rather
than a day of the month. What that phrase means was disputed long before the crucifixion — and in
the crucifixion year, uniquely, both readings land on the same Sunday.

### Lessons about Jesus

- He is called the firstfruits by the same word the Greek Old Testament uses for the sheaf — a
  named type, not an inferred resemblance.
- His resurrection is presented as the legal release of a harvest, not a solitary wonder:
  "Christ the firstfruits, then at his coming those who belong to Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:23).
- He said the same thing about himself in the same agricultural terms: "unless a grain of wheat
  falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24).

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** The sheaf was waved before the farmer knew what the harvest would come to. The offering
  was an act of confidence in a crop still standing in the field, and the order matters: the
  firstfruits go up first, not once the yield is safely counted.
- **Attitude.** Leviticus 23:14 makes the whole harvest unavailable until the first of it is given.
  Consider what you treat as yours to spend before God has had any of it — time and attention as
  readily as money.
- **Do.** Name one thing you are currently giving God out of what is left over. Move it to the front
  of the sequence this week rather than the end.

## What the statute actually commands

> ✝️ Leviticus 23:9-11 (ESV)
>
> 9 And the LORD spoke to Moses, saying, 10 "Speak to the people of Israel and say to them, When you
> come into the land that I give you and reap its harvest, you shall bring the sheaf of the
> firstfruits of your harvest to the priest, 11 and he shall wave the sheaf before the LORD, so that
> you may be accepted. On the day after the Sabbath the priest shall wave it.

Four things bear on any typology built from it.

**It is one sheaf, not a tithe.** The Hebrew is <span dir="rtl">עֹמֶר</span> (*omer*, H6016) — a
bundle of stalks small enough to hold in one hand, as the *NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible* glosses
it at this verse. The quantity is nominal. What is being offered is not a portion of the harvest's
value but its *precedence*.

**It is barley.** The *CSB Ancient Faith Study Bible* footnotes verse 10 simply "= the barley
harvest," and the Cultural Backgrounds volumes make the agricultural point: barley ripens first in
the spring, wheat some weeks later, which is why the grain offering of the later feast at
Leviticus 23:16 is a different crop. Ruth 1:22 marks the same seasonal order. So Firstfruits sits at
the front edge of the harvest year, not at its climax.

**The verb is "wave," and it describes a gesture.** <span dir="rtl">נוּף</span> (*nuph*, H5130)
occurs twice in verse 11 alone. The *NKJV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* describes the
*tenuphah* at Leviticus 7:30 as an offering "lifted up toward the heavens in presentation and
dedication to God and then lowered into the hands of the priest." Presented upward, received back
down — which is a fair description of what happens to the offering this feast turned out to prefigure.

**And nothing may be eaten until it is done.** This is the clause that gives the feast its force, and
it is easy to read past:

> ✝️ Leviticus 23:14 (ESV)
>
> 14 And you shall eat neither bread nor grain parched or fresh until this same day, until you have
> brought the offering of your God: it is a statute forever throughout your generations in all your
> dwellings.

A standing, ripe field was legally untouchable until one sheaf of it had been lifted before the LORD.
The firstfruits offering is not a thank-you for a harvest already enjoyed. It is the condition on
which the harvest becomes available at all.

## The first one ever kept

The statute begins "when you come into the land," so it could not be obeyed in the wilderness. Joshua
records the first occasion it could be — and the narrative marks it:

> ✝️ Joshua 5:10-12 (ESV)
>
> 10 While the people of Israel were encamped at Gilgal, they kept the Passover on the fourteenth
> day of the month in the evening on the plains of Jericho. 11 And the day after the Passover, on
> that very day, they ate of the produce of the land, unleavened cakes and parched grain. 12 And the
> manna ceased the day after they ate of the produce of the land. And there was no longer manna for
> the people of Israel, but they ate of the fruit of the land of Canaan that year.

"Parched grain" is the same commodity Leviticus 23:14 had forbidden until the sheaf was waved, and
the Cultural Backgrounds volumes note that Passover falls at the barley harvest, which is what makes
the sheaf available at all. The sequence in Joshua is exact: Passover, then the produce of the land,
then the manna stops.

**What ends here is the food that came down from heaven; what begins is the food that comes up out
of the ground.** The wilderness provision was daily, external and temporary. The land's harvest is
seasonal, cultivated, and permanent — and it opens with a sheaf lifted before God. Whatever else
Firstfruits means, its debut in the story is the day Israel stopped being fed and started being
harvested.

## "On the day after the Sabbath" — the disputed date

This phrase decides whether Firstfruits is always a Sunday or always Nisan 16, and it has been
argued over for well over two thousand years. The honest answer is that the text does not settle it.

**There is no textual variant.** The Hebrew at Leviticus 23:11 is
<span dir="rtl">מִמָּחֳרַת הַשַּׁבָּת</span> (*mimmochorath hashabbat*), "from the morrow of the
sabbath," and the identical phrase recurs at 23:15. The Samaritan Pentateuch reads with the Masoretic
Text at both verses, and the Qumran Leviticus scroll **4Q24** attests verse 11 including this phrase.
Anyone claiming the dispute rests on a corrupted text is mistaken; it is an interpretive fork, and
both branches are ancient.

| Reading | Takes *hashabbat* as | Firstfruits falls on | Held by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Festival** | Nisan 15, the rest day that opens Unleavened Bread (Leviticus 23:7) | Nisan 16, any weekday | Pharisees, and later rabbinic Judaism |
| **Weekly** | the ordinary seventh-day Sabbath | always a Sunday | Sadducees, Samaritans, and the Qumran calendar |

**The Septuagint witnesses against itself, and that is the most telling piece of evidence.** At
Leviticus 23:11 the Greek reads *τῇ ἐπαύριον τῆς πρώτης* — "on the morrow of **the first**," which
is the festival reading, written into the translation. But five verses later, rendering the *same
Hebrew phrase*, it reads *ἀπὸ τῆς ἐπαύριον τῶν σαββάτων* — "from the morrow of **the sabbaths**."

One phrase, two renderings, in one book, by translators working centuries before the Christian era.
The ambiguity is not a Christian invention or a rabbinic one; the tradition that produced the Greek
Old Testament had already failed to resolve it. The *NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible* reaches the
same place from the Hebrew alone, calling the phrase open between two readings and adding that "the
ambiguity may be intentional to allow for variations in the ripening of the crops from year to year."

This study does not adjudicate it. The Sunday reading is the one a Christian typology would prefer,
which makes it exactly the reading to be careful about accepting on convenience — and, as the next
section shows, it is not needed.

## Why the dispute did not matter in AD 33

Here is the observation that makes the typology work without anyone having to win the argument above.

On the reconstruction [Chronology Anchors](../last-things/chronology-anchors.md#settling-the-crucifixion-year)
reaches, Nisan 14 in the year of the crucifixion was **Friday 3 April AD 33**. Lay the two readings
side by side on that week:

| Hebrew date | Day | What it was |
|---|---|---|
| Nisan 14 | **Friday** | Passover / the Preparation — the crucifixion |
| Nisan 15 | **Saturday** | the first day of Unleavened Bread, a rest day — *and* the weekly Sabbath |
| Nisan 16 | **Sunday** | the empty tomb |

Nisan 15 fell on the weekly Sabbath that year. So the two readings collapse into one:

- On the **festival** reading, the sheaf is waved the day after Nisan 15 — Sunday.
- On the **weekly** reading, the sheaf is waved the day after the seventh-day Sabbath — and that
  Sabbath *is* Nisan 15, so again Sunday.

**Both reckonings put the wave offering on the morning the tomb was found empty.** In most years the
two part company and the calendar has to choose. In that year it did not have to, because the
festival rest day and the weekly Sabbath were the same day. It also explains, in passing, why John
calls that Sabbath "a high day" (John 19:31) — two observances had fallen together on it.

That is the load-bearing point of this study, and its limits should be stated with it. It does not
prove the crucifixion was in AD 33; that argument runs on Luke 3:1 and the Passovers John records,
and is made elsewhere. What it shows is that *given* that reconstruction, the
resurrection lands on Firstfruits without the study needing to settle a dispute the sources never
settled. The typology does not depend on picking a side.

(A caution on the other side of the ledger: the *ESV Study Bible*'s festal chart dates Firstfruits to
"16th of first month" without flagging the dispute at all — it simply adopts the festival reading. It
is a reminder that a chart is a set of decisions with the reasoning removed.)

## Christ the firstfruits

> ✝️ 1 Corinthians 15:20-23 (ESV)
>
> 20 But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen
> asleep. 21 For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead. 22 For
> as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive. 23 But each in his own order: Christ
> the firstfruits, then at his coming those who belong to Christ.

**The link is verbal, not merely conceptual.** The Septuagint's Leviticus 23:10 offers
*δράγμα ἀπαρχὴν τοῦ θερισμοῦ ὑμῶν* — "a sheaf, the firstfruits of your harvest." Paul's word at
1 Corinthians 15:20 and 23 is that same **ἀπαρχή**. He is not comparing the resurrection to a harvest
in general terms; he is using the technical noun of the offering, to readers whose Greek Bible used
it of this feast.

**The commentaries give it as a foretaste; the statute gives it as a release.** The *ESV Study Bible*
glosses ἀπαρχή as "a first sample of an agricultural crop that indicates the nature and quality of the
rest," and the Cultural Backgrounds volumes as the guarantee that "once the harvest had begun, the
rest of the harvest was certain." Both are true and both are less than Leviticus 23:14 says. The
sheaf does not merely *indicate* the harvest — until it is waved, the harvest cannot be eaten. Read
back into Paul, "Christ the firstfruits" says more than that his resurrection body previews ours. It
says the rest of the harvest was legally in the field until he was lifted up, and is now available.

**"Each in his own order" is the sequence the feast already taught.** ἀπαρχή makes no sense as a
solitary category — a first requires a second. Paul's argument through the chapter is that denying
the general resurrection while affirming Christ's is incoherent, and the feast is why: nobody waves a
sheaf over a field they do not intend to reap.

## What the firstfruits guarantees

Paul uses ἀπαρχή nine times in the New Testament, and the other occurrences fill out what the
guarantee covers.

- **Our own bodies.** "We ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we
  wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23). The Spirit is
  himself a firstfruits — a first instalment of the same substance as what is coming.
- **A people.** "Of his own will he brought us forth by the word of truth, that we should be a kind
  of firstfruits of his creatures" (James 1:18). The harvest metaphor turns and the church stands in
  the sheaf's place.
- **Israel.** "If the dough offered as firstfruits is holy, so is the whole lump, and if the root is
  holy, so are the branches" (Romans 11:16) — the same logic of a consecrated first guaranteeing the
  whole.

The pattern holds across all of them: a first that is genuinely part of what follows, offered to God,
and therefore securing the rest. It is why the New Testament can call Christ "the firstborn from the
dead" (Colossians 1:18; Revelation 1:5) in the same breath as it calls him the firstfruits — both
titles say *first of more*.

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** The Septuagint calls the sheaf of Leviticus 23:10 an **ἀπαρχή**, and Paul uses
   that exact noun of Christ. What does reaching for the offering's technical term claim that a
   general harvest metaphor would not?
2. **The text in its context.** Leviticus 23:14 made a ripe field untouchable until one sheaf had
   been waved. What does that add to "Christ the firstfruits" that "a foretaste of the harvest"
   leaves out?
3. **Deeper in Scripture.** In Joshua 5 the manna stops the day after Israel eats the land's
   produce. What changes about the way God provides, and why might that be marked with a feast?
4. **The hard part.** The Septuagint translators rendered the same Hebrew phrase two different ways
   five verses apart, and the question was never settled afterward. How should a reader hold a text
   whose own earliest translators disagreed about what it meant?
5. **Prophecy, and Christ.** Paul says the resurrection of Christ and the resurrection of believers
   stand or fall together. Why might someone want to affirm the first while quietly doubting the
   second?
6. **Be transformed.** Firstfruits was offered before the harvest was counted. Where does that
   order — first to God, then the remainder — actually cost something in your week?
