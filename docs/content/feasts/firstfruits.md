# Firstfruits: The Sheaf Waved on the Third Day

![A barley sheaf on a full gold moon: Firstfruits, fulfilled at His first coming](../assets/img/feasts/firstfruits.svg){ align=right width="96" }
Firstfruits is the third of the seven [appointed times](feasts.md) of Leviticus 23. Passover is "a
memorial day" for the exodus (Exodus 12:14), and Booths recalls the wilderness (Leviticus 23:43).
Firstfruits is given no memory to keep. It has a farmer, a handful of barley, and a prohibition:
until this sheaf is waved, nobody eats.

That prohibition is the point. The sheaf releases the harvest. And when Paul reaches for one word to
say what the resurrection of Jesus did, he reaches for this feast's word.

**In one sentence:** God made Israel's whole harvest wait on one sheaf lifted before Him "on the day
after the Sabbath," and Paul names the risen Jesus by that offering's own word, ἀπαρχή, so His
resurrection is the first sheaf of a harvest that includes you.

## Key Takeaways

*(This section follows the [Key Takeaways](../about/key-takeaways.md) format — see that page for what

### Types & Prophecy

- **Type.** The sheaf is the pattern, and the New Testament names it. The Septuagint calls the
  offering of Leviticus 23:10 an **ἀπαρχή** (*aparchē*, G536, "firstfruits"), and Paul applies that
  noun to Christ twice in four verses: "Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of
  those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20, ESV). The statute makes three things explicit.
  The offering is *first*, it is *the same crop as the rest*, and until it is presented **the
  harvest may not be touched** (Leviticus 23:14).
- **The day.** Leviticus 23:11 dates the waving "on the day after the Sabbath." Firstfruits and
  Weeks are the only feasts in the chapter given no day of the month, and what "the Sabbath" means
  here was disputed before the time of Jesus. On this site's reconstruction of AD 33, the two main
  readings land on the same Sunday, the day the tomb was found empty.

### Lessons about Jesus

- **He is the sheaf, by name.** Paul calls Him ἀπαρχή, the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the
  sheaf at Leviticus 23:10.
- **His resurrection opens a harvest.** "Christ the firstfruits, then at his coming those who belong
  to Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:23, ESV). God raised Jesus as the first of a resurrection He means to
  complete in everyone who belongs to Him.
- **He used the same picture of Himself.** "Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it
  remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24, ESV).

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** Your resurrection is as certain as His. A sheaf was waved over a field that was going to
  be reaped, and God raised Jesus as the first of "those who belong to Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:23,
  ESV).
- **Attitude.** Leviticus 23:14 kept the whole harvest unopened until the first of it was given.
  Consider what you treat as yours to spend before God has had any of it: time and attention as
  readily as money.
- **Do.** Name one thing you now give God out of what is left over, and move it to the front of your
  week.

## What the statute actually commands

> ✝️ Leviticus 23:9-11 (ESV)
>
> 9 And the LORD spoke to Moses, saying, 10 "Speak to the people of Israel and say to them, When you
> come into the land that I give you and reap its harvest, you shall bring the sheaf of the
> firstfruits of your harvest to the priest, 11 and he shall wave the sheaf before the LORD, so that
> you may be accepted. On the day after the Sabbath the priest shall wave it.

Four things bear on any typology built from it.

**It is one sheaf.** The Hebrew is <span dir="rtl">עֹמֶר</span> (*omer*, H6016), "a bundle of
stalks of grain that could be held in one's hand," as the *NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible* glosses
it at verse 11. The quantity is nominal. What is offered is the harvest's *precedence*: the first of
it, before any is used.

**It is barley.** The *CSB Ancient Faith Study Bible* footnotes verse 10 simply "= the barley
harvest," and the *NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible* makes the agricultural point: barley ripens
first in the spring and wheat some weeks later, which is why the grain offering of the later feast at
Leviticus 23:16 is a different crop. Ruth 1:22 and 2:23 mark the same order, from "the beginning of
barley harvest" to "the end of the barley and wheat harvests." So Firstfruits sits at the front edge
of the harvest year.

**The name "firstfruits" covers two feasts.** This barley sheaf is one. But Numbers 28:26 calls the
Feast of **Weeks** — Pentecost, fifty days later — "the day of the firstfruits, when you offer a grain
offering of new grain to the LORD," and Exodus 34:22 calls that same feast "the firstfruits of wheat
harvest." Two crops, two dates, one name, and a reader who looks the term up in a concordance will
find both. This study is about the first of them, the barley sheaf of Leviticus 23:9-14, which is the
one Paul reaches for at 1 Corinthians 15:20.

**The verb is "wave," and it describes a gesture.** <span dir="rtl">נוּף</span> (*nuph*, H5130)
occurs twice in verse 11 alone. The *NKJV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* describes the
*tenuphah* at Leviticus 7:30 as an offering "lifted up toward the heavens in presentation and
dedication to God and then lowered into the hands of the priest."

**Nothing may be eaten until it is done.**

> ✝️ Leviticus 23:14 (ESV)
>
> 14 And you shall eat neither bread nor grain parched or fresh until this same day, until you have
> brought the offering of your God: it is a statute forever throughout your generations in all your
> dwellings.

A standing, ripe field was legally untouchable until one sheaf of it had been lifted before the LORD.
The offering is the condition on which the harvest becomes available at all. God placed Himself at
the front of Israel's harvest. The land was His gift, "the land that I give you" (Leviticus 23:10,
ESV), and the first of its yield went back to Him before His people ate.

## The first one ever kept

The statute begins "when you come into the land," so it could not be obeyed in the wilderness. Joshua
records the first spring Israel could keep it:

> ✝️ Joshua 5:10-12 (ESV)
>
> 10 While the people of Israel were encamped at Gilgal, they kept the Passover on the fourteenth
> day of the month in the evening on the plains of Jericho. 11 And the day after the Passover, on
> that very day, they ate of the produce of the land, unleavened cakes and parched grain. 12 And the
> manna ceased the day after they ate of the produce of the land. And there was no longer manna for
> the people of Israel, but they ate of the fruit of the land of Canaan that year.

Joshua does not mention the sheaf. What it records is the first eating of "parched grain," the very
food Leviticus 23:14 had forbidden until the sheaf was waved, so the offering is implied by the
statute and left unnarrated. The *NKJV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible* reads it the same way: the
barley sheaf offered at Passover, and the produce eaten the following day. The sequence in Joshua is
exact: Passover, then the produce of the land, then the manna stops.

**What ends here is the food that came down from heaven; what begins is the food that comes up out
of the ground.** The wilderness provision was daily, external and temporary. The land's harvest is
seasonal, cultivated, and permanent — and it opens with a sheaf lifted before God. Whatever else
Firstfruits means, its debut in the story is the day Israel stopped being fed and started reaping.

## "On the day after the Sabbath" — the disputed date

This phrase decides whether Firstfruits is always a Sunday or always Nisan 16, and it has been argued
over for more than two thousand years. The text does not settle it.

**There is no textual variant.** The Hebrew at Leviticus 23:11 is
<span dir="rtl">מִמָּחֳרַת הַשַּׁבָּת</span> (*mimmochorath hashabbat*), "from the morrow of the
sabbath," and the identical phrase recurs at 23:15. The Samaritan Pentateuch reads with the Masoretic
Text at both verses, and the Qumran Leviticus scroll **4Q24** attests verse 11 including this phrase.
The dispute is over interpretation, and both readings are ancient.

| Reading | Takes *hashabbat* as | Firstfruits falls on | Held by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Festival** | Nisan 15, the rest day that opens Unleavened Bread (Leviticus 23:7) | Nisan 16, any weekday | Pharisees, and later rabbinic Judaism |
| **Weekly** | the ordinary seventh-day Sabbath | always a Sunday | The Boethusians, a party close to the Sadducees |

The Mishnah records the dispute. The sheaf was reaped with public ceremony, even when the evening
fell on a Sabbath, to refute the Boethusians, who held that the omer was not reaped at the close of
the festival day (Mishnah Menahot 10:3, paraphrased from the Hebrew).

**The Septuagint renders the phrase two ways.** At Leviticus 23:11 the Greek reads
*τῇ ἐπαύριον τῆς πρώτης* — "on the morrow of **the first**," which is the festival reading, written
into the translation. Four verses later, rendering the *same Hebrew phrase*, it reads
*ἀπὸ τῆς ἐπαύριον τῶν σαββάτων* — "from the morrow of **the sabbaths**."

One phrase, two renderings, in one chapter, by translators working centuries before the Christian
era. The ambiguity is older than both the church and the rabbis. The *NIV Biblical Theology Study
Bible* finds the phrase open even among those who take it as a weekly Sabbath — the Sabbath inside the
week of Unleavened Bread, or the one at its end — and adds that "the ambiguity may be intentional to
allow for variations in the ripening of the crops from year to year."

**Joshua 5:11 sharpens the question without settling it.** Israel "kept the Passover on the
fourteenth day of the month", and "the day after the Passover, on that very day, they ate of the
produce of the land, unleavened cakes and parched grain" (Joshua 5:10-11, ESV). Numbers 33:3 calls
the fifteenth "the day after the Passover", and Leviticus 23:14 forbids parched grain until the sheaf
is waved. On the weekly reading Joshua fits if the fourteenth fell on a Sabbath that year, so that the
fifteenth was its morrow. On the festival reading it fits only if "the Passover" in Joshua means the
festival day of the fifteenth, so that its morrow was the sixteenth. Joshua does not say which.

This study does not adjudicate it. The Sunday reading is the one a Christian typology would prefer,
which makes it exactly the reading to be careful about accepting on convenience. The next section
shows the typology does not need it.

## Why the dispute did not matter in AD 33

On the reconstruction [Chronology Anchors](../last-things/chronology-anchors.md#settling-the-crucifixion-year)
reaches, Nisan 14 in the year of the crucifixion was **Friday 3 April AD 33**. Lay the two readings
side by side on that week:

| Hebrew date | Day | What it was |
|---|---|---|
| Nisan 14 | **Friday** | Passover / the Preparation — the crucifixion |
| Nisan 15 | **Saturday** | the first day of Unleavened Bread, a rest day — *and* the weekly Sabbath |
| Nisan 16 | **Sunday** | the empty tomb, "toward the dawn of the first day of the week" (Matthew 28:1, ESV) |

Nisan 15 fell on the weekly Sabbath that year. So the two readings collapse into one:

- On the **festival** reading, the sheaf is waved the day after Nisan 15 — Sunday.
- On the **weekly** reading, the sheaf is waved the day after the seventh-day Sabbath — and that
  Sabbath *is* Nisan 15, so again Sunday.

**Both reckonings put the wave offering on the day the tomb was found empty.** In most years the two
part company and the calendar has to choose. In that year it did not have to, because the festival
rest day and the weekly Sabbath were the same day. That also fits John's note that "that Sabbath was
a high day" (John 19:31, ESV): a feast day fell on the weekly Sabbath. That is the usual reading of
John's phrase; [Three Days and Three Nights](../jesus/three-days-and-three-nights.md#the-counter-case-at-its-strongest)
weighs the case for a separate festival Sabbath.

This is the study's central observation, and it has limits. It does not prove the crucifixion was in
AD 33; that argument runs on Luke 3:1 and the Passovers John records, and is made elsewhere. What it
shows is that *given* that reconstruction, the resurrection lands on Firstfruits without the study
needing to settle a dispute the sources never settled. It covers the two readings in the table. On
the reading that waits for the Sabbath at the end of the festival week, the sheaf falls a week later.

(A caution on the other side of the ledger: the *ESV Study Bible*'s festal chart dates Firstfruits to
"16th of first month" without flagging the dispute at all — it simply adopts the festival reading. It
is a reminder that a chart is a set of decisions with the reasoning removed.)

## Christ the firstfruits

> ✝️ 1 Corinthians 15:20-23 (ESV)
>
> 20 But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen
> asleep. 21 For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead. 22 For
> as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive. 23 But each in his own order: Christ
> the firstfruits, then at his coming those who belong to Christ.

**The link is a shared word.** The Septuagint's Leviticus 23:10 offers
*δράγμα ἀπαρχὴν τοῦ θερισμοῦ ὑμῶν* — "a sheaf, the firstfruits of your harvest." Paul's word at
1 Corinthians 15:20 and 23 is that same **ἀπαρχή**. He uses the technical noun of the offering, to
readers whose Greek Bible used it of this feast.

**The commentaries give it as a foretaste; the statute gives it as a release.** The *ESV Study Bible*
glosses ἀπαρχή as "a first sample of an agricultural crop that indicates the nature and quality of the
rest," and the Cultural Backgrounds volumes as the guarantee that "once the harvest had begun, the
rest of the harvest was certain." Both are true and both are less than Leviticus 23:14 says. The
sheaf does not merely *indicate* the harvest — until it is waved, the harvest cannot be eaten. Read
back into Paul, "Christ the firstfruits" says more than that His resurrection body previews ours. It
says the rest of the harvest was legally in the field until He was lifted up, and is now available.

On that Sunday in Jerusalem, on the reconstruction above, a priest lifted a handful of barley in the
temple and waved it before the LORD. Outside the city wall, a man named Jesus walked out of a grave.
The offering that made a nation's harvest lawful to eat and the event that makes your resurrection
certain happened on the same day. God kept His own calendar: the sheaf He commanded at Sinai and the
Son He raised met on one day. **The sheaf was a promise about Him, and He is the promise about you.**

**"Each in his own order" is the sequence the feast already taught.** ἀπαρχή makes no sense as a
solitary category — a first requires a second. Paul argues through the chapter that denying the
general resurrection while affirming Christ's is incoherent, and the feast runs on the same logic:
nobody waves a sheaf over a field they do not intend to reap. "In Christ shall all be made alive"
(1 Corinthians 15:22, ESV). Your union with Him is your share in His harvest.

## What the firstfruits guarantees

ἀπαρχή occurs nine times in the New Testament, seven of them in Paul. The other occurrences fill out
what the guarantee covers.

- **Our own bodies.** "We ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we
  wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23, ESV). The Holy
  Spirit is Himself a firstfruits — a first instalment of the same life as what is coming.
- **A people.** "Of his own will he brought us forth by the word of truth, that we should be a kind
  of firstfruits of his creatures" (James 1:18, ESV). James writes to "the twelve tribes in the
  Dispersion" (James 1:1, ESV), and the harvest picture turns: believers born again by the word stand
  in the sheaf's place.
- **The redeemed.** In Revelation the 144,000 "have been redeemed from mankind as firstfruits for God
  and the Lamb" (Revelation 14:4, ESV): a first company offered to God out of a larger harvest.
- **Israel.** "If the dough offered as firstfruits is holy, so is the whole lump, and if the root is
  holy, so are the branches" (Romans 11:16, ESV). Paul is citing a statute: Numbers 15:20-21 commands
  that "of the first of your dough you shall present a loaf as a contribution." Same logic as the
  sheaf — a consecrated first, and the whole lump holy because of it.

The pattern holds across all of them: a first that is part of what follows, offered to God, and
securing the rest. It is why the New Testament can also call Jesus "the firstborn from the dead"
(Colossians 1:18, ESV) and "the firstborn of the dead" (Revelation 1:5, ESV). Both titles say *first
of more*.

## Discussion questions

1. **The language.** The Septuagint calls the sheaf of Leviticus 23:10 an **ἀπαρχή**, and Paul uses
   that exact noun of Christ. What does reaching for the offering's technical term claim that a
   general harvest metaphor would not?
2. **The text in its context.** Leviticus 23:14 kept a ripe field untouched until one sheaf had been
   waved. What would that wait have asked of a farmer whose family needed the grain?
3. **Deeper in Scripture.** In Joshua 5:10-12 the manna stops the day after Israel eats the land's
   produce. What changes about the way God provides for His people?
4. **The hard part.** The Septuagint renders the same Hebrew phrase two different ways at Leviticus
   23:11 and 23:15, and the question was never settled afterward. How should a reader hold a text
   whose own earliest translators disagreed about what it meant?
5. **Prophecy, and Christ.** Paul says the resurrection of Christ and the resurrection of believers
   stand or fall together (1 Corinthians 15:12-23). Why might someone want to affirm the first while
   quietly doubting the second?
6. **Be transformed.** Firstfruits was offered before the harvest was counted. Where does that
   order — first to God, then the remainder — actually cost something in your week?
