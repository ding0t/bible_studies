# Heaven and Earth Will Pass Away

In the week He was crucified, sitting on the Mount of Olives with Peter, James, John and
Andrew (Mark 13:3), Jesus set His own words against the whole created order: "Heaven and earth will
pass away, but my words will not pass away" (Matthew 24:35, ESV). Two of the men who heard it wrote
letters that lean on that sentence. Peter took its verb. Paul took its images. Both took its command.

**In one sentence:** Jesus staked His words against heaven and earth, and Peter and Paul built their
teaching on the day of the Lord out of those words, so God's promise that His Son is coming is the
most certain thing in creation, and you are to meet it awake.

## Key Takeaways

*(This section follows the [Key Takeaways](../about/key-takeaways.md) format — see that page for what each part is for.)*

### Types & Prophecy

**Prophecy.** Matthew 24:35 predicts the end of the present heaven and earth. Isaiah had already
promised what follows it: "For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth" (Isaiah 65:17, ESV).
Peter names that promise as the thing believers wait for (2 Peter 3:13), and John sees it done: "the
first heaven and the first earth had passed away" (Revelation 21:1, ESV).

**The flood as pattern.** Jesus compared His coming to "the days of Noah" (Matthew 24:37), and Peter
uses the same flood as proof that the world has been judged once already by God's word and will be
again (2 Peter 3:5-7).

### Lessons about Jesus

- **His words carry God's own permanence.** Isaiah said "the word of our God will stand forever"
  (Isaiah 40:8, ESV). Jesus says it of His own words, and sets them above the creation that God made.
  Only God can say that and be telling the truth.
- **He is the thief who comes unannounced.** The image is His own (Matthew 24:43), and the risen
  Jesus uses it of Himself twice more in Revelation (3:3; 16:15).
- **He died so that you will live with Him.** Paul grounds the believer's safety on the day of the
  Lord in the cross: "our Lord Jesus Christ, who died for us so that whether we are awake or asleep
  we might live with him" (1 Thessalonians 5:9-10, ESV).

### Be Transformed

- **Think.** The most permanent thing you own is a promise Jesus made. The ground under you and the
  sky over you are both temporary; His word is the fixed point you can measure them against.
- **Attitude.** Wait with patience of your own, because God is patient toward you: "not wishing that
  any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Peter 3:9, ESV). The years of waiting
  are His mercy at work.
- **Do.** Live awake this week. Peter's question is "what sort of people ought you to be" (3:11), and
  his answer is holiness, godliness and peace (3:11, 14). Name one thing you would put right if you
  knew Jesus came tomorrow, and put it right today.

## One sentence on the Mount of Olives

### Where the verse sits

Matthew 24:35 closes the paragraph Jesus opens with the fig tree (24:32-35). The next verse turns to
the timing: "But concerning that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels of heaven, nor the
Son, but the Father only" (24:36, ESV). Verse 35 is the hinge. It guarantees everything Jesus has
just predicted (24:4-31) before He tells the disciples they will not be given its date. The saying
stands word for word in all three Synoptic Gospels (Mark 13:31; Luke 21:33).

The discourse reaches past the return and the kingdom, to the end of the physical creation. Verse 35
sits immediately after the one ["This Generation"](fig-tree-and-this-generation.md#this-generation-whose-and-of-what)
works through, and the two are a single sentence, so neither is finished alone.

### One verb, two senses

Jesus uses one verb in two senses in consecutive sentences:

> ✝️ Matthew 24:34-35 (ESV)
>
> 34 Truly, I say to you, this generation will not pass away until all these things take place.
> 35 Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away.

All three are παρέρχομαι (*parerchomai*, par-ER-kho-my, G3928). At verse 34, of the generation,
Louw-Nida reads it as time elapsing (67.85). At verse 35, of heaven and earth and of His own words,
the sense shifts to ceasing to exist (13.93). A generation that will not run out before these things
happen; a cosmos that will one day cease; words that will not. A reading of "this generation" that
stops at verse 34 has cut a sentence in half.

Both denials use οὐ μή (*ou mē*, oo MAY) with the aorist subjunctive, Greek's emphatic form of denial: the generation will *certainly not* pass away until all these things happen, and His words will
*certainly not* pass away at all.

## Words that outlast creation

The *ESV Study Bible* puts the claim in a sentence: Jesus here "attributes divine authority and
permanence to his own teaching" (note on 24:35). Two earlier texts show how large it is.

**Isaiah 51:6.** God tells Israel to look up and down: "the heavens vanish like smoke, the earth will
wear out like a garment ... but my salvation will be forever, and my righteousness will never be
dismayed" (ESV). The shape is Jesus' shape — heaven and earth set against something of God's that
lasts — and in Isaiah the thing that lasts is God's own salvation. Isaiah 40:8 says the same of "the
word of our God" (ESV); the sibling study on "this generation" works through that verse.

**Matthew 5:18.** Jesus had used the phrase once before, of the Law: "until heaven and earth pass
away, not an iota, not a dot, will pass from the Law until all is accomplished" (ESV). The Greek has
παρέρχομαι twice there as well. In the Sermon on the Mount, heaven and earth are the outer limit of
the Law's standing. On the Mount of Olives, Jesus names something that stands beyond that limit: His
own words.

**This shows that God has spoken in His Son with His own authority.** The deity of Jesus is written
into the grammar of verse 35. A prophet says "the word of the LORD"; Jesus says "my words," and
promises them the permanence Isaiah gave to God alone. So the return Jesus promised is as sure as
God Himself. When you read what He said about His coming, you are reading the one part of the
universe that will still be standing at the end of it.

## Peter's echo

### The letter and its readers

Peter was sitting there when Jesus said it (Mark 13:3). Decades later, writing "the second letter"
to the same readers (2 Peter 3:1), he answered teachers who mocked the return as a myth; his reply
is that the apostles "were eyewitnesses of his majesty" (1:16, ESV). The scoffers' taunt, the
flood, and God's patience in 3:3-9 are worked through in
[A Day Is a Thousand Years](day-is-a-thousand-years.md#the-verse-in-its-own-argument). What matters
here is where Peter's vocabulary came from. He tells his readers to "remember ... the commandment of
the Lord and Savior through your apostles" (3:2, ESV), and then reaches for the discourse:

> ✝️ 2 Peter 3:10, 13 (ESV)
>
> 10 But the day of the Lord will come like a thief, and then the heavens will pass away with a roar,
> and the heavenly bodies will be burned up and dissolved, and the earth and the works that are done
> on it will be exposed... 13 But according to his promise we are waiting for new heavens and a new
> earth in which righteousness dwells.

παρέρχομαι occurs exactly once in the whole of 2 Peter, at 3:10, of the heavens passing away — the
same verb and the same Louw-Nida sense (13.93) as Matthew 24:35.

### Point by point

The verb is one borrowing among several. 2 Peter 3 works through the discourse point by point:

| 2 Peter 3 | The Olivet Discourse |
|---|---|
| 3:4 the scoffers' taunt: "Where is the promise of his **coming**?" | *parousia* — Matthew 24:3, 27, 37, 39 |
| 3:5-6 the flood, the world that "perished" | the days of Noah — Matthew 24:37-39 |
| 3:10 "the day of the Lord will come like a **thief**" | "if the master of the house had known in what part of the night the **thief** was coming" — Matthew 24:43 |
| 3:10 "the heavens will **pass away**" (παρελεύσονται) | "heaven and earth will **pass away**" (παρελεύσεται) — Matthew 24:35 |
| 3:11 "what sort of people ought you to be" | "stay awake... you also must be ready" — Matthew 24:42, 44 |
| 3:7, 10, 12 fire; 3:13 new heavens and new earth | the step past the return that the discourse leaves untaken |

Peter also answers the objection the discourse leaves open. If nobody knows the day, and it has been
a long time, has the promise failed? "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count
slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach
repentance" (3:9, ESV). The delay the scoffers read as failure is mercy, deliberately extended.

### What Peter asks of you

Peter draws the same conclusion Jesus did, and draws it in the second person: "Since all these
things are thus to be dissolved, what sort of people ought you to be in lives of holiness and
godliness, waiting for and hastening the coming of the day of God" (3:11-12, ESV). "Coming" is
*parousia* again. Then: "since you are waiting for these, be diligent to be found by him without
spot or blemish, and at peace" (3:14, ESV). The certainty of the end is the ground of a holy life
now.

## What passes, and what comes

### "Will be found"

The last clause of 2 Peter 3:10 is one of the harder textual problems in the New Testament, and
English versions differ because the manuscripts do. The Greek of the SBL edition reads
εὑρεθήσεται (*heurethēsetai*, hyoo-reh-THAY-seh-tai), "will be found," which the ESV renders "will
be exposed." Other manuscripts read "will be burned up" (the LSB and CSB footnote it), and a few
Syriac and Coptic manuscripts read "will not be found" (CSB footnote), which is the reading the
NA28 prints. The *ESV Study Bible* takes "will be found" as a divine passive — the earth and every
work on it laid open before God's judgment (note on 3:10).

### Renewal or replacement

Interpreters divide on what the passing of heaven and earth means, and the question is open.

- **Renewal.** The *ESV Study Bible* argues from the "will be found" reading, from Romans 8:18-25,
  and from the continuity of Jesus' resurrection body with His earthly one that God will purify and
  transform the present creation (note on 3:10).
- **Replacement.** Others read "dissolved" (3:10-12) and Revelation's "earth and sky fled away, and
  no place was found for them" (20:11, ESV) as the end of this creation and a new act of creation in
  its place, which is how Isaiah 65:17 words it: "I create new heavens and a new earth."

Both readings agree on what the text puts in the foreground: the present heaven and earth end, God
judges every work done on the earth, and a new heaven and new earth follow in which righteousness is
at home.

### Where it falls

Revelation places the passing of heaven and earth after the thousand-year reign of Jesus and the
great white throne (Revelation 20:4-15; 21:1). A dispensational reading, which this site holds (see
the [statement of faith](../about/statement-of-faith.md#end-times)), therefore takes Peter's "day of
the Lord" as a period that opens with the sudden, thief-like coming of judgment and closes with the
dissolution of the heavens a thousand years later. Readers who expect no intervening millennium put
the return, the judgment and the new creation together in one event. Peter's own sentence makes
the point on which both agree: the day that comes like a thief is the same day in which the heavens
pass away.

**This shows that God finishes what He starts.** The creation He called good, He will judge and make
new, and His Son's words will stand through the whole of it. So you may hold everything you can see
loosely and hold His promise hard.

## Paul's thief

### The setting

Paul wrote 1 Thessalonians around AD 49-51 from Corinth, to a young church (*ESV Study Bible*,
introduction). He had just told them what would happen to believers who had died, and told
them "by a word from the Lord" (4:15, ESV): Jesus will come, the dead in Christ will rise, and the
living will be caught up with them. Then he turns to the timing:

> ✝️ 1 Thessalonians 5:1-6 (ESV)
>
> 1 Now concerning the times and the seasons, brothers, you have no need to have anything written to
> you. 2 For you yourselves are fully aware that the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the
> night. 3 While people are saying, "There is peace and security," then sudden destruction will come
> upon them as labor pains come upon a pregnant woman, and they will not escape. 4 But you are not in
> darkness, brothers, for that day to surprise you like a thief. 5 For you are all children of
> light, children of the day. We are not of the night or of the darkness. 6 So then let us not
> sleep, as others do, but let us keep awake and be sober.

"The times and the seasons" (χρόνων καὶ καιρῶν, *chronōn kai kairōn*) are the same pair of words
Jesus used at His ascension: "It is not for you to know times or seasons that the Father has fixed
by his own authority" (Acts 1:7, ESV). The Thessalonians already knew the answer Jesus had given.

### Two images from one discourse

Paul draws on the same discourse for the same purpose, and takes two images from it in two
consecutive verses: "the day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night" (5:2), and "sudden
destruction will come upon them as labor pains come upon a pregnant woman" (5:3) — κλέπτης
(*kleptēs*, "thief") and ὠδίν (*ōdin*, "labour pain"), Matthew 24:43 and 24:8. He then applies it
exactly as Jesus did: "let us keep awake and be sober" (5:6).

### Children of the day

Paul adds what the discourse leaves implicit: who the thief surprises. "Sudden destruction will come
upon *them*" (5:3); "*you* are not in darkness, brothers, for that day to surprise you like a thief"
(5:4). Believers belong to the day already (5:5, 8). And the reason is the cross: "God has not
destined us for wrath, but to obtain salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ, who died for us so
that whether we are awake or asleep we might live with him" (5:9-10, ESV).

A pretribulational reading, the one this site holds, takes 5:9 with 4:13-18: the church is caught up
to meet Jesus before the day of the Lord's wrath begins (see [The Restrainer](the-restrainer.md) and
[The Rapture of the Church](rapture.md)). Other readers take "wrath" as final judgment and the whole of 4:13-5:11
as one event at the end of the tribulation. The point of 5:9-10 stands on either reading: God's
purpose for you in Jesus is salvation, secured by His death.

**This shows that God has already decided where you stand on that day.** Paul's instruction is
therefore encouragement: "encourage one another and build one another up" (5:11, ESV). You watch as
a child of the day who is expected home.

## The thief image, traced

The image Jesus used of Himself travels further than any other in the discourse:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    J["Jesus, Mount of Olives<br/>'the thief' — Matt 24:43<br/>(kleptes, G2812)"]
    J --> P["Paul — 1 Thess 5:2, 4<br/>'the day of the Lord will come<br/>like a thief in the night'"]
    J --> PE["Peter — 2 Pet 3:10<br/>'the day of the Lord<br/>will come like a thief'"]
    J --> R1["Christ to Sardis — Rev 3:3<br/>'I will come like a thief'"]
    J --> R2["Rev 16:15, in the bowls<br/>'Behold, I am coming like a thief!'"]
```

Five occurrences across three writers and the risen Christ's own letters, all of the same event, all
traceable to a sentence spoken on a hillside to four men. And the last word of the whole chain is the
one Jesus attached to it in the first place: "Blessed is the one who stays awake" (Revelation 16:15,
ESV).

The wider discourse, and the parables of servants ready and unready that follow this saying, are in
[The Olivet Discourse](olivet-discourse.md) and
[The Parables of the Olivet Discourse](olivet-discourse-parables.md).

## Discussion Questions

1. Matthew 24:34-35 uses παρέρχομαι three times — of a generation, of heaven and earth, and of
   Jesus' words. What does reading the two verses as one sentence change about how you read "this
   generation"?
2. In Matthew 5:18 heaven and earth are the limit of the Law's standing; in Matthew 24:35 Jesus'
   words outlast them. What is Jesus claiming about Himself in the second saying?
3. Peter was one of the four on the Mount of Olives (Mark 13:3). Which of the borrowings in 2 Peter 3
   would his readers have recognised, and what would that recognition have done to the scoffers'
   taunt in 3:4?
4. 2 Peter 3:10 is read as renewal of this creation or as its replacement. Holding Isaiah 65:17
   beside Romans 8:18-25, which way do you lean, and what does the promise of 3:13 look like on that
   reading?
5. Paul says the day will not surprise believers "like a thief" (1 Thessalonians 5:4), yet Jesus
   tells Sardis He will come on them like one (Revelation 3:3). What does it look like in your own
   week to be awake and sober?
