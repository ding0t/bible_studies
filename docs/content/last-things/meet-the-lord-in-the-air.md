# Meet the Lord in the air

Paul wrote to a young church burying its dead. He told them the dead in Christ would rise, and the
living would be "caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air" (1
Thessalonians 4:17, ESV). One Greek word in that sentence, *apantēsis*, "meeting," is the most
common lexical argument against a rapture before the tribulation. The argument deserves a full
hearing, and so does the Bible Paul read.

**In one sentence:** *Apantēsis* means going out to meet someone arriving, and what happens after
the meeting is set by the occasion; Paul names the occasion as the Lord Jesus coming for His own,
promises that "we will always be with the Lord," and does not say which way the company travels
next, so the word leaves room for Jesus to take His Church to the Father's house first and bring her
back with Him at the end of the tribulation.

### Lessons about Jesus

- **Jesus comes Himself.** "The Lord himself will descend from heaven" (1 Thessalonians 4:16, ESV).
  He sends no delegate for His people; He comes for them in person.
- **Jesus keeps His promise to take you where He is.** "I will come again and will take you to

- **Jesus is the Bridegroom His people go out to meet.** "Here is the bridegroom! Come out to meet
  him" (Matthew 25:6, ESV), and those ready "went in with him to the marriage feast" (25:10, ESV).

## The passage

Paul, Silas and Timothy had planted the church in Thessalonica and then been driven out of the city
(Acts 17:5-10). Paul wrote back from Corinth, most likely in A.D. 49-51 (*ESV Study Bible*,
introduction to 1 Thessalonians). Since he left, believers had died. The church did not know what
became of them at the Lord's coming, and they were grieving.

> ✝️ 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18 (ESV)
>
> 13 But we do not want you to be uninformed, brothers, about those who are asleep, that you may not
> grieve as others do who have no hope. 14 For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even
> so, through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep. 15 For this we declare to
> you by a word from the Lord, that we who are alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord, will
> not precede those who have fallen asleep. 16 For the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a
> cry of command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the sound of the trumpet of God. And the
> dead in Christ will rise first. 17 Then we who are alive, who are left, will be caught up together
> with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air, and so we will always be with the Lord. 18
> Therefore encourage one another with these words.

The point of the paragraph is verse 18: comfort. Paul answers one question, whether the dead miss
out, and he answers it with an order. The Lord descends, the dead rise first, the living are caught
up with them, and all of them meet Him together. The paragraph opens a longer section on the Lord's
coming that runs to 5:11 and ends on the same note: "God has not destined us for wrath, but to obtain
salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ" (5:9, ESV).

Two words in the paragraph carry the objection. Verse 15 calls the event the Lord's **παρουσία**
(*parousia*, par-oo-SEE-ah, G3952), "coming, arrival." Verse 17 says the saints are caught up **εἰς
ἀπάντησιν τοῦ κυρίου**, "to meet the Lord." **ἀπάντησις** (*apantēsis*, ah-PAHN-tay-sis, G529) is a noun,
"a meeting," and Paul uses it only here.

## The objection at full strength

### The civic reception

In Greek cities a *parousia* could be the formal visit of a king or governor. The citizens went out
to meet him on the road and escorted him back into the city with honour. In 1930 Erik Peterson
gathered papyri, inscriptions and literary texts showing the pattern and argued that Paul's pairing
of *parousia* and *apantēsis* draws on it ("Die Einholung des Kyrios," *Zeitschrift für
systematische Theologie* 7). His reading became the majority view among commentators.

On that reading, the Church rises to meet Jesus in the air and turns straight round to escort Him
down to earth. The rapture and the visible second coming become one event, and there is no interval
for a tribulation between them.

### Its best advocates

Two of this site's own reference works state it. The *ESV Study Bible* says the word is often used
of a city's inhabitants who come out to welcome an honoured guest and "then accompany him into the
city," and that it "may indicate" the saints' subsequent movement is downward, toward the earth (note
on 4:16-17). The *NIV Biblical Theology Study Bible* is firmer: "Paul's word choice, therefore,
implies that the church, once it has been 'raptured' to Christ in the air, escorts him to earth"
(note on 4:17).

When Michael Cosby challenged Peterson in 1994, Robert Gundry answered that the parallels between 1
Thessalonians 4:15-17 and the Hellenistic receptions are closer than Cosby allowed, and that Paul's
*apantēsis* probably does reflect the custom (*Bulletin for Biblical Research* 6, 1996). Gundry's
own reading of the rapture is posttribulational (*The Church and the Tribulation*, 1973).

### The New Testament texts that fit it

The objection's strongest evidence is the New Testament itself. *Apantēsis* appears in the SBL Greek
New Testament three times, and both of the others fit the civic shape:

- **Acts 28:15-16.** The believers in Rome "came as far as the Forum of Appius and Three Taverns to
  meet us" (ESV), εἰς ἀπάντησιν. Then "we came into Rome" (28:16, ESV). They went out, met Paul, and
  came back with him.
- **Matthew 25:6.** "Here is the bridegroom! Come out to meet him" (ESV), εἰς ἀπάντησιν αὐτοῦ.

The related noun **ὑπάντησις** (*hypantēsis*, hoo-PAHN-tay-sis, G5222) gives the clearest picture of all.
When the crowd heard "that Jesus was coming to Jerusalem," they "went out to meet him" (John
12:12-13, ESV), εἰς ὑπάντησιν, and He rode into the city to their hosannas.

**This is a serious argument.** The custom was real, the vocabulary matches, and two of the three New
Testament uses show a company going out and coming back in.

## What the word carries

### The Septuagint's range of outcomes

The objection rests on what the word implies after the meeting. So the question is what *apantēsis*
does elsewhere in the Bible Paul read. In Brenton's edition of the Septuagint the noun occurs
twenty-four times across twenty-two verses of the Old Testament books, and every one is the phrase
εἰς (τὴν) ἀπάντησιν, "to meet." The Open Scriptures lemma data, which includes Judges' A-text and the
Apocrypha, lists forty-one verses.

The outcome of the meeting changes with the situation:

- **A greeting.** Saul "went out to meet him and greet him" when Samuel arrived (1 Samuel 13:10,
  ESV).
- **A battle.** Israel went out εἰς ἀπάντησιν αὐτοῖς εἰς πόλεμον, "to meet them for war" (1 Samuel
  4:1, Brenton's Greek), against the Philistines.
- **A rebuke.** Michal "came out to meet David" and mocked him for dancing before the ark (2 Samuel
  6:20, ESV).
- **A massacre.** Ishmael "came out from Mizpah to meet" eighty pilgrims, brought them into the city,
  and slaughtered them (Jeremiah 41:6-7, ESV; LXX 48:6-7).

In the four verses above the Greek phrase renders the Hebrew <span dir="rtl">לִקְרַאת</span>
(*liqrat*, lik-RAHT), "to meet, toward," a preposition that says where someone goes and nothing about
what follows.

### The New Testament's own range

The New Testament shows the same thing with *hypantēsis*. After Jesus freed the Gadarene demoniacs,
"all the city came out to meet Jesus, and when they saw him, they begged him to leave their region"
(Matthew 8:34, ESV). A whole city went out to meet Him, and the meeting ended with Him sent away.

The word means *to go out and meet*. Welcome, war, rebuke, ambush and escort are each supplied by the
occasion.

### Sinai, the other meeting

One more meeting stands behind the passage. At Sinai, amid "a very loud trumpet blast," "Moses
brought the people out of the camp to meet God" (Exodus 19:16-17, ESV). The Greek there is a
different noun, εἰς συνάντησιν τοῦ θεοῦ, so this is an allusion and a contested one. But Paul's
passage has the same three elements: the Lord comes down, a trumpet sounds, and His people go out to
meet Him. The *ESV Study Bible* lists Exodus 19:16 among the trumpets that "proclaimed the Lord's
presence" (note on 4:16-17). At Sinai nobody escorted God anywhere. The people met Him, and He spoke.

**This shows that God sets the terms of His own coming.** The meeting in 1 Thessalonians 4:17 takes
its shape from the Lord who comes and from what He has promised, and a civic custom cannot overrule
His word.

## What the civic-sense commentary actually says

**The commentary that presses the civic sense states the condition on it.** The *NIV Cultural
Backgrounds Study Bible* puts it this way at 1 Thessalonians 4:17: "**when used in a context of a
royal 'coming'**," those welcoming him "would go to form his escort **en route to his destination**."

Both halves matter. The sense depends on the context of a royal coming. And the
escort accompanies the king to *his* destination, which the note does not identify. If that
destination is the Father's house, the escort travels up. Jesus said, "In my Father's house are many
rooms ... I go to prepare a place for you," and promised, "I will come again and will take you to
myself, that where I am you may be also" (John 14:2-3, ESV).

Paul's own sentence ends on the same promise: "and so we will always be with the Lord" (1
Thessalonians 4:17, ESV). He says who we will be with. He does not say where the company goes next.

## The escort, completed later

### The return at the end

The objection assumes the pattern needs an *immediate* return. What it needs is a return. Christ is
met in the air (1 Thessalonians 4:17), and Christ comes back with His own at the end of the
tribulation, among the armies of heaven clothed in fine linen (Revelation 19:14). [Does the Church
ride out with him?](rapture.md#does-the-church-ride-out-with-him) in The Rapture of the Church sets
out that case. On that reading the civic pattern is completed, with an interval inside it.

Paul's own letter already has the Lord coming with His people. "God will bring with him those who
have fallen asleep" (4:14, ESV).

### The wedding that is also a meeting

Matthew 25 makes the interval concrete, because it is the one *apantēsis* text that is also a
wedding. The virgins "went to meet the bridegroom" (Matthew 25:1, ESV) and at midnight hear "Come out
to meet him" (25:6, ESV). Then "those who were ready went in with him to the marriage feast, and the
door was shut" (25:10, ESV). Meeting, then feast, then everything after.

That is the order [The Rapture of the Church](rapture.md) sets out: caught up, assessed at the
judgment seat of Christ, married, and returning. Matthew 25:6 is usually cited against the pretribulational reading. On that
sequence it fits it.

**This shows that God keeps His Son's bride with Him.** Jesus meets His Church, takes her in to the
marriage feast, and brings her back with Him when He comes to reign. The same Jesus "delivers us from
the wrath to come" (1 Thessalonians 1:10, ESV), so you can wait for Him with joy.

## Settled and contested

[![Meeting the Lord in the air: the word apantesis and its four Septuagint outcomes; the civic custom of Acts 28:15, going out from Rome and returning with Paul; and two readings of 1 Thessalonians 4:17, the posttribulational escort straight down, and the pretribulational journey to the Father's house with the return at the end of the week.](../assets/img/last-things/meeting-the-lord.svg)](../assets/img/last-things/meeting-the-lord.svg)

**Settled from the text:**

- *Apantēsis* occurs three times in the SBL Greek New Testament: 1 Thessalonians 4:17, Matthew 25:6
  and Acts 28:15.
- In the Septuagint the phrase εἰς ἀπάντησιν covers greeting, war, rebuke and murder. The noun names
  the going out; the occasion supplies the rest.
- Paul does not state the direction of travel after the meeting. He states the company: "always with
  the Lord."

**Contested:**

- **Whether Paul alludes to the civic reception at all.** Peterson and Gundry say yes; Cosby argues
  the word was no technical term and that the main elements of a formal reception are missing from 1
  Thessalonians 4:15-17 (*Bulletin for Biblical Research* 4, 1994). If he does allude to it, the
  question becomes whose destination the escort serves.
- **Which way the company goes next.** The posttribulational reading supplies downward from the
  custom. The pretribulational reading supplies upward from John 14:2-3, with the return following
  after the tribulation. Both are inferences; the verse itself is silent.

None of this makes *apantēsis* a plank in the pretribulational case. That case rests on
[the imminence of the rapture](rapture.md#the-imminence-of-the-rapture), the absent-signs contrast
with Matthew 24, and 1 Thessalonians 5:9. What the evidence shows is that the word is weaker as an
objection than it is usually made to carry, and the Septuagint is where that shows.

## Discussion Questions

1. In the Septuagint, people go out εἰς ἀπάντησιν to greet, to fight, to rebuke and to kill. What
   in 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18 tells you what kind of meeting this one is?
2. The Thessalonians were grieving believers who had died (4:13). How does the order in verses
   15-17 answer their fear?
3. John 12:13 has a crowd going out to meet Jesus and escorting Him into Jerusalem. John 14:3 has
   Jesus taking His own to where He is. Which of the two does 1 Thessalonians 4:17 sound more like to
   you?
4. Matthew 25:6-10 has the virgins meet the bridegroom and then go in to the marriage feast. What
   does the feast between the meeting and the return tell you about Jesus?
5. Paul ends, "Therefore encourage one another with these words" (4:18). Who do you know who needs
   these words this week?
