# The rapture in the early church

Paul had to leave Thessalonica in a hurry (Acts 17:5-10). Within months some of the believers he left
behind had died, and the living grieved as though the dead had lost their share in Christ's coming.
So Paul wrote them the passage the word "rapture" comes from, and told them to "encourage one another
with these words" (1 Thessalonians 4:18, ESV).

The church kept reading that passage. What did the generations after the apostles take it to mean?
The writings of the church fathers are the place to ask, and this study reads them from the texts on
disk: the Latin of Irenaeus and Victorinus, Caspari's 1890 edition of the sermon called
Pseudo-Ephraem, and the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* translations. It carries on from
[The Rapture of the Church](rapture.md), which makes the case from Scripture.

**In one sentence:** The early church waited for Jesus to come and reign on earth, and a few of its
writers spoke of the Church "caught up" or "gathered before the tribulation"; that witness is real,
it is contested at every point, and it stands under the word of God it was reading, which is where
your hope rests.

### Lessons about Jesus

- **Jesus is the one the whole church has waited for.** Justin, Irenaeus and Tertullian all expected
  the Lord Himself to come from heaven, end the reign of Antichrist and set up His kingdom on earth.
  Paul wrote it first: "the Lord himself will descend from heaven" (1 Thessalonians 4:16, ESV).
- **Jesus delivers His people.** Paul calls Him "Jesus who delivers us from the wrath to come"
  (1 Thessalonians 1:10, ESV). Pseudo-Ephraem's sermon urges its hearers to prepare "for the meeting
  of the Lord Christ" so that He may rescue them from the confusion coming on the world.

## What Paul told the Thessalonians

Paul, Silvanus and Timothy wrote 1 Thessalonians from Corinth, most likely in AD 49-51 (*ESV Study
Bible*, introduction). The church was weeks old when a riot drove Paul out of the city by night
(Acts 17:5-10). He had not finished teaching them, and when believers died the church did not know
what their death meant for the Lord's coming.

Paul answers that one question.

> ✝️ 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18 (ESV)
>
> 13 But we do not want you to be uninformed, brothers, about those who are asleep, that you may not
> grieve as others do who have no hope. 14 For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even
> so, through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep. 15 For this we declare to
> you by a word from the Lord, that we who are alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord, will
> not precede those who have fallen asleep. 16 For the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a
> cry of command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the sound of the trumpet of God. And the
> dead in Christ will rise first. 17 Then we who are alive, who are left, will be caught up together
> with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air, and so we will always be with the Lord.
> 18 Therefore encourage one another with these words.

"Will be caught up" is ἁρπαγησόμεθα (*harpagēsometha*, G726), future passive of ἁρπάζω (*harpazō*, G726), "to
seize, snatch, take away." God does the lifting. The living are caught up "together with" the risen
dead, "to meet" the Lord: εἰς ἀπάντησιν (*eis apantēsin*). The *ESV Study Bible* notes that
*apantēsis* often describes a city going out to welcome a dignitary and escort him in. Readers who
place the rapture at the end of the tribulation lean on that sense; [The Rapture of the
Church](rapture.md) weighs it.

The point of the paragraph is verse 18. Paul wrote this to comfort a grieving church. **This shows
that God binds the resurrection of believers to the resurrection of Jesus**: "since we believe that
Jesus died and rose again, even so … God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep" (4:14).
Your dead in Christ are His, and your meeting with them is fixed in His plan.

Paul does not date the event against the tribulation in this paragraph. The timing question is
settled, if it is settled, from 1 Thessalonians 5:9, Revelation 3:10 and 2 Thessalonians 2, which
[The Rapture of the Church](rapture.md) and [The Restrainer](the-restrainer.md) work through. The
fathers below are asked a narrower question: how did the church read these promises in the
centuries closest to Paul?

## A kingdom on earth

The post-apostolic church was broadly premillennial. It expected Christ to return and reign on earth
for a thousand years, the same basic framework [The Rapture of the Church](rapture.md) sets out.
Historians of the early church do not seriously dispute that.

- **Papias** (early 2nd century), as Eusebius reports him, taught "that there will be a period of
  some thousand years after the resurrection of the dead, and that the kingdom of Christ will be set
  up in material form on this very earth" (Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.12, NPNF).
- **Justin Martyr** (mid-2nd century) told Trypho: "I and others, who are right-minded Christians
  on all points, are assured that there will be a resurrection of the dead, and a thousand years in
  Jerusalem, which will then be built, adorned, and enlarged" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 80, ANF).
- **Irenaeus** (late 2nd century) wrote that Antichrist "will reign for three years and six months,
  and sit in the temple at Jerusalem; and then the Lord will come from heaven in the clouds",
  bringing in "the times of the kingdom" (*Against Heresies* 5.30.4, ANF).
- **Tertullian** (early 3rd century): "we do confess that a kingdom is promised to us upon the
  earth … after the resurrection for a thousand years in the divinely-built city of Jerusalem"
  (*Against Marcion* 3.24, ANF).

The same sources show the view was held alongside dissent. Justin admits in the same chapter that
"many who belong to the pure and pious faith, and are true Christians, think otherwise." By the 4th
century Eusebius calls Papias "of very limited understanding" and blames him for leading Irenaeus
and others into the view (*Church History* 3.39.13). Premillennialism was early and widespread, and
it was never universal.

These writers also expected the Church to meet Antichrist. Irenaeus has the ten kings "put the Church
to flight" before the Lord's coming destroys them (*Against Heresies* 5.26.1). Whether any of them
taught a *pretribulational* rapture — the Church removed before the tribulation starts, as an event
distinct from the second coming — is far more contested. Four texts carry most of that debate.

## Irenaeus: the Church "suddenly caught up"

Irenaeus of Lyons saw Polycarp in his youth, and Polycarp, he says, "was not only instructed by
apostles, and conversed with many who had seen Christ" (*Against Heresies* 3.3.4). In 5.29.1 he
writes:

> "And therefore, when in the end the Church shall be suddenly caught up from this, it is said,
> 'There shall be tribulation such as has not been since the beginning, neither shall be.' For this
> is the last contest of the righteous, in which, when they overcome they are crowned with
> incorruption." (ANF 1)

The Latin behind "shall be suddenly caught up" reads *cum in fine repente hinc Ecclesia assumetur*
(Harvey's edition): "when at the end the Church is suddenly taken up from here." Irenaeus then quotes
Jesus' words about the great tribulation (Matthew 24:21).

**The case for a pre-tribulation reading.** The Church's being taken up comes first in the sentence,
and the tribulation follows it. *Assumetur* is a verb of being taken up, and *repente*, "suddenly,"
fits an event that comes without warning.

**The case against.** The next sentence calls the tribulation "the last contest of the righteous,"
in which the overcomers are crowned. A paragraph earlier Irenaeus writes that "tribulation is
necessary for those who are saved," so that they may be "fitted for the royal banquet" (5.28.4), and
in 5.26.1 the Church is put to flight by Antichrist's kings. On that reading the Church is caught up
*at the end of* the trial, as its crown.

The sentence can be read both ways, and his wider scheme places the righteous in the conflict. What
it does show is that a late 2nd-century bishop, taught by a man the apostles taught, spoke of the
Church being "suddenly taken up" in connection with the great tribulation.

## The Shepherd of Hermas: escaping the great tribulation

The *Shepherd of Hermas* (2nd century, Rome) is a book of visions. In the fourth, Hermas meets a
beast "of such a size that it could destroy peoples" and passes it unharmed because he trusted the Lord. The Church,
appearing as a virgin, tells him:

> "You have escaped from great tribulation on account of your faith … this beast is a type of the
> great tribulation that is coming. If then ye prepare yourselves, and repent with all your heart,
> and turn to the Lord, it will be possible for you to escape it." (*Shepherd*, Vision 4.2, ANF 2)

**For:** the believer is promised escape from "the great tribulation that is coming," and the
condition is preparation and a pure heart.

**Against:** in the vision Hermas walks past the beast. He is kept safe in its presence, so the
picture is of preservation in the tribulation. The text says nothing about being taken up.

## Pseudo-Ephraem: gathered before the tribulation

The single most-cited text is a Latin sermon *On the Last Times, the Antichrist, and the End of the
World*, preserved in two 8th-century manuscripts and attributed in them to Ephraem the Syrian and to Isidore of Seville. The critical text is C. P. Caspari's (1890). Caspari concluded that
neither man wrote it: the author used Ephraem's sermons but differs too much from him in style and
thought, and probably wrote in Greek. Scholars therefore call the author Pseudo-Ephraem.

### The line

Caspari's §2 exhorts the hearers: "Why then do we not cast away every care for earthly things and
prepare ourselves for the meeting of the Lord Christ (*in occursum domini Christi*), that He may
rescue us from the confusion which will overwhelm the whole world?" A few lines later comes the
sentence:

> *Omnes enim sancti et electi Dei ante tribulationem, quae uentura est, colliguntur et ad Dominum
> adsumuntur, ne quando uideant confusionem, quae uniuersum propter peccata nostra obruet mundum.*
>
> "For all the saints and elect of God are gathered, prior to the tribulation that is to come, and
> are taken to the Lord, lest they see the confusion that is to overwhelm the world because of our
> sins." (Caspari, p. 211; the English is the rendering usually cited)

### When it was written

The date is contested. The sermon speaks of "two brothers" who come to rule the Roman empire.
Caspari first read them as Valentinian and Valens, which would put the sermon around 373. He then
judged that reading doubtful and preferred the 5th or 6th century, or the first decades of the 7th,
with 627-628 as the latest possible date. Advocates of the earlier date lean on the two brothers;
advocates of the later one follow Caspari's final judgment.

### What it means

**For:** the saints are "gathered" (*colliguntur*) and "taken to the Lord" (*ad Dominum
adsumuntur*) *ante tribulationem*, "before the tribulation," for the stated purpose that they not
see it. The surrounding exhortation is addressed to living hearers preparing for "the meeting of the
Lord Christ." On any date, this is a pre-medieval text placing a gathering of the saints to the
Lord before the tribulation.

**Against:** later in the same sermon (§9), when Antichrist's distress "has covered all, the
righteous and the ungodly," the righteous are there "that they may be proved to their Lord"; Enoch
and Elijah are sent to call the faithful back to God. Critics read the sermon as expecting
believers on earth under Antichrist. Pretribulational readers answer that these are people who turn
to God after the gathering, like the multitude of Revelation 7:14, "the ones coming out of the great
tribulation" (ESV). The sermon's last paragraph (§10) sharpens the question: when Christ appears
after the three and a half years, an angelic trumpet cries *Surgite, dormientes, surgite, occurrite
Christo*, "Arise, sleepers, arise, go to meet Christ." Critics hear 1 Thessalonians 4:16-17 placed at
the visible coming; pretribulational readers hear the raising of the saints who died in the
tribulation. The sermon does not say which.

## Victorinus and Jerome's revision

Victorinus of Pettau, who died around 304, wrote the earliest surviving Latin commentary on
Revelation. Jerome later revised it, and Haussleiter's critical edition (CSEL 49, 1916) prints both
versions side by side. Two lines in the revised version are cited for a rapture:

- On Revelation 6:14, "The sky vanished like a scroll that is being rolled up" (ESV): *caelum
  inuolui: ecclesia de medio fit*, "the heaven rolled up: the Church is taken out of the midst." ANF
  renders it "that the Church shall be taken away."
- On Revelation 15:1, the seven last plagues: they "shall be in the last time, when the Church shall
  have gone out of the midst" (*cum ecclesia de medio exierit*).

**For:** the plagues of wrath fall after the Church has left the scene, which is the
pretribulational order.

**Against:** neither line is in Victorinus's own edition. Haussleiter notes that the explanation of
chapter 15 is missing from Victorinus, and that the Revelation 6 section was added by Jerome's and
later recensions from the commentary of Tyconius. The fuller Tyconian passage, preserved by Beatus,
reads the heaven's departure as the Church moved "from its place" in Antichrist's last persecution,
the good part "fleeing." So the lines witness to readers after Victorinus, and in their Tyconian
context "out of the midst" describes flight.

Victorinus's own comment on 2 Thessalonians 2:7 identifies the restrainer with Roman imperial power;
[What Victorinus actually says](the-restrainer.md#what-victorinus-actually-says) covers it.

## What the evidence adds up to

An any-moment, escape-oriented hope has real roots earlier than critics of dispensationalism often
allow. Irenaeus spoke of the Church "suddenly taken up" in connection with the great tribulation.
Hermas promised escape from it. Pseudo-Ephraem placed a gathering of the saints to the Lord *ante
tribulationem* more than a thousand years before Darby.

A fully worked-out doctrine separating the rapture from the second coming, as two events years
apart, appears in developed form much later, with 19th-century teachers like John Nelson Darby and
the Brethren movement. Each text above can be read another way, and the same fathers place the
Church in conflict with Antichrist. A claim that "the early church all clearly believed this"
overclaims what the texts support.

That does not settle whether the doctrine is true. The Trinity did not receive its creedal
formulation until Nicaea and Constantinople, and the church had always read it from Scripture. The
fathers are readers of the word of God, however early, and they answer to it. The Bereans were
commended for "examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so" (Acts 17:11, ESV),
and that was the apostle Paul they were checking.

**This shows that God keeps His promise by His word, and has kept a people waiting on it in every
century.** Your hope rests on what Paul wrote: "God has not destined us for wrath, but to obtain
salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Thessalonians 5:9, ESV). You are waiting for "our blessed
hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (Titus 2:13, ESV), as
Justin and Irenaeus waited. Wait for Him with the same expectancy, so that you encourage one another
with these words.

## Discussion Questions

1. In 1 Thessalonians 4:17 the verb ἁρπάζω (*harpazō*) is passive: "we … will be caught up." Who is
   acting, and what does that tell a grieving believer about whose work the resurrection is?
2. Paul wrote 4:13-18 to a church he had been driven out of before he finished teaching it (Acts
   17:5-10). What were the Thessalonians afraid of, and how does verse 18 answer it?
3. Irenaeus writes that the Church "shall be suddenly caught up," then calls the tribulation "the
   last contest of the righteous." How would you read those two sentences together?
4. Pseudo-Ephraem gathers the saints "before the tribulation," then puts "the righteous" under
   Antichrist in §9. Who might those righteous be?
5. Justin says "many … true Christians, think otherwise" about the thousand years. How should that
   shape the way you speak with believers who disagree with you about the rapture?
6. Titus 2:13 calls Christ's appearing "our blessed hope." What would change in your week if you
   expected Him to come before it ends?
